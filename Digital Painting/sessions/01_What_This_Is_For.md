## Lecture beats

### Beat 1: The course is not about Photoshop

**Slide:** *Photoshop is the tool. Not the subject.*

Open with the thing they expect and take it away. Say it plainly: the catalog calls
this a Photoshop class, and Photoshop will be open every single day, but nobody is
getting hired for knowing where a menu is. Point out that the version number on the
lab machines will be wrong within two years and the panels will have moved.

What carries: knowing whether the image is working, and being able to say why. That
is the thing the course grades, and it is the thing that survives the software.

### Beat 2: What the jobs actually look like

**Slide:** *Extend a set. Build a surface. Hand off a file.*

Three jobs, three projects, and each one is a thing somebody pays for. Walk the room
through them at the level of "here is what lands in your inbox on a Tuesday."

Somebody shoots a plate and the real location stops short of what the story needs,
so a painter extends it. Somebody models a prop and it is gray, so a painter decides
what it is made of. Somebody needs the painting to be usable by the next person down
the line, so a painter builds a file rather than an image.

Do not oversell this. Some of these are jobs that are getting harder to find, and
the room will know that. The reason to teach them is that the judgment underneath
them is the part that transfers.

### Beat 3: The three tiers

**Slide:** *Baseline. Target. Extension.*

This is the beat that saves the term, so spend real time on it.

Explain that every project in this course carries three stated levels on one brief.
Baseline is the floor that passes, written precisely enough that missing it is a
choice. Target is the actual assignment. Extension is the harder problem for anyone
who wants it.

Say the part that students do not expect to hear out loud: reaching for the
Extension tier and failing costs nothing, because the Target underneath it is what
gets assessed. Reaching has to be free or nobody reaches.

Then say the other part: the floor is real, and it is reachable on a lab machine
with a mouse in the studio hours already on the schedule. Nobody in this room needs
to buy anything.

### Beat 4: Where the machine sits

**Slide:** *AI extends imaging. It does not replace judgment.*

State the position once, early, and flatly, so it is not a rumor for four weeks.

Generative tools will come up repeatedly in this course, roughly ten minutes at a
time, always attached to whatever technique is being taught that day. They are
treated as an extension of digital imaging rather than as a separate subject,
because that is what they are: a generated element is a source, it has to be
integrated, and it gets judged on whether it integrated.

Concede the counterargument before anyone raises it, because somebody in the room
holds it and holds it seriously. Some artists consider this cheating, and that
position is not stupid. Nothing in this course requires a student to generate
anything, no tier is closed to anyone who declines, and every one of those segments
names what to do instead.

What is not optional is being able to see the failures. A person who cannot tell
that a generated image has three inconsistent light sources is going to ship it, and
that is true whether or not they approve of the tool.

End the beat on one line: the tool is a means of expression and the person using it
is not an operator.

### Beat 5: The working file

**Slide:** *Layers. Masks. Groups. Names.*

Pivot to the hands-on thing. Four ideas, and the room will claim to know all four.

A working file is one that can be changed tomorrow by somebody who is not you. That
means adjustments live on adjustment layers rather than in the pixels, removal
happens on masks rather than with the eraser, related things live in named groups,
and the names say what the group is for rather than saying "Layer 47 copy 3."

The test to give them, and to repeat all term: **could somebody else open this and
change the sky?** If the answer is no, the file is not finished, however good the
image looks.

### Beat 6: Destructive and non-destructive, side by side

**Slide:** *Eraser deletes. Mask hides.*

One worked example, done live in the demo that follows. Set it up here: two files,
identical on screen, one built with the eraser and flattened adjustments, one built
with masks and adjustment layers. Ask the room which is better and let somebody say
"they look the same," because they do.

Then the reveal is the demo.

### Beat 7: The home equivalent

**Slide:** *Photopea. Browser. Free. Same file.*

Name the hardware and license situation before a student raises it, because one of
them will and it is better coming first.

Photopea opens a PSD in a browser, does masks, does blend modes, does adjustment
layers, and costs nothing. It is not as good and it does not need to be. Every
technique in this course has a path that works on a machine with a mouse and no
subscription, and those paths get named as they come up.

## The demo

Twenty minutes, live, screen up.

1. Open the two identical files from Beat 6. Show them side by side.
2. Ask for a change: make the sky more dramatic, move the horizon, whatever the room
   calls out.
3. Attempt the change on the destructive file first. Let it go badly on purpose.
   The eraser took the pixels, the levels are baked, the only route back is undo
   history that does not stretch far enough.
4. Make the same change on the non-destructive file in about forty seconds.
5. **The deliberate mistake:** somewhere in step 4, paint on the mask with white
   when black was needed, notice it out loud, and fix it by painting back. This is
   the single most useful thing they will see today, because the whole point of a
   mask is that a mistake on it is free.
6. Finish by saving both files with correct names into a correctly named folder, out
   loud, narrating the naming convention. Delivery discipline starts on day one and
   it starts by being watched.

## Studio

Seventy-five minutes.

**1:05 The diagnostic (25 minutes).** Hand out one image and five operations. Nobody
is graded on it and say so twice, because the room needs to be honest for it to be
worth running.

The five: make a selection of one object and refine the edge; hide part of a layer
without deleting it; change the brightness of one element only; set one layer to
Multiply and say what happened; save a flattened JPEG alongside the layered file.

Circulate and write down who can do what. This is instructor data rather than
student assessment, and it determines how much of class 2 is review.

**1:30 CHECKPOINT.** Everyone stops. Two or three screens go up on the projector,
volunteered or drafted gently. Talk about one thing that went right and one thing
that went wrong on each. This is a fixed moment on the clock rather than a
raised-hand moment, on purpose, so the students who will not ask get reached anyway.

**1:40 The rebuild (40 minutes).** Hand out the destructive file from the demo.
Rebuild it as a working file: adjustments on adjustment layers, removals on masks,
elements in named groups. Same image at the end, completely different file.

Circulate. The conversation at every desk is the same one: what happens when
somebody asks you to change this tomorrow.

## Tiers for today's work

| Tier | The rebuild |
|---|---|
| **Baseline** | Three named groups, one mask, one adjustment layer. Saved with the correct file name. |
| **Target** | Every destructive edit in the source file replaced with a non-destructive equivalent, and the file opens to something a stranger could navigate. |
| **Extension** | Add a change the original file could not survive: a different time of day, built entirely from adjustment layers and masks, toggled on and off with one group. |

## Exit ticket

Three files, correctly named, in a correctly named folder, posted before anyone
leaves: `Lastname_C1_rebuild.psd`, `Lastname_C1_rebuild.jpg`, and
`Lastname_C1_notes.txt` with one sentence saying what was hardest.

It looks trivial. It is the first delivery of the term and roughly a third of the
room will get the naming wrong, which is worth finding out now rather than at the
midterm.

## Assigned

**EX1 Diagnostic**, due at the start of class 2. The take-home version of the five
operations, on a different image, with a short written answer for each: what you
did, and what you would do differently with more time. Under an hour. Not graded on
quality.

## What breaks

**The room assumes it already knows this.** Several students have had a Photoshop
course and will treat session 1 as review, right up until the diagnostic. The
diagnostic exists to make that collision happen quietly and early rather than loudly
at the midterm. Do not announce that purpose.

**The AI beat gets away from you.** Beat 4 can eat twenty minutes if the room takes
the bait, and it will take the bait. Hold it to the stated position, promise the
argument gets a whole session later (class 17), and move on. Writing "class 17" on
the board is usually enough.

**Somebody asks about tablets in the first ten minutes.** Answer it directly: no
project in this course requires one at any tier, and matte painting in particular is
much less brush-dependent than concept art. Then move on rather than defending it.

**The demo goes too well.** If the destructive file gets rescued too easily, the
point is lost. Pick a change that genuinely cannot be undone, and rehearse it once
before class.
