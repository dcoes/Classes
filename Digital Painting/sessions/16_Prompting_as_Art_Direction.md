## Lecture beats

### Beat 1: What the last three weeks were actually for

**Slide:** *You already know how to do this.*

Open by collecting the debts rather than by introducing a tool.

Eight times this term, ten minutes at a time, generated material has come up attached
to a technique. Each time the failure was named in the vocabulary of that day. The
generative fill edge was a masking problem. The generated plate was a value and light
logic problem. The generated tile was a frequency problem. The generated albedo was a
baked lighting problem. The generated video was a consistency problem.

None of those were AI problems. They were imaging problems, and the tool happens to
produce a lot of them.

Which means the room already has the only thing that makes any of this useful, which
is the ability to look at an image and say precisely what is wrong with it. A person
who cannot do that gets whatever the machine hands them. A person who can do it is
running a review.

### Beat 2: A prompt is a brief

**Slide:** *Subject. Treatment. Constraint. Reference.*

Reframe the whole activity as something the industry has done for a century.

An art director briefing an illustrator does not say "make it good." They say what
the subject is, what the treatment is, what the constraints are, and they put a
reference on the table. A prompt is the same document written for a different reader.

**Subject** is what is in the frame. **Treatment** is how it is rendered and lit, and
it is where most of the useful specificity lives. **Constraint** is what must be true:
the ratio, the camera height, the time of day, the thing that has to stay consistent.
**Reference** is the image handed over, which in practice does more work than every
word combined.

Say the consequence plainly: the students who write good briefs will get good results
and it will have very little to do with knowing any particular tool.

### Beat 3: Specificity is the whole skill

**Slide:** *"Dramatic" is not a direction.*

The beat that does the most work, and it is worth being slow.

Vague words produce averages, because an average is the only honest answer to a vague
request. "Dramatic lighting" returns whatever dramatic lighting looks like across
everything the model has ever seen, which is a committee. "A single hard source from
low camera left, the subject's face half in shadow, the background falling to near
black" returns a decision.

The vocabulary for that specificity is the vocabulary from class 3, which is the four
light questions, and from class 9, which is the four material behaviors. A student
who writes "rough unfinished oak, scattering, low specular, worn at the leading edge
where hands would grip" is not doing prompt engineering. They are doing art direction
and typing it.

### Beat 4: Control beats vocabulary

**Slide:** *An image in beats a hundred words.*

The practical hierarchy, ranked honestly by what actually changes an output.

A reference image beats a description. A rough painted blockout fed back in beats a
reference image. A mask limiting where the change happens beats all of it, which is
why generative fill inside a careful selection is more controllable than a text box
generating a whole frame.

The general shape: **the more of the decision you supply, the less of it gets made
for you.** Everything above is a way of supplying more of the decision.

Worth saying that this is why the useful workflow for somebody in this room is almost
never "type a prompt, receive an image." It is paint a blockout, generate into it,
paint over the result, generate into a selection, paint again. The generation sits
inside the painting rather than replacing it.

### Beat 5: Iteration, and the stopping problem again

**Slide:** *Cheap generation. Expensive attention.*

Class 7's stopping problem, arriving in a much worse form.

Generation makes it possible to produce fifty variants in ten minutes, and fifty
variants is not progress, it is fifty. The danger is real and it is not hypothetical:
a student can spend an entire studio block rerolling and end with nothing they chose,
only something they settled for when the clock ran out.

The questions that fix it are the ones from the brief. What problem does this image
solve? What does success look like? Is this iteration improving it or just changing
it? Can I say what is different about this version?

A useful discipline, and it is the one professionals arrive at on their own: decide
the target before generating anything, then stop when it is hit, even if the next one
might be better. The cheaper creation gets, the more expensive attention gets, and
attention is what is actually in short supply.

### Beat 6: Provenance, properly this time

**Slide:** *Tool. Prompt. License. Date.*

Ten minutes on the boring thing, because it is the part that has consequences.

Four things get recorded for every generated asset: which tool, what prompt, what the
license on the output is under that tool and that plan, and when. That is the whole
record and it takes fifteen seconds.

Why it matters, in working terms rather than legal ones. A studio cannot deliver a
shot it cannot account for. Clients increasingly ask, and some contracts now prohibit
generated content outright, which means a painter who cannot say what is in their
file cannot sign the delivery. In addition, the rights attaching to a generated
output are not settled in the same way rights in a photograph are, they differ by
jurisdiction, and they change. Nobody in this room needs to be a lawyer about it.
Everybody in this room needs to be able to answer the question.

Concede the reasonable objection here, since this is the session where it is most
likely to surface. It is possible to argue that none of this should be used at all,
on grounds that have nothing to do with craft. That position is coherent, it is held
by working professionals, and nothing in this course requires anyone to abandon it.
The record-keeping is the same discipline either way, because a sourced photograph
needs it just as much.

### Beat 7: What this does not replace

**Slide:** *Somebody still has to decide.*

Close the lecture on one line and do not extend it into a speech.

The tool produces candidates. It does not know what the image is for, who it is for,
what the client actually meant, or whether the result is any good. Somebody has to
decide, and the deciding is the job that was always the job.

The tool is a means of expression and the person using it is not an operator.

## The demo

Twenty-five minutes, live, one image directed to a stated target.

The point is the loop rather than the result, so narrate every decision.

1. **Write the target first, on screen, before anything is generated.** Four lines:
   subject, treatment, constraint, reference. Say out loud that this is the part
   nobody does.
2. Generate three candidates from the brief. Look at them with the room. Reject all
   three out loud and say precisely why, in the vocabulary of classes 2, 3 and 6.
3. Revise the brief based on what the rejections revealed, which is the actual
   mechanic being taught. The first brief is always wrong and the rejections tell you
   how.
4. Generate again. Take the best one. It is still not right.
5. **Paint into it.** Fix the value structure by hand. Fix the light direction on one
   element by hand. This is the beat that matters most, because it puts the
   generation back in its place as a source.
6. Generate into a selection to fix one specific area, showing that a mask is the
   strongest control available.
7. **The deliberate mistake:** at step 4, keep rerolling instead of painting. Do it
   five or six times, visibly, and let it get worse rather than better. Stop, say
   what happened, and go paint it in ninety seconds. Nobody forgets this one.
8. Fill in the provenance record live, all four fields, in fifteen seconds.

## Studio

Sixty minutes.

**1:15 The directed image (45 minutes).** One image, taken to a stated target that
was written before anything was generated. The deliverable is the image plus the log,
and the log is worth as much as the image.

The rules for the block, and they are the exercise rather than decoration:

- The target is written first and does not change. If it changes, write down why.
- Every generation gets logged: tool, prompt, what was wrong with the result.
- At least one substantial correction is painted by hand rather than regenerated.

**The non-generative route, and it is a full route rather than a consolation.**
Students who would rather not use these tools do the identical exercise with stock
and their own photography: write the target first, source three candidates, reject
them in writing with reasons, source again, composite, paint the correction. Same
log, same deliverable, same grade. The exercise is the direction and the record, and
those do not require a generator.

**2:00 CHECKPOINT.** Four or five logs on the projector rather than the images. Read
the rejection reasons aloud. The quality of those reasons is the entire measure of
this session, and a log that says "bad" three times is the finding.

**2:10 Finish the log (5 minutes).**

## What breaks

**The session becomes a tool tour.** It will try to. The material is the direction and
the record, not the interface, and every question about which tool is best gets
answered briefly and redirected to the brief.

**Rerolling eats the studio block.** The deliberate mistake in the demo exists
entirely to preload this and it will still happen. The log is the control: a student
who has to write down why each result was rejected starts rejecting for reasons, and
reasons lead to painting rather than to rerolling.

**The AI-averse students disengage.** This is the session where it is most likely and
the non-generative route has to be presented as a full route at the start rather than
offered as a concession when somebody objects. Read the block aloud. It is the same
exercise, it is graded identically, and the log is the deliverable either way.

**The room wants to argue about whether any of this is legitimate.** Good argument,
wrong day. It is the whole of next class and saying so is usually enough.

**Provenance gets skipped.** Fifteen seconds, four fields, and it is half the exit
ticket. Fill one in live during the demo so nobody can claim not to know the format.
