## Lecture beats

### Beat 1: A new problem, and why it is harder

**Slide:** *Last time you chose the light. Now you do not.*

Open by naming the shift, because it reframes everything the unit does.

In P1 the light was yours. You picked a direction, you held it, and every element
obeyed. In P2 somebody else lights the object, possibly from a direction nobody has
chosen yet, possibly six months from now, possibly in an engine you have never
opened.

So the painting has to describe what the surface **is** rather than what it looked
like on one particular afternoon. That is a genuinely different job and it is the
reason this unit exists.

### Beat 2: Four behaviors, not four appearances

**Slide:** *Absorb. Reflect. Scatter. Transmit.*

The whole unit reduces to these four, and each one gets a physical object.

**Absorb.** Charcoal, matte black paint, soot. Light goes in and does not come back.
These surfaces are dark everywhere and they barely change when the light moves, which
is why they read as flat and why students find them boring to paint.

**Reflect.** Chrome, still water, a polished floor. Light bounces off in an organized
way, so the surface shows you the room rather than itself. This is the one students
get wrong most often, because a chrome object has almost no color of its own and
painting it gray produces plastic.

**Scatter.** Unfinished wood, cloth, plaster, skin. Light goes in a little way,
bounces around, and comes back out softened and colored. Most things in the world do
this, and it is why most things have a soft falloff from lit to unlit rather than a
hard line.

**Transmit.** Glass, water, thin cloth, a leaf held up to the sun. Light passes
through and changes on the way, and what you see is mostly what is behind the object
rather than the object.

Say the working consequence once: **naming which of the four a surface does tells you
how to paint it, before any reference is opened.**

### Beat 3: The two questions that identify any material

**Slide:** *How sharp is the reflection. How much comes back.*

Reduce the four behaviors to two dials, because two dials is something a student can
actually hold in their head at a desk.

**How sharp is the reflection?** A mirror gives a perfect image. Brushed metal gives
a smeared one. Chalk gives none at all. That dial is roughness, and it is the single
most informative thing about a surface.

**How much light comes back at all?** A white wall returns most of it. Asphalt
returns very little. That dial is roughly value in the albedo, and students
over-think it constantly.

Almost every material in the world can be placed with those two questions plus a
color. That is not a simplification made for a classroom, it is more or less how
modern renderers are built.

### Beat 4: Wear is where the story is

**Slide:** *Damage happens where hands go.*

The beat that separates a surface that reads from one that is merely accurate.

Nothing in the world is uniformly worn. Edges wear because edges get knocked. The
middle of a step wears because that is where feet land. A handle is polished where a
hand grips it and dull two inches away. Dust collects in recesses and is wiped from
flats. Rust starts where water sits.

So wear is not noise, it is a record of use, and it is placed rather than scattered.
A student who covers a surface edge to edge in evenly distributed scratches has
painted static. A student who puts the polish exactly where a hand would go has
painted a history.

### Beat 5: Scale, and the mistake everyone makes

**Slide:** *How big is this, actually?*

A surface has a real-world size and students forget to decide it.

Wood grain on a table is a different size from wood grain on a matchstick, and a
texture painted without a stated scale will look correct in Photoshop and enormous on
the object. Decide, out loud, in the file: this texture covers a surface roughly two
feet across. Everything else follows.

### Beat 6: The cold reversal

**Slide:** *Name the material. Then name the behavior.*

Eight tightly cropped photographs, no context, no object visible, nothing but
surface. Painted metal, wet stone, old leather, brushed aluminum, unfinished pine,
glazed ceramic, cast concrete, worn rubber.

Two answers per image: what is it, and which of the four behaviors is it doing. Take
the answers, write the disagreements on the board, and do not settle them. Several of
these are genuinely ambiguous and the argument is the exercise.

## The demo

Twenty minutes, live. One shape, four materials.

Use a simple painted sphere or a flat rounded rectangle, and repaint the same shape
four times while narrating the two questions.

1. **Rough plaster.** Scatter. Soft falloff, almost no specular, small surface
   variation, low contrast.
2. **Brushed metal.** Reflect, smeared. Long directional highlight, almost no local
   color, dark where it is reflecting nothing.
3. **Varnished wood.** Scatter underneath with a reflect layer on top. Two things
   happening at once, which is why wood is harder than it looks and why students
   reach for it first.
4. **Wet stone.** The same stone as a dry version, plus water. Show that wet is not a
   new material, it is the same material with the roughness dropped and the dark
   values pushed darker.

5. **The deliberate mistake:** paint the metal with a mid-gray base and local color,
   the way instinct says. Let it read as plastic. Say so, strip the local color out,
   darken it, and let the room see it become metal. This is the most useful ninety
   seconds of the session.

## Studio

Fifty-five minutes.

**1:20 The swatch set (40 minutes).** Six squares, 1024 each, on one canvas. Six
different materials, chosen from a provided list so nobody spends twenty minutes
deciding. Reference is expected and encouraged, and copying a photograph directly is
not the exercise.

Each square gets a label layer with two things written on it: which behavior, and how
big the real surface is.

**1:55 CHECKPOINT.** Swatches on the projector with the labels hidden. The room names
the material. When they cannot, the swatch is not doing its job, and that is much
more useful information than any note about technique.

**2:05 Fix one (10 minutes).** Everybody fixes the one square the room could not
name.

## What breaks

**Everybody paints wood.** Wood, brick, rusted metal, three terms running. The
provided list exists to break that, and it should include at least two awkward
choices nobody would pick voluntarily: glazed ceramic, worn rubber, painted metal
with the paint failing.

**Metal gets painted gray.** Every term. The demo's deliberate mistake preloads it,
and it still happens, so use the same fix at the desk: what is it reflecting, and
what color is that?

**Wear gets scattered evenly.** A noise layer set to Overlay is not wear. The
question at the desk: where would a hand actually touch this?

**Scale never gets decided.** Ask one question at every desk today and make it this
one: how big is that, in inches? Half the room will not have an answer, which is the
finding.

**Somebody panics about Maya.** It is three classes away and the floor does not need
it. Answer once, plainly, and move on.
