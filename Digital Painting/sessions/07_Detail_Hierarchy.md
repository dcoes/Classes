## Lecture beats

### Beat 1: Detail is a currency and you have a fixed amount

**Slide:** *Spend it where the eye goes.*

Open with the economics rather than the technique.

Every image has a limited budget of attention. A viewer looks somewhere first, then
somewhere second, and then mostly stops. Detail placed where nobody looks is spent
money that bought nothing, and detail spread evenly across a frame is the same as no
hierarchy at all, which is why an image that is rendered everywhere reads as busy
rather than as rich.

The question to ask of any area of a painting: is this where I want them looking? If
not, why is it the most detailed thing in the frame?

### Beat 2: Three tiers of resolution

**Slide:** *Focal. Supporting. Suggested.*

A physical object for it: a shop window at night. The thing in the window is sharp,
the shelf behind it is legible, and the back of the shop is a set of shapes.

**Focal** areas get real detail, real edges, and real contrast. There are usually one
or two of them in an image and almost never four. **Supporting** areas get enough
information to be identified and no more. **Suggested** areas get value and shape
only, and the viewer fills the rest in more convincingly than any amount of painting
would have.

The last part is worth saying directly, because it runs against instinct: a suggested
area is frequently more convincing than a rendered one, because the viewer's own
guess is specific to them and a painted guess is specific to you.

### Beat 3: Edge hierarchy carries the depth

**Slide:** *Sharp forward. Soft back.*

Class 2's four edges, applied structurally rather than locally.

The sharpest edges in an image should be in the focal area and the softest should be
furthest from it, and that pattern alone creates depth even in a flat value range.
When everything has the same edge quality, the image goes flat regardless of how
correct the perspective is. When the edges are ranked, a viewer reads depth before
they read anything else.

Give the practical check: zoom out to fit on screen and ask which thing is crispest.
If the answer is a rock in the corner, the hierarchy is upside down.

### Beat 4: The three things that finish an image

**Slide:** *Grain. Grade. Atmosphere.*

The unify pass, from class 5's four stages, made concrete.

**Grain** over everything at once, one layer, at the top, which unifies sources with
different noise structures and does an unreasonable amount of work for how cheap it
is. **Grade** meaning one overall color and contrast move, usually a gentle one,
which gives the whole image a single lighting condition. **Atmosphere** meaning the
distance haze checked one final time across all planes at once, not element by
element.

Ten minutes, all three, at the very end. They are the difference between a composite
and a picture of separate things.

### Beat 5: When to stop

**Slide:** *Stop when the next hour makes it worse.*

The hardest thing in the session and the least teachable, so approach it through
tests rather than through advice.

Some of these include: **the squint test**, which is whether the image still reads
at a glance, because an image that reads worse squinted than it did an hour ago has
been over-rendered. **The thumbnail test**, which is whether it holds at two inches
wide, which is the size most people will ever see it. **The overnight test**, which
does not fit in a block schedule and is worth naming anyway, because nothing reveals
a problem like coming back cold. And **the note test**, which is whether you can say
in one sentence what the next hour would buy. If the answer is "more detail," that is
not an answer.

Concede the counterargument, because it is real. Stopping early can be an excuse, and
a student who stops at the blockout and calls it a stylistic choice is not making a
stylistic choice. The distinction is whether there is a stated reason, and the reason
has to be about the image rather than about the clock.

### Beat 6: The cold reversal

**Slide:** *Which of these is over-rendered?*

Three images: one under-resolved, one correctly hierarchied, one rendered evenly
across the whole frame at maximum detail. The last one is frequently the one students
pick as best, which is the useful part.

Ask, take the vote, and do not say who was right. It comes back in the critique next
class.

## Where the machine fits

Ten minutes, and this one is genuinely a diagnostic skill rather than a tool demo.

**The beat.** Generated images have a characteristic texture of error, and it is
learnable in about ten minutes once somebody has the vocabulary from classes 2, 3 and
6. Put four generated images on the projector and take the failures from the room.

What they will find, once prompted:

- **Perspective drift.** Lines that converge toward nothing in particular. Windows on
  the same wall on different vanishing points. Class 6 makes this visible.
- **Light logic failures.** Two or three light directions in one image, a shadow that
  falls the wrong way, an object with no contact shadow at all. Class 3 makes this
  visible.
- **Edge mush.** Everything at the same slightly soft, slightly overworked edge, with
  no hierarchy at all. Class 2 and today's Beat 3 make this visible.
- **Even detail.** Maximum rendering everywhere, which is exactly Beat 2's failure,
  and it is why so many generated images feel impressive at thumbnail size and
  hollow at full size.
- **Text, hands, repeated structure.** The famous ones. Worth mentioning and worth
  not dwelling on, because they get fixed by each new model and the four above do not.

Say the honest thing about this list. Most of it will be less true in two years. What
will still be true is that somebody has to look at the output and know whether it is
working, and the four items above are just the names for looking.

**The non-generative equivalent.** The same diagnostic run on a student composite
finds the same four problems, because they are not generative failures, they are
imaging failures that a generator happens to produce a lot of. Students who prefer
not to touch the tools run the exercise on last term's anonymized student work
instead and lose nothing.

## The broken file

Fifteen minutes, live, and then it becomes the studio exercise.

Open a deliberately mangled PSD on the projector. It should be genuinely unpleasant:
forty layers named "Layer 12 copy 3," several flattened groups, adjustments baked
into pixels, one element erased rather than masked, a smart object nobody can find
the source of, and two layers that do nothing at all.

Work it for fifteen minutes out loud. The order that works:

1. Turn everything off. Turn things back on one at a time and find out what each
   thing actually is.
2. Name as you go. Not later. Later never comes.
3. Group by role rather than by order of creation: sky, far, mid, near, effects.
4. Find the destructive edits and note which ones are recoverable and which are gone.
   Some of them are gone, and saying so out loud is the lesson.
5. **The deliberate mistake:** spend two minutes trying to rescue the erased element
   before admitting it is unrecoverable and repainting it instead. The cost of an
   eraser is a concrete number of minutes, and this is where it gets shown.

## Studio

Seventy minutes.

**1:05 The recovery (30 minutes).** Everyone gets the mangled file. Make it workable.
Named, grouped, non-destructive where it can be, with a short list of what was lost
for good.

This is the closest thing in the course to an actual first week on a job, and it is
worth saying so.

**1:35 CHECKPOINT.** Three recovered files on the projector, layer panel only, image
hidden. The room reads the layer stack and says what the image probably is. When they
can, the file is organized. When they cannot, it is not.

**1:45 P1 finishing (30 minutes).** Back to the project. Edge hierarchy, then grain,
grade and atmosphere. Nothing new gets added in this block, which is the rule for the
block: refine and unify only.

## Tiers for today's work

| Tier | The recovery |
|---|---|
| **Baseline** | Every layer named, everything in five groups, and a list of what was unrecoverable. |
| **Target** | The above, plus the destructive edits replaced with non-destructive equivalents wherever the pixels allow it. |
| **Extension** | Rebuild the file so it could survive a change nobody asked for yet: a different sky, a different time of day, a crop to a different ratio. Then say which of the three it still could not survive. |

## Exit ticket

The mangled PSD, recovered, posted as a screenshot of the layer panel. The panel is
the deliverable. The image is not.

## What breaks

**The recovery is treated as busywork.** It looks like filing. Sell it once, plainly:
this is what the first week of a job looks like, and the person who can open a
stranger's file and make it workable gets kept.

**P1 gets new elements added in the last block.** Somebody always decides at 1:50
that the composition needs a tower. The block is refine and unify only, and it needs
enforcing, because a new element at this hour will be the least integrated thing in
the frame tomorrow.

**Over-rendering accelerates instead of stopping.** The students who are enjoying
themselves will render for thirty minutes past the point of improvement. Use the
squint test at the desk rather than arguing: have them drop the black-and-white layer
and compare against a screenshot from an hour ago.

**The generative error beat turns into an argument about AI.** It will try to. Keep
it on the four failures, which are imaging failures, and let the argument itself wait
for class 17, which is where it has room.
