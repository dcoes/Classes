## Lecture beats

### Beat 1: Value is the load-bearing wall

**Slide:** *Value first. Color later. Always.*

Start with the demonstration rather than the claim. Put up a photograph, then put up
the same photograph with the color destroyed but the values intact, then the same
photograph with the values destroyed but the color intact.

The first two are readable. The third is mush. Let the room sit with that for a
moment before naming it.

This is the same standard the Storyboarding course teaches for the sequence, and the
language is deliberately identical so it compounds rather than fragmenting: value
and silhouette are what carry an image, and everything else is refinement on top of
them.

### Beat 2: The squint, and the three ways to do it

**Slide:** *Squint. Grayscale. Walk away.*

Three methods, one purpose: strip detail and see structure.

Squinting throws the image out of focus and leaves the large value masses. A
black-and-white adjustment layer at the top of the stack does the same thing more
honestly and can be toggled. Standing up and walking six feet back does it best of
all, and nobody does it, because it requires standing up.

Give them the instruction plainly: do one of the three every twenty minutes, all
term. It is the cheapest quality control available and it catches problems an hour
before they become expensive.

### Beat 3: Reduce to five

**Slide:** *Five values. No more.*

Any image that works can be reduced to about five values and still read. If it
cannot, the value structure was never there.

This is a diagnostic rather than a style. Posterize a photograph down to five levels
and the composition appears: where the light mass is, where the dark mass is, what
the eye goes to first. Do it to a strong painting and the same thing happens. Do it
to a muddy student composite and it goes to soup, which tells you exactly what is
wrong and where.

### Beat 4: Light logic, and the four questions

**Slide:** *Where from. How hard. What color. How far.*

Four questions, answered once per image, then held across every element in it.

**Where is the light coming from,** meaning direction, and it has to be one answer
rather than three. **How hard is it,** meaning the size of the source, because a
small source makes sharp-edged shadows and a large one makes soft ones, which is why
a sunny day and an overcast day look nothing alike. **What color is it,** because
light is almost never white and the shadow is almost never just a darker version of
the lit side. **How far does it reach,** meaning falloff, which is what makes a lamp
look like a lamp rather than like a sticker.

Write the four answers on the file. Genuinely, in a text layer, at the top of the
stack. Students who do this stop producing composites with contradictory lighting,
and it takes eleven seconds.

### Beat 5: Atmosphere is distance made visible

**Slide:** *Far things: lighter, flatter, cooler.*

A physical object for it: air is not nothing. It is a slightly milky substance, and
looking at a mountain twenty miles away means looking through twenty miles of it.

So distance does three things, reliably. Contrast drops, because the air is scattering
light into the shadows. Value rises toward the sky value, because that is what the
air is lit by. And the hue shifts toward the color of the air, which is usually
cooler in daylight and can be much warmer in dust, smoke, or late sun.

This single adjustment does more for believability in a composite than any amount of
detail, and it is one gradient on a masked layer.

### Beat 6: One worked example

**Slide:** *Why does this element look pasted on?*

One composite on screen, deliberately broken, with a figure that does not belong in
the plate. Work it in front of them, out loud, in order: check value against the
surroundings first, then light direction, then contrast for distance, then hue.

Fix it one step at a time and pause after each so the room can see what each step
bought. The value correction will do most of the work and the room should notice
that.

### Beat 7: The cold reversal

**Slide:** *Three composites. What is wrong with each?*

Three broken images, each broken in a different way: one with a value mismatch, one
with a contradictory light direction, one with no atmospheric falloff at all.

Ask. Do not answer. Let the room argue, let somebody be wrong out loud, and go to
the demo without resolving it. Whoever works it out during the studio hour owns it.

## The demo

Twenty minutes, live.

Take a cut-out person from a completely different photograph and put them into a
landscape plate where they obviously do not belong.

1. Drop them in. Let it look terrible. Ask what is wrong and take the first three
   answers.
2. **Value first.** A Curves layer clipped to the figure, pushed until the figure
   sits in the same value range as everything at that distance. Do this before
   anything else and stop to let them see how much it fixed.
3. **Light direction.** Find where the plate's light is coming from. If the figure
   disagrees, flip the figure horizontally, which is the unglamorous fix nobody
   teaches and everybody uses.
4. **Color of the light.** A warm pass on the lit side, a cool bounce in the shadow,
   both on masked layers at low opacity.
5. **Contact.** A Multiply shadow at the feet. Without it the figure floats, and the
   room will see the difference immediately because it is enormous.
6. **Grain, last.** One noise layer over everything, unifying the two different
   sources.
7. **The deliberate mistake:** at step 4, push the warm pass far too far so it goes
   orange and obvious. Say so, pull it back, and name the lesson: almost every one
   of these corrections is right at about a third of where it feels right.

## Studio

Seventy-five minutes.

**1:05 The five-value reduction (25 minutes).** Three photographs, provided. Reduce
each to five values, by hand or by posterize, then look at what appeared. Write one
sentence per image saying where the eye goes and why.

Short and fast, and it is the exercise that makes the rest of the term easier.

**1:30 CHECKPOINT.** Reductions on the projector, three or four of them. Ask the
room where the eye goes before the artist says. When the room agrees, the value
structure is doing its job.

**1:40 The match (40 minutes).** A provided plate and a provided figure from an
entirely different source. Integrate it, in the demo's order, with the four light
questions written in a text layer at the top of the file.

Circulate. The question at every desk is the same one: what does the black-and-white
layer say.

## What breaks

**Students correct color and call it done.** The hue slider is right there and it
feels like progress. Watch for it at the desks and redirect with one question:
what does the grayscale layer say. Most of them have not looked.

**The corrections get pushed too far.** Every adjustment in this session looks right
at roughly three times the correct amount, because the eye adapts while you work.
The fix is to make the adjustment, walk away for two minutes, and come back. Say
this at the checkpoint, not at the start, because it means more after they have done
it wrong once.

**The contact shadow gets forgotten.** It is the largest single improvement
available and it is one soft Multiply blob. If a student's figure still floats at
the checkpoint, this is why, nine times out of ten.

**The five-value reduction gets treated as busywork.** It is twenty-five minutes and
it looks like nothing. Sell it by doing one live in Beat 3 on an image the room
already likes, and letting the structure appear.
