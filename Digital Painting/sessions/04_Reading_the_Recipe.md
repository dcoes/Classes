reductions up while people are settling, without commentary. They work better as
something seen than as something discussed.

## Lecture beats

This is the most image-heavy session in the course and the one that needs the least
software. Almost all of it is looking at professional work on a projector and taking
it apart out loud. Nobody touches a mouse for the first hour and that is fine.

### Beat 1: The oldest trick still running

**Slide:** *Glass. Camera. Painter. 1907.*

Open with the mechanism, because the mechanism explains everything that follows.

A sheet of glass on a stand, some distance in front of the camera, with a painting on
part of it and clear glass on the rest. The camera photographs the painting and the
real set at the same time, in the same exposure, which means the painting and the
photograph are locked together by the same lens and the same film. That is a glass
shot, and versions of it were running by around 1907 or so.

Everything that has happened since is a variation on solving the same problem, which
is that the real place stops short of what the story needs. The glass became a
painted plate, the plate became a digital file, the file became a projected texture
on geometry. The problem has not changed once.

### Beat 2: What a matte painting is for

**Slide:** *The set that could not be built.*

Three reasons the job exists, and they are almost always economic rather than
artistic.

Building it would cost too much, meaning a city, a cathedral, a mountain range. It
does not exist, meaning a period, a future, a place that was destroyed. Or it exists
and is unusable, meaning the location was shot with a parking lot and three cranes
in frame.

Worth naming plainly: the third one is the most common by far, and it is most of
what the work actually is now. Removing a crane and extending a wall is not glamorous
and it pays.

### Beat 3: The four questions to ask any frame

**Slide:** *How many plates. What was painted. Where is the seam. What did the camera do.*

This is the tool of the day, and the room will use it for the rest of the session.

**How many separate pieces is this?** Count them. There are almost always more than
the first guess. **What part is painted and what part is photographed?** The answer
is usually that much less is painted than students assume. **Where is the seam
hiding?** There is always a seam, and it is nearly always hidden behind something:
an edge of architecture, a band of atmosphere, a foreground object, a shadow, a
change in focus. **What did the camera do?** A locked-off camera and a camera that
pushes in are completely different jobs, and the second one determines how the file
had to be built.

### Beat 4: Where seams hide

**Slide:** *Architecture. Atmosphere. Foreground. Focus.*

Each one gets a physical object and an example on screen.

Architecture hides a seam because a real vertical edge gives you somewhere to stop
painting. Atmosphere hides a seam because a band of haze across the middle distance
lowers contrast exactly where two sources would otherwise disagree. A foreground
object hides a seam by sitting in front of it, which is why so many of these shots
have a branch, a railing, or a doorway in the near field. And focus hides a seam
because anything soft is much harder to argue with.

Say the working rule once: **a good composite is designed around where the seams
will go, before any of it is painted.**

### Beat 5: The teardown, worked live

**Slide:** *One frame. Four answers.*

Take one professional still and run the four questions on it out loud, slowly, in
front of the room. Point at the screen. Count the pieces. Name the seam. Guess at
the camera and say that it is a guess.

Then play the actual breakdown, if the material has one, and let the room find out
how close the guess was. This is the single best thing in the session and it works
because the guess comes first.

### Beat 6: The cold reversal

**Slide:** *Three frames. No breakdowns.*

Three more stills, no breakdown material shown, no answers given today.

The room writes their four answers for one of them, which becomes the exit ticket.
Do not resolve these at the end of class. If somebody wants to know whether they
were right, the answer is to go find the breakdown themselves, which is a more useful
habit than being told.

## The P1 brief

Twenty minutes, out loud, with the brief on screen.

Walk the three tiers first and be specific about who each one is for. Then the
deliverables, then the milestones, then the provenance rule. Spend real time on the
sources file, because it sounds like paperwork and it is the thing a studio will
actually fire somebody over.

Two things to say plainly:

- The plates are provided. Four class days is not enough time to also go hunting for
  a photograph of a harbor, and the assignment is the integration rather than the
  sourcing.
- The Extension tier on this one, a second time of day sharing the same geometry, is
  the strongest single thing anybody in this room could put in a portfolio this term.
  Say why: it proves the scene was understood as a place with a light source rather
  than as an arrangement of photographs, and anybody hiring for this work knows the
  difference on sight.

## Studio

Sixty-five minutes.

**1:10 The teardown (30 minutes).** Four frames, provided as high-resolution stills.
In pairs, run the four questions on all four and write the answers down. Pairs
rather than individuals here, on purpose, because the argument between two people
about where the seam is does more than either of them working alone.

**1:40 CHECKPOINT.** Two pairs present one frame each, three minutes apiece, pointing
at the projector. The room disagrees. Let it.

**1:50 Plate selection (25 minutes).** Open the P1 source folder. Look at the base
plate, decide what the extension is going to be, and write three lines: what is being
added, where the light is coming from, and where the seam is going to hide.

That third line is the whole point of today and it is due before anybody paints
anything.

## Exit ticket

Three lines on one of the cold-reversal frames: how many plates, what was painted,
where the seam is. A guess is required. Being right is not.

## Assigned

**P1 The Set Extension.** Briefed today, blockout due class 6, rough due class 7,
final due class 8. See [`assignments/P1_Set_Extension.md`](../assignments/P1_Set_Extension.md).

**EX4 Shoot Your Own Plates**, due at the start of class 5. Ten photographs taken
with whatever camera you own, of surfaces and structures that could be used as source
material: a wall, a roofline, a texture, a sky, a window. Shot flat, shot in even
light, shot square to the surface where possible.

Phone cameras are fine and are what most of you will use. Three things to work
against: the lens is wide, so stand back rather than getting close; the automatic
exposure will fight you, so lock it if the phone allows; and shoot more than you
think you need, because roughly half of any batch is unusable and there is no way to
know which half until later.

EX4 is not required by any P1 tier. It exists so that whoever wants to build from
their own material has a path, and because a painter who can shoot their own source
is much harder to make redundant.

## What breaks

**The room guesses too low on the plate count.** First answers are usually "two,
maybe three." Push. Count out loud with them on the worked example until somebody
says eleven, because that is closer to the truth and it resets the scale for the
whole session.

**Students want to know whether they were right.** Especially on the cold reversal.
Holding the line here is uncomfortable and it is the point: the habit worth building
is going to find the breakdown, not waiting for an answer key.

**The brief gets skimmed.** Twenty minutes on a brief feels long and it is the
cheapest twenty minutes in the unit. Every question answered now is a question not
answered eleven times at the desks during class 5.

**Somebody decides their extension is a whole city.** It happens every term, usually
from one of the stronger students, and it eats the project. Redirect at the plate
selection block rather than at the class 7 rough, when it is too late: what is the
smallest addition that changes what this place is?
