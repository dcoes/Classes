# Class 8: Midterm Critique

**Digital Painting · session 8 of 19 · P1 final due · no lecture**

> **The argument of the day:** a note is data. It is not a verdict, and it is not
> about you.

## Before anyone speaks

Ten minutes on the frame, every critique, all term. It is repetition on purpose.

**The note is data.** Somebody telling you the building looks pasted on is reporting
what happened when they looked at it. That report is true regardless of whether the
building is pasted on, because it is a report about their eye rather than about your
file. Arguing with it is arguing with a thermometer.

**Do not defend.** The instinct is to explain the constraint: the source was bad, the
time was short, the plate was difficult. All of that may be accurate and none of it
is available to a viewer, and it will not be available to a client either. Write the
note down. Say thank you. Move on.

**Describe before you judge.** The first thing said about any image should be what is
actually in it and what it is doing, not whether it is good. This is the single
discipline that separates a useful critique from twenty minutes of people being
polite.

**Everybody speaks.** Every student gives at least two notes today and both go in
writing. That is the exit ticket and it is graded on having been given, not on being
right.

---

## Round one: the pass

Seventy-five minutes, four minutes each, timed.

The order is randomized and posted before class so nobody has to volunteer, which is
the accommodation that matters most on a day like this. Students who find presenting
genuinely difficult may have their file read by the room without speaking, and that
option is stated out loud at the start rather than arranged privately.

**Per file, in this order:**

| | Who | What |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | The artist | One sentence. What the extension is and where the light comes from. Nothing else. No apologies, no list of what they ran out of time for. |
| 0:30 | The room | Describe. What is in this image, what is photographed, what is painted. |
| 1:30 | The room | Two notes. One thing working, one thing not, both specific. |
| 3:00 | Instructor | The note behind the notes, and one thing to do next. |
| 3:45 | The artist | Reads back the note they are taking away. One sentence. |

That last thirty seconds is the part that makes the critique stick. A student who has
to say the note out loud in their own words has processed it. A student who nods has
not.

**Enforce the timer.** Four minutes times eighteen students is seventy-two minutes
and there is no slack. A file that generates a long argument gets held for round two.

---

## Round two: the four questions, turned around

Twenty-five minutes on three files, selected during round one.

Run the class 4 teardown questions on student work rather than on professional work:
how many plates, what was painted, where is the seam, what did the camera do.

The last question is the interesting one, because none of these were built for a
camera and the answers will be honest guesses. It is also the bridge into P3, which
gets briefed at class 14 and asks exactly that question of their own file.

Pick the three to hold back deliberately: one that is working and can be taken apart
usefully, one that has a single fixable problem the whole room can see, and one
ambitious attempt that did not land. That third one is the important slot, and it
should go to a student who reached for the Extension tier and missed, so the room
watches reaching get treated well.

---

## Grading, said out loud

Two minutes, near the start, because the room is thinking about it and will not
listen properly until it is answered.

The rubric is in the brief and it is five rows. Nothing on it is drawing ability.
Nothing on it requires a tablet. Fifteen percent of it is the sources file and the
contact sheet, which several people in the room will not have done, and that is worth
saying now rather than in the returned grade.

**Extension attempts are not graded against the Target underneath them.** Say it
again here, in front of everyone, on the day it becomes real. Somebody in this room
reached and missed, and the entire tier system depends on the room watching that cost
them nothing.

---

## Exit ticket

Two written notes to two different peers, submitted rather than spoken. Each note is
two sentences: one describing what the image is doing, one naming a specific change
and why.

Plus one line for yourself: the note you are taking away, in your own words.

---

## What breaks

**The room says "it looks good" eighteen times.** The default state of a critique
with mixed skill levels is mutual politeness, and it is worthless. The fix is the
describe-first rule, enforced hard for the first four files until it takes. Once
somebody describes well, the rest of the room copies it.

**Somebody defends their file for the full four minutes.** Interrupt kindly and
early, on the first instance rather than the third, and name the rule rather than the
person. It only has to be done once.

**The timer slips and the last four students get ninety seconds.** This is the most
common failure of a critique day and the one students remember. Put the clock on the
projector where everyone can see it, and take the hit on your own commentary rather
than on theirs.

**The strongest work gets nothing useful.** A good file generates "that's great" and
then silence, which teaches the best student in the room nothing. Have a harder
question ready for those: what is the weakest decision in this image, and what would
the next version be.

**Somebody did not finish.** Several somebodies, most terms. They present anyway, at
whatever state the file is in, and the critique is run on it normally. A student who
gets to skip the day learns that not finishing is free.

## Prep

- Randomized presentation order, posted before class.
- The projector queued from the submission folder, tested from that folder rather
  than from a copy on your machine.
- A visible countdown clock.
- The three round-two files chosen during round one, written down as you go.
- The critique frame on one slide or one printed sheet. One page, not a deck.
