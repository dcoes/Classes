## Lecture beats

### Beat 1: Everything so far ended at an image

**Slide:** *This one ends at a file.*

Open by naming the shift, since it is the whole reason the final is the final.

P1 was a composite. P2 was a map set. Both were finished when the picture was
finished. P3 is finished when somebody else can open it and work, which is a
different bar and a harder one.

Say why in concrete terms rather than abstract ones. A painting that gets used in
production is almost never used as a painting. It is opened by somebody else, pulled
apart, pushed through a camera move, relit, recolored, cropped to a ratio nobody
mentioned, or handed to a compositor at eleven at night. What determines whether that
goes well is not how good the painting is. It is whether the person who built it
thought about the person who would open it.

### Beat 2: Tidy is not the same as useful

**Slide:** *Named groups. Wrong cuts. Useless file.*

The distinction that carries the session.

A tidy file has everything named and grouped. A useful file is cut along the lines
the shot actually needs. Those are different, and a file can be immaculate and
worthless at the same time.

If the camera pushes forward, the file has to be cut into depth planes. If the light
is going to change, the file has to be cut by material and by surface instead, so a
warm pass can sit on the stone and not on the glass. Same painting, two completely
different files, and the difference is decided by an intent that has to exist before
the cutting starts.

### Beat 3: Write the intent first

**Slide:** *What does the camera do, and why?*

One paragraph, before anything is cut. Not paperwork, a tool: it is the thing that
tells you where the cuts go.

Two questions in it. What is the camera doing, physically, over how many seconds.
And what is the shot for, meaning what does an audience know at the end of it that
they did not know at the start.

The second question is the one students will write badly. "The camera slowly moves in
to create a sense of atmosphere" describes roughly every shot ever made and gives no
instruction about where to cut anything.

### Beat 4: A cut is a hole

**Slide:** *Separate the tree. Now paint behind it.*

The central mechanic, and it is worth being slow about.

Separating a foreground element onto its own layer does not move it out of the way.
It leaves a hole in everything behind it, shaped exactly like the element. The moment
the camera moves even slightly, that hole is exposed, and whatever is in it is what
the audience sees.

So every cut creates a painting job. A file with forty layers has forty holes and
almost nobody paints forty holes properly, which is why forty layers is usually worse
than six.

The working rule: **cut where the depth actually changes, not everywhere.**

### Beat 5: How many planes, really

**Slide:** *Sky. Far. Mid. Near. Foreground.*

Five is plenty and three is often enough.

The planes are decided by parallax rather than by objects. Two objects at roughly the
same distance move together when the camera moves, so they belong on the same layer
however different they look. A building and the tree next to it are one plane. The
same tree and a tree two hundred feet behind it are two.

Give the test: if the camera moved a foot, would these two things slide past each
other? If not, same layer.

### Beat 6: What to paint into the hole

**Slide:** *Enough. Not everything.*

Practical, and it lowers the workload considerably once students hear it.

A hole only has to contain what the camera will actually expose, which is usually a
band a few percent wide around the edge of the element that was removed. A six second
push does not reveal the whole back wall, it reveals a sliver. So the painting is
done at the edges of the hole and fades to approximate in the middle.

Worth naming the honest limit of this: a bigger move exposes more, and a move that
exposes something nobody painted is exactly how these files fall apart on somebody
else's machine at eleven at night.

### Beat 7: Name groups by role

**Slide:** *"PLANE_03_MID_buildings" not "Layer 47 copy."*

Ninety seconds, and it closes a loop that opened in class 1 and came back in class 7.

The name has to say what the group is for, and for a file cut for depth that means
saying where it sits in space. Somebody who opens this file and reads the layer panel
should be able to describe the shot before looking at the image, which is the same
test the class 7 checkpoint used.

### Beat 8: The cold reversal

**Slide:** *Where would you cut this one?*

Three finished paintings on screen, none of them layered, none of them yours.

For each, the room decides how many planes and where. The third one should be an
image where the correct answer is not depth at all, meaning something where the
obvious intent is a light change rather than a camera move, so somebody has to notice
that the question has a different answer.

Do not settle it. The studio block settles it.

## The P3 brief

Twenty minutes with the brief on screen.

Tiers, deliverables, milestones. Three points to spend real time on:

- **Baseline is Photoshop only and does not move.** A finished painting, delivered
  layered and organized. Say it clearly so the room hears that the final does not
  require After Effects.
- **The Extension tier moves, and it is five operations taught next class.** Nobody's
  pass depends on it and it is the strongest thing on offer.
- **Either a new painting or a substantial revision of P1.** Revising P1 is a
  legitimate choice and not a lesser one, and the critique notes from class 8 are the
  brief for it. Say that out loud, because several students will assume revision is
  the lazy option and it is frequently the better project.

## The demo

Twenty minutes, live, one painting cut into planes.

1. Read the intent paragraph out loud first. Six seconds, slow push forward, a reveal
   of what is behind the foreground.
2. Decide the planes by asking the parallax question about four pairs of objects out
   loud. Land on four planes.
3. Cut the nearest plane onto its own layer, on a mask.
4. Turn off that layer and reveal the hole. Let the room look at it. This is the beat
   of the session and it should get a few seconds of silence.
5. Paint into the hole. Show that only the edge band needs real attention.
6. Cut the second plane. Second hole. Paint it faster, since the principle has landed.
7. **The deliberate mistake:** cut a plane that did not need cutting, meaning two
   objects at the same depth split onto separate layers. Show the unnecessary hole
   that now has to be painted for no benefit. Merge them back. Narrate the cost as
   labor rather than as error.
8. Name every group properly, out loud, and then read the layer panel back as though
   describing the shot to somebody who has not seen the image.

## What breaks

**Everybody cuts too much.** Forty layers, forty holes, none of them painted. The
parallax test is the fix and it needs asking at every desk: if the camera moved a
foot, would those two things slide past each other?

**The holes never get painted.** Students separate everything and stop, because
separating feels like progress and painting a hole feels like doing work that will
not be seen. It will be seen. That is the only reason the cut exists.

**The intent paragraph says nothing.** Push once at the desk with the second question:
what does the audience know at the end of this that they did not know at the start?
If there is no answer, the file has no shape to be cut into.

**Revising P1 is treated as the lazy option.** Correct this early and by name. A
second version of a piece that has already been critiqued, built properly this time,
is frequently the strongest thing a student produces all term.

**Somebody is already opening After Effects.** Next class. The painting is the grade.
