## Lecture beats

### Beat 1: What photobashing actually is

**Slide:** *Photographs, taken apart, made to agree.*

Name the thing, and name what it is not.

Photobashing is building an image out of photographic pieces, then painting over,
between and through them until they read as one thing. It is not collage, because
collage wants the seams visible. It is not painting from scratch, and anybody who
tells a student it is cheating is describing a practice that has been standard in
film for about twenty-five years and in concept art for at least fifteen.

The scare quotes are worth putting on the word: "photobashing" is a slightly
embarrassed term for something professionals do constantly and do not always announce.

### Beat 2: The order of operations

**Slide:** *Block. Match. Integrate. Unify.*

Four stages, and doing them out of order is the single most common way this project
goes wrong.

**Block** means everything in place, badly, at the right size and the right
perspective, with no detail at all. **Match** means value and light, per class 3,
before anything is refined. **Integrate** means edges, contact, atmosphere, the
things that make two sources share a space. **Unify** means the final pass over
everything at once: grain, a color grade, a slight overall contrast move.

Say the rule that follows from it: **never refine anything until everything is
placed.** A beautifully rendered element at the wrong scale is a total loss, and it
is a loss that took four hours.

### Beat 3: Scale is decided by the horizon

**Slide:** *Eye level is one line. Everything obeys it.*

This is a preview of class 6 and it belongs here because students will need it in
twenty minutes.

Find the horizon in the plate, which is the camera's eye level. Anything standing on
the same ground at the same height crosses that line at the same point on itself. A
six-foot person twenty feet away and a six-foot person two hundred feet away both
have the horizon at the same place on their body. That single observation places
almost everything in a composite correctly and it costs one guide line.

### Beat 4: Perspective distortion, and why elements fight

**Slide:** *Wrong lens. Wrong height. Wrong day.*

Three ways a source refuses to sit in a plate.

The lens was different, so the building tapers at a rate the plate does not. The
camera was at a different height, so the element is being looked at from above while
everything around it is being looked at from below. Or it was simply a different day,
different weather, different hour.

The first two are fixable with warp and free transform, roughly. The third is fixable
with class 3. What is not fixable is all three at once in a source that was never
going to work, and recognizing that early and throwing the source away is a skill
rather than a failure.

### Beat 5: Paint where the photographs cannot go

**Slide:** *Photos for stuff. Paint for glue.*

The division of labor, said once so it lands.

Photographs give you material: brick, rust, foliage, glass, the enormous specificity
of real surfaces that nobody has time to paint. Paint gives you the things a
photograph cannot supply, which are almost always transitions. Atmosphere between
planes. A shadow falling from one source onto another. Light spilling across a seam.
Dirt in a joint. Contact.

Most of a good composite is photographic, and almost all of the work is in the small
percentage that is painted.

### Beat 6: One worked example, then the cold reversal

**Slide:** *This one works. These two do not. Why?*

Put up one integration that succeeds and walk the four stages on it out loud. Then
put up two that fail, one failing at match and one failing at integrate, and ask the
room which stage each one broke at.

Do not answer. Do not resolve it before the studio. Somebody will get it during the
blockout hour and that is worth more than being told now.

## The demo

Twenty-five minutes, live, one element from source to finished.

1. Find the horizon in the plate. Drop a guide on it. Say out loud that this is the
   first mark on every composite in this course.
2. Drop the raw element in. Scale it against the horizon rather than against how it
   looks.
3. Correct the perspective with a free transform and a warp. Show that the warp is
   doing a small job, not a heroic one.
4. Cut it in, on a mask, with the edge appropriate to its distance. Class 2 material,
   used in anger.
5. Value match with a clipped Curves layer, before touching color. Class 3 material.
6. Atmosphere: a masked gradient of the plate's haze color, opacity low, sitting over
   the element according to its distance.
7. Contact: a Multiply shadow where the element meets the ground or the wall.
8. **The deliberate mistake:** at step 2, scale it by eye instead of to the horizon,
   commit to it, then discover at step 7 that the contact shadow refuses to make
   sense. Go back and rescale. Narrate the cost, which is that everything after step
   2 had to be redone.
9. Grain over everything, last.

## Where the machine fits

Ten minutes.

**The beat.** A generated element is a plate. It enters the file at exactly the same
stage as a stock photograph, it goes through exactly the same four stages, and it is
judged on exactly the same thing: does it agree with everything around it.

Show it live. Generate one element, drop it into the demo plate raw, and let the room
say what is wrong with it. They will now have the vocabulary for it, which is the
point of running this beat after class 3 rather than before: the failures come out as
"the value is too light" and "the light is coming from the wrong side," which are
specific, fixable, and exactly what a client note sounds like.

**Provenance, and this part is not optional.** Whatever the source, it goes in the
sources file with the tool named and the prompt recorded. Say why in working terms
rather than in legal ones: a studio that cannot prove where an element came from
cannot deliver the shot, and "I do not remember" has ended contracts. In addition,
the license on the material matters and differs by tool and by plan, and a student
who has never once looked at those terms will eventually deliver something they did
not have the right to deliver.

**The non-generative equivalent.** Everything the beat demonstrates can be done with
stock, with a photograph from EX4, or with a piece of another plate. The four stages
do not change. Nobody is required to generate anything at any tier in this project.

## Studio

Sixty minutes.

**1:15 P1 blockout (45 minutes).** Everything in place, badly. Correct scale, correct
perspective, no detail, no refinement, no rendering. The horizon guide goes down
first and the three lines from class 4 go in a text layer at the top of the file.

Circulate constantly. The question at every desk is: where is your horizon, and does
this element cross it correctly.

**2:00 CHECKPOINT.** Everyone stops and drops a black-and-white adjustment layer on
top of their file. Walk the room and look at fifteen grayscale blockouts in fifteen
minutes. This is the fastest diagnostic in the course and it catches almost
everything worth catching at this stage.

| **Extension** | Block the alternate time of day at the same time, as a second group, so the two versions share geometry from the start rather than being retrofitted in class 7. |

## What breaks

**Somebody starts rendering in the first twenty minutes.** Usually one of the
stronger students, usually on the sky. This is the failure the four stages exist to
prevent and it needs catching at the desk in the first half hour, not at the
checkpoint.

**The horizon never gets found.** Students will place elements by eye because it
feels faster, and it is faster, right up until the contact shadows refuse to work.
The demo's deliberate mistake exists to preload this, so refer back to it by name
when it happens at a desk.

**Sources get used that were never going to work.** Three contradictions at once,
and the student keeps fighting it because they have already put an hour in. Give
them permission out loud: throwing a source away is a decision, not a defeat.

**The generative beat pulls focus from the blockout.** Same containment as class 2.
Projector only, ten minutes, and the studio block is scored on placement.
