## Lecture beats

This is the most technical session in the course and it is also the one where
students most reliably produce something that is correct and completely lifeless.
Beat 7 exists for that and should not be cut for time.

### Beat 1: Perspective is not a drawing subject here

**Slide:** *You are not inventing it. You are finding it.*

Open by narrowing the scope, because half the room has had a perspective class and
is bracing for two-point construction from a blank page.

That is not this. A photographic plate was made by a real lens in a real place, which
means it already contains a horizon, a set of vanishing points, and a scale. All of
that is recoverable from the image itself, with a straight edge and about four
minutes. The work is archaeology rather than construction.

### Beat 2: The horizon is the camera's eye level

**Slide:** *One line. It tells you where the camera stood.*

Say it once and demonstrate it twice, because this single idea does more work than
everything else in the session.

The horizon in an image is not where the ground meets the sky. It is the height the
lens was at. In a photograph taken standing up, it sits at about five and a half feet
off the ground, everywhere in the image, at every distance. In a photograph taken
from a third-floor window it is thirty feet up, and every object in the frame will
tell you so.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. Anything you are looking at the top of is
below the horizon. Anything you are looking at the bottom of is above it. Students
who hold those two sentences stop placing buildings that are simultaneously above and
below the camera.

### Beat 3: Finding the vanishing points

**Slide:** *Extend the parallels. Where they meet.*

The method, in three steps, done with the line tool and nothing else.

Find a set of lines in the photograph that are parallel in the real world: the top
and bottom of a window band, a roofline and a curb, the courses of brick in a wall.
Extend them. Where they converge is a vanishing point, and it sits on the horizon if
the lines were horizontal in the real world.

Do it twice on two different sets of parallels and the horizon draws itself as the
line between the two points. That is the whole technique. It works on almost any
photograph with a manufactured object in it and it takes less time than arguing about
it.

### Beat 4: Scale by the horizon

**Slide:** *Same height, same crossing point.*

Return to class 5's preview and make it a method.

Take one thing in the plate whose height you know, or can guess honestly: a door is
about seven feet, a storey is about ten, a car is about five to the roof. Mark where
the horizon crosses it. Anything else standing on that same ground at that same
height will have the horizon crossing it at the same proportion of its height,
however far away it is.

That gives a measuring stick. A new building placed in the plate is scaled by walking
that stick back along the ground plane, not by dragging the corner until it looks
about right.

### Beat 5: The ground plane, and what students forget

**Slide:** *Things stand on something.*

The most common perspective failure in a composite is not a wrong vanishing point. It
is an object that is not standing on anything.

An element pasted into a plate floats unless three things are true: it meets the
ground somewhere visible, it casts something where it meets it, and the place where
it meets is at the correct depth for its size. Miss any of the three and the eye
reads it as a sticker, even when every line in it is perfect.

### Beat 6: Lens, and when the grid stops helping

**Slide:** *Wide lenses bend. Long lenses flatten.*

A brief, honest limit on the method.

A wide lens spreads the frame and pushes vanishing points far outside the image,
which is why phone photographs taken close to a building look distorted at the edges.
A long lens compresses depth until the perspective barely converges at all, which is
why a telephoto shot of a street looks stacked.

So a source shot on a long lens will fight a plate shot on a wide one, and no amount
of grid work fixes that. It has to be warped, cropped, or thrown away. Knowing which
of the three is the actual skill.

### Beat 7: Correct is not the same as good

**Slide:** *A right answer can still be dead.*

The beat that matters most and the one that will get skipped if the demo runs long.

A composite can have a perfect grid, correct scale, accurate vanishing points, and
still be lifeless, because a grid answers where things go and answers nothing about
whether the image is worth looking at. Perspective is structure. Structure is
necessary and it is not sufficient.

The questions that make the difference are the ones from the other sessions. Where
does the eye go first? What is the value structure? What is the shot for? A student
who spends the whole studio hour on guide lines and never squints at the result has
built a technically correct picture of nothing.

### Beat 8: The cold reversal

**Slide:** *Find the horizon in these three.*

Three photographs, no guides drawn, no answers shown. The room finds the horizon in
each and writes down roughly where it is. Two of them are straightforward. One was
shot from a height and will split the room.

Do not resolve it. It comes back as the exit ticket on their own plate.

## The demo

Twenty-five minutes, live.

1. Open a photograph with obvious architecture. Nothing else on screen.
2. Line tool, on a new layer, in a color that screams. Extend one set of real-world
   parallels. Extend another set. Mark where each converges.
3. Draw the horizon between the two points. Step back from the screen and let the
   room see that it sits at about eye height on the people in the photograph, which
   is the moment the idea lands.
4. Pick a door. Mark the horizon crossing on it. Walk that measurement back into the
   depth of the image twice.
5. Place a new element using the measurement rather than by eye.
6. **The deliberate mistake:** place a second element by eye first, deliberately,
   then measure it and discover it is about forty percent too large. Fix it in front
   of them. Say the cost out loud, which is that everything painted on it would have
   been wasted.
7. Ground it: contact point, contact shadow, correct depth.
8. Turn off the guide layer. Look at the result. Ask whether it is any good, which is
   a different question from whether it is correct, and let the room sit in the gap.

## Studio

Sixty-five minutes.

**1:10 Grid your own plate (20 minutes).** On the P1 plate, on a new layer, in a
loud color: horizon, at least one vanishing point, and one measuring stick from a
known object. Twenty minutes, hard stop.

**1:30 CHECKPOINT.** Blockouts from the start of class go back out, now with the
grids on them. Walk the room. The question at every desk is whether the elements
placed in class 5 survive contact with the grid drawn today. For a good number of
them the answer is no, which is exactly why the grid is drawn at the blockout stage
rather than at the rough.

**1:45 Rebuild on the grid (30 minutes).** Fix the placements the grid just
invalidated. This is unglamorous and it is the difference between a composite that
holds and one that never quite does.

## Exit ticket

The P1 plate with the guide layer visible: horizon and one vanishing point, drawn.
One image. The guides are the deliverable, not the painting.

---

## What breaks

**The grid becomes the project.** Some students will spend seventy minutes drawing
beautiful guide lines and place nothing. Twenty minutes is a hard stop for a reason.
Beat 7 is the argument, and it needs repeating at the desks.

**Nobody believes the horizon is at eye level.** It sounds wrong until it is
demonstrated on a photograph with people in it. Have that image ready and use it the
moment somebody pushes back.

**Elements get placed above and below the camera at once.** The single most common
error. The fix is the two sentences from Beat 2, and asking one question at the desk:
are you looking at the top of that, or the bottom?

**The rebuild gets resisted.** Being told at the checkpoint that an hour of placement
was wrong is discouraging, and a few students will argue rather than move things.
Frame it the way production does: this is what a blockout is for, and finding it now
costs thirty minutes while finding it at the rough costs the project.
