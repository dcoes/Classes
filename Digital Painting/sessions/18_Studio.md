## Why this is scheduled rather than open

Two minutes, said once at the start.

An open work day produces a room where the students who need the most attention ask
for the least of it, which is the opposite of what is wanted. So everybody has a time
slot, posted before class, ten minutes each, and the slot happens whether or not the
student thinks they need it.

That is the same reasoning as the checkpoints in every studio block this term. Drift
gets caught on the clock rather than by raised hand.

---

## On the board before anyone arrives

Three things, and they stay up all session.

**The desk crit schedule.** Names and times, ten minutes apiece. Eighteen students in
125 minutes leaves reasonable slack for overruns.

**The finish checklist.** The things that are easy to lose in the last two days:

- Intent paragraph written, and the file actually cut for that intent
- Every hole painted at the edges
- Groups named by role
- Sources file complete, including anything generated
- Contact sheet of the build, three stages
- Flat JPEG and layered PSD, correct names, correct folder
- Extension only: the export, 1920 by 1080, h264, six to ten seconds

**The delivery spec**, copied verbatim from the brief, in the exact file naming the
submission expects.

## The desk crit

Ten minutes each, and it runs the same shape every time so it stays on schedule.

| | What |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | The student says the intent out loud. One sentence, not read off the screen. |
| 0:30 | Grayscale check. Black and white layer on, look at the value structure together. |
| 2:00 | Layer panel, image hidden. Read the stack. Can the shot be described from it? |
| 4:00 | One note, and only one, chosen for what it buys in the time remaining. |
| 7:00 | The student says what they will do about it, and roughly when. |
| 9:00 | Next. |

**One note per student.** Not five. With one class left, five notes produce paralysis
and one produces a finished project. Choose the note by asking what has the largest
effect for the smallest cost, which at this stage is almost never "add more detail"
and almost always one of: fix the value structure, paint the hole, name the groups,
or write the intent properly.

For students at the Extension tier, the note is frequently about the move rather than
the painting, and it is usually the same one: it is too fast.

## For students who are behind

Some will arrive with very little, and this is the day that is decided.

Do not spend the slot on encouragement. Spend it on triage, out loud, in the brief's
own language: here is the Baseline, here is what is missing from it, here is the
order to do it in, here is what to abandon. A student who leaves with a list of four
concrete things in order will finish. A student who leaves feeling better will not.

Naming the Baseline explicitly is the point of the tier system and today is when it
pays.

---

## For students who are ahead

The risk at the top of the room on the second-to-last day is over-rendering, which
class 7 named and which comes back now with more time to do damage.

The note for those students is almost always a stopping note, and the tests from class
7 are the way to deliver it without it sounding like a compliment withheld: does the
image still read squinted, does it hold at two inches, and what would the next hour
actually buy.

The alternative note, for anyone genuinely finished, is to put the time into the
contact sheet and the sources file, which are thirty-five percent of the grade
combined and which almost nobody treats as work.

## Delivery dry run

Fifteen minutes at the end, everybody, no exceptions.

Build the submission folder, with the real names, in the real place, and submit it.
Not the final work, whatever exists right now. Then open the submitted version from
the submission location on a different machine and confirm it is what was expected.

This catches, every term: files named wrong, a PSD saved as a flattened copy, a
layered file missing because it was too large to upload, an export that was never
rendered, a sources file that exists only as an untitled text window.

Fifteen minutes now saves the version of class 19 where a third of the room is
troubleshooting uploads instead of presenting.

## Exit ticket

The desk crit sheet, signed: the note received, in the student's own words, and what
they intend to do about it before class 19.

Signed rather than typed, because the point is that the conversation happened.

## What breaks

**The schedule slips and the last four slots vanish.** The most common failure of a
scheduled crit day and it lands on whoever is last, which is often whoever needed it
most. Keep a timer visible. Ten minutes is ten minutes, and a conversation that needs
longer gets a second slot at the end rather than an overrun.

**Somebody skips their slot.** Go to them. The slot happens whether or not they think
they need it, and the students who avoid it are the reason the day is scheduled.

**The room treats it as a free period.** Setting the tone in the first ten minutes
handles it: the board is up, the schedule is posted, the first crit starts at 0:10.

**Five notes get given instead of one.** The instructor failure mode, and it is well
intentioned. Write the other four down and hold them for the class 19 critique, where
they belong.

**The dry run gets skipped because people are working.** It is the single highest
value fifteen minutes of the day. Stop the room.
