# Writing Style Analysis — Professional & Academic Register

**Author:** Daniel R. Coes
**Corpus:** Undergraduate dissertation chapter (*Into the Virtual*), cover letter, promotion letter, letter of recommendation, teaching philosophy, industry talk outline, lecture decks, professional email.

---

## Scope

There is already a style analysis in this project built on casual conversation — the thinking-aloud voice, the Socratic pushback, the "I don't know" used to open a thought rather than close one. This document covers the other side of the corpus: writing done *for* someone. Applications, recommendations, lectures, an academic paper, correspondence with a stranger in the industry.

The useful finding is that the two are not different writers. The professional writing is the conversational voice wearing a collar. It hedges more, it thanks people, it organizes itself into bullets when it has to — but the sentences still accumulate rather than resolve, the abstractions still arrive attached to a physical object, and the author still concedes the opposing point before anyone asks him to.

---

## Tone

### 1. Warm, unpretentious, slightly deferential

The default posture toward a reader is generous and a little modest. Letters open by giving the other person room and close by handing the exchange back to them:

> "Thank you very much for your time, I look forward to hearing from you."

> "Again, nice to hear from you. I hope to keep in touch!"

Exclamation points are rare enough to mean something when they appear. An emoticon shows up once, in email, and reads as sincere rather than performative.

### 2. Systematically self-deprecating about his own competence

This is one of the most consistent tonal habits in the corpus, and it runs against the grain of the documents it appears in. He undersells himself *in job applications*:

> "Without much skill or patience as a programmer, I was not able to create a very functional code..."

> "...a **somewhat** broad view of various parts of the 3D pipeline"

> "becoming **quite** familiar with it (and fond of it)"

> "You can't do everything, but you want to understand **enough** about the technical part..."

Where another writer would write *deep* or *extensive*, he writes *somewhat*, *quite*, *enough*, *more or less*. The credential gets stated and then immediately trimmed. Any imitation that stacks superlatives will sound wrong instantly.

### 3. Enthusiasm stated plainly, without inflation

Excitement is reported as fact, not dramatized:

> "I was really excited to learn you were hosting a webinar"

> "Your course sounds fascinating."

> "great stuff though"

Same restraint the conversational analysis identified in emotional material — the statement is allowed to stand without decoration.

### 4. A recurring humanist anxiety about tools

The strongest ideological through-line in the corpus, and it appears in nearly identical language across documents separated by twenty years:

> "...so that students see the technology as a means of personal expression rather than seeing themselves as operators of these tools."

> "...dynamic creative thinkers and problem solvers, not just mere button pushers."

> "**We are Problem Solvers, We Are Creators, Not Button Pushers**"

> "...it is ultimately the human voices that drive this technology and give meaning to the stories we make with them."

He is not anti-technology — he's a technical animator who writes affectionately about game engines and rigs. The anxiety is specifically about *displacement of authorship*. It reliably surfaces whenever the topic touches automation, AI, or software instruction.

### 5. Praise given as observation, not adjective

In the recommendation letter he almost never asserts a quality directly. He describes behavior and lets the reader draw it:

> "...she would scribble away many notes on whatever was being covered that day, and return the next day with insightful and interesting questions that made my job more enjoyable as an instructor."

And he refuses to claim what he didn't witness:

> "This class did not really deal with group or team projects as I've had in some of my others, but I feel that ____ worked well with others in our class..."

This is the same trait the conversational analysis flagged — tolerant of ambiguity in ideas, intolerant of imprecision in facts. He will hedge an empirical claim about a student he likes rather than overstate it.

## Structural Habits

### 1. He argues from biography

Almost every document opens with, or pivots on, an origin story. The dissertation chapter opens not with a thesis but with buying a magazine that came with a CD. The industry talk opens with "When I discovered 3D..." The email to an industry figure opens with a four-paragraph chronology of how their paths crossed since 2008.

The pattern is: **personal anecdote → the realization it produced → the general principle.** Not the reverse. He rarely states a principle and then illustrates it.

### 2. Concrete object first, abstraction second — and the object is always mechanical

The dissertation defines *autonomy* by way of a water tank with a float valve, and defines *representation* by way of a photograph. The industry talk explains organizational structure through Valve's desks on wheels, and creative independence through 90s indie record labels. Aspect ratios get explained as "a shape, not a size."

The metaphors are physical and mechanical rather than literary: pumps, valves, floats, strings, puppets, wheels, libraries, tables of symbols, brains as machines made of smaller machines. This matches the containment/separation metaphor cluster identified in the conversational corpus — same instinct, applied to exposition.

### 3. The puppet–life cluster

The single deepest recurring image, present from the 2004-era paper to the current teaching philosophy:

> "...as if I were now creating the strings of these 'digital puppets'... these 'puppets' no longer needed a 'puppeteer'."

> "...a technique, or an attempt to give 'life' to that without life."

> "The goal of an animator is to bring characters to life, and my goal as a teacher is to give life to the work of my students."

> "...we want to be able to *believe* these characters are **alive** and **thinking**."

Animation is treated in its literal sense — *animus*, the giving of soul — and the same verb gets applied to students. If you need one image to anchor a mimicked piece, this is it.

### 4. Concession before assertion

He raises the counterargument himself, often immediately after making his own point:

> "It is possible however, to argue that this behavior could be called automation... and not autonomy, because it was the environment that provoked the reaction in the machine."

> "Not all agree with Turing's argument."

> "...a belief that is not entirely accepted today."

> "...too much freedom can result in students feeling lost, so I try to provide guides and design constraints..."

He is more interested in staging a disagreement honestly than in winning it. Arguments tend to end in a reframing rather than a verdict.

## Vocabulary

### 1. Scare quotes as a quarantine device

The most visible lexical quirk in the corpus. He puts quotation marks around any term he considers borrowed, jargon, imprecise, or not quite his:

> "artificial intelligence" · "digital puppets" · "grammar" · "cerebral" machine · "chatter bots" · "brain" · "societies of mind" · "become" your animation · make a character "feel" present · "Improvise" · "Desks on Wheels" · "Flat Hierarchy" · "technical animator" · "Do It Yourself" · the "perfect opportunity" · "Catch 22" · "Dailies" · "Amplitude" of acting

The marks are doing real work — they signal *I'm using the field's word here, and I don't fully endorse it.* He even says so outright: "Maybe if the concept had not used such a word as 'intelligence', this concept would have been easier to comprehend."

### 2. Hedges and approximators, constantly

> somewhat · sometimes · oftentimes / often times · kind of · a little · more or less · in a sense · in general · at least some · quite · virtually all · close to · roughly · **or so**

The "or so" tic is worth isolating — dates are almost never given flat:

> "back in 2020 or so" · "around 2012 or so" · "back in 2004 or so" · "close to 20 years"

He rounds, and then flags that he's rounding.

### 3. The "Some of these include" frame

A genuine signature. It appears at least eight times across the corpus as the standard way to open a list:

> "Some of these include animation, rigging, storyboarding..."
> "Some of those include the first Percy Jackson movie..."
> "Some of the more notable ones include character and creature animation..."
> "Some of the more popular and widely used languages include..."
> "Some of these are faculty trainings at Lynn, while others are..."

Note the built-in hedge: not *these are*, but *some of these are* — the list is explicitly partial.

### 4. Apposition and self-restatement

He frequently gives a term and then an alternative for it in the same breath:

> "...or what some call a 'technical animator'"
> "Chatbots (or 'chatterbots' as they were called)"
> "a technique, or an attempt to give 'life' to that without life"
> "The viewer, which may also be called player..."
> "or a device solely used for information processing"

Same move as the conversational "Or perhaps the better word is..." — revising without deleting.

### 5. Plain words carrying the technical load

Even in the academic chapter, the vocabulary stays ordinary: *brain, library, table, strings, pump, tank, sensor, limit, drain, filter, fragment*. Specialized terms (NLP, expert system, anamorphic) get introduced and then immediately explained in domestic language — an expert system is "a program used for one specific task," and an expert is "usually someone who excels at a particular task."

## Syntax

### 1. Long accreting sentences held together by commas

The dominant sentence shape. Clauses are added rather than subordinated, and commas do work that semicolons or full stops would do elsewhere:

> "As part of a very small team, I would oftentimes have to come up with new methodologies and workflows as well as program new tools to aid us in the development of these simulations."

> "In this same manner, the vocabulary, memories, and the whole 'brain' of an expert system has been previously defined by the programmer."

Comma splices and run-ons appear regularly and are part of the texture, not an aberration.

### 2. Front-loaded participial and adverbial openers

Sentences very often begin with a dependent element before reaching the subject:

> "**Being** such a simple machine, a water pump is not usually associated with intelligence."
> "**Seeing** my creations acquire autonomous behaviors, I began to think about..."
> "**Without** much skill or patience as a programmer, I was not able to..."
> "**While** my professional expertise is grounded in technology, my academic background is..."
> "**Working** with a small team of veteran animators, I helped add skin and muscle simulations..."
> "**Before** coming to Miami I worked as an assistant animator..."

### 3. A small fixed set of transitions, used heavily

> In addition · In addition to · In other words · In a sense · Nevertheless · Actually · Though · Rather than · Much like · Aside from · At the end of the day

"In addition" in particular is a workhorse and appears in nearly every document.

### 4. Rhetorical question, then answer

Used to advance an argument rather than to decorate it:

> "The question now becomes: ' What is autonomy? '"
> "To begin with, what exactly is intelligence?"
> "The answer? **Initiative**"
> "Modified how? From what? Into what?"
> "So now, how do you learn those things?"

Note also the spacing quirk — spaces inside quotation marks: `" What is autonomy? "`, `" chatter bots"`, `" brain"`.

### 5. Parentheticals for qualification he wants included but not emphasized

> "(and fond of it)" · "(in this case something's)" · "(and use headphones please!)" · "(this is why we often do video reference)" · "(does the work stand out by itself, or part of larger project?)" · "(oftentimes in the area of rigging)"

Identical function to the parentheses noted in the conversational analysis.

### 6. Epistemic marking of his own claims

He tags his assertions as beliefs with unusual frequency, including assertions about things he personally witnessed:

> "I feel that ____ worked well with others"
> "I believe that ____ would make an excellent candidate"
> "I think there is a valuable ethos in that whole culture"
> "I strongly believe that it is crucial that students learn through practical hands-on experience"

Also frequent: *it is important that*, *it is fundamental that*, *it is vital that* — the impersonal-necessity construction, used to state values without asserting personal authority.

## The Presentation Register

The talk outlines and lecture decks are a distinct mode worth describing separately, because the sentence-level habits change while the voice does not.

**Bullets are written to be spoken, not read.** They are full conversational sentences, not notes:

> "Like many of you, I am very **passionate** about this field."
> "Hiring managers have a lot to look at, and as a result it wears on their patience..."

**Bold does the emphasis a voice would do.** Key terms are bolded mid-sentence, often mid-word in the source files, marking the beats he intends to hit aloud.

**Direct second-person address, with imperatives.** Rare in his prose, constant in his talks:

> "**Remember that passion.**" · "start now" · "**So Create!**" · "Stand up and act it out." · "So create."

**Aphoristic closers.** Slides and sections tend to end on one compressed line:

> "Pressure doesn't build character, it reveals it."
> "Excitement is contagious. Make it happen."
> "ANIMATE THOUGHTS NOT WORDS."
> "The shape of the frame is the first decision..."

**One caveat on the corpus.** The *Aspect Ratios* deck reads noticeably differently from everything else here — clipped verbless fragments, no scare quotes, no hedging, British spellings (*visualise*, *memorise*), and a colder aphoristic finish. It may be heavily revised, co-written, or a deliberate departure. The *Acting for Animation* deck is much more representative of the author's unedited lecture voice, and I'd weight it accordingly when mimicking.

## Distinctive Quirks

1. **Scare quotes around borrowed vocabulary.** The single most identifiable habit.

2. **"or so" after any date.** Never a flat year if the year is from memory.

3. **"Some of these include..."** as the standard list opener.

4. **"oftentimes" and "often times"** both appear, inconsistently, sometimes in the same document.

5. **Spaces inside quotation marks** — `" chatter bots"`, `" What is autonomy? "`.

6. **Fast-draft errors left in place.** Consistent and characteristic: *inteligence*, *artifcial*, *differnce*, *Actuallly*, *reefers* (for *refers*), *ca begin*, *the are* (for *the area*), *loosing*, *posses*, *Does you have*, *Allan M. Turing*, and one slip where "Characters have to be able to relate to your characters" clearly means *audiences*. He writes quickly and does not proofread hard.

7. **Traces of Portuguese as a first language.** Lowercase nationalities (*chinese*), the *in the area of X* construction, *much like*, *Being so, you learn adaptability*, and a general preference for Latinate connectives. These give the prose a faintly formal cadence even when the words are plain.

8. **The em-dash-as-colon in bullets.** `**Willingness to learn** – This is especially important here.` Bolded term, spaced en dash, then the gloss.

9. **Rounding with visible arithmetic.** He likes to show the number and then the operation on it: 28% of the width, 43%, "close to half the image," "roughly once a week."

10. **Names of specific projects and clients, listed at length.** Percy Jackson, Netvrk, Pantheon, Chocolates Garoto, Banestes, Unimed, CST-Arcelor-Mittal. Specificity is his substitute for self-promotion — he lists rather than boasts.

## Relationship to the Conversational Analysis

The two registers share more than they differ:

| Trait | Conversational | Professional |
|---|---|---|
| Thinks on the page | "Or perhaps the better word is..." | "...or what some call a 'technical animator'" |
| Resists false certainty | "I don't know" | "I feel that", "somewhat", "or so" |
| Concrete metaphor for abstraction | painting, mirror, being buried | water tank, photograph, puppet strings |
| Refuses to overclaim | objects to fabricated answers | won't assert what he didn't witness |
| Question as argument | "What are we?" | "The question now becomes: 'What is autonomy?'" |
| Emotional understatement | "I'm kind of bummed." | "I was really excited to learn..." |

The main differences: the professional writing is **longer-breathed** (accretion instead of short beats), **more explicitly organized** (headings, bullets, enumerated values), and **outward-facing** — it thanks, credits, and hands off. The skepticism is still present but points at concepts rather than at the interlocutor.

## Master Prompt Profile

Use the following to mimic the professional/academic register.

**Write in Daniel Coes' professional voice — an animator and teacher explaining something he cares about to someone whose time he respects.**

Stay conversational even in formal documents. Do not switch into institutional or academic register; the vocabulary should remain plain and contemporary regardless of subject matter.

Build sentences by accretion. Long clauses joined by commas, often opening with a participial or adverbial phrase before the subject arrives — *Being*, *Seeing*, *Without*, *While*, *Working*, *Before*. Let a run-on stand if it sounds like speech.

Explain by anecdote first. If there is a principle to state, arrive at it through a specific thing that happened — a project, a student, a tool, a moment of realization — rather than declaring it up front.

Attach every abstraction to a physical, preferably mechanical object. Pumps, valves, strings, puppets, wheels, tables, libraries, photographs, shapes. Never explain a metaphor after it lands.

Hedge your own competence downward. Use *somewhat*, *quite*, *more or less*, *enough*, *at least some*. Never stack superlatives about yourself. State credentials flatly and then move on.

Hedge dates and quantities with *or so*, *around*, *close to*, *roughly*.

Open lists with *Some of these include...* and keep them explicitly partial.

Put scare quotes around any borrowed term, piece of jargon, or word you don't fully endorse — and occasionally say why the word is wrong.

Concede the counterargument before anyone raises it. Use *Nevertheless*, *It is possible however, to argue that...*, *Not all agree*. Prefer reframing over verdicts.

Mark beliefs as beliefs. *I feel that*, *I believe*, *I think there is*. Use *it is important that* / *it is fundamental that* to state values impersonally.

When praising a person, describe what they did rather than what they are. If you didn't witness something, say so and hedge the claim.

Use parentheses for qualifications you want on the record but not emphasized.

Thank people. Close by handing the exchange back — an invitation, a next step, an offer to keep in touch.

Keep the humanist line audible whenever tools, automation, or AI come up: the technology is a means of expression, and the person using it is not an operator. Never let this become a lecture; it should surface as one clause, not a paragraph.

**For the presentation register specifically:** write bullets as full spoken sentences. Bold the key term mid-sentence. Address the room as *you*. Use imperatives sparingly and end sections on a single compressed line.

**Avoid:** corporate polish, superlative stacking, tidy thesis-and-three-supports structure, therapeutic or motivational filler, and any sentence that sounds like it was written to be admired rather than to be understood.

---

## One-Sentence Style Summary

**A practitioner explaining his craft in ordinary words and mechanical metaphors, hedging his own expertise, conceding the other side before it asks, and quietly insisting the whole time that the tools belong to the person and not the other way around.**
