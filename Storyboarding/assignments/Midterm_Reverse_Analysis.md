# Midterm — Reverse Analysis
**100 points · Single submission**
**Rubric:** [`rubrics/Midterm_Reverse_Analysis.md`](../rubrics/Midterm_Reverse_Analysis.md)
**Handouts:** H1 (Notation) · H2 (Shot Vocabulary) · H4 (Continuity Rules)

---

## The assignment

You are given a finished sequence. You board it **backwards** — from the screen to the page — and then explain what the director did and why.

This is the one assignment in the course where you invent nothing. Everything you need is already on the screen. The test is whether you can *see* it.

---

## What to deliver

**1 · Shot log.** Every shot in the sequence, in order, with:
- shot size (H2)
- camera angle (H2)
- camera movement, if any
- approximate duration in seconds

**2 · Boards from observation.** Panels reconstructing the sequence. Framing, character position, screen direction, and eyelines should match what's on screen. Camera moves notated per H1.

**3 · Written analysis.** 500–800 words. See below.

**4 · Peer discussion.** Substantive contributions to at least two other students' analyses in the critique session.

---

## How to watch it

**At least three times.**

1. **Once for the story.** Don't take notes. Just watch it.
2. **Once for the shots.** Pause constantly. Log every cut.
3. **Once with a stopwatch.** Get the durations.

You cannot log shots you haven't watched, and it will be obvious in your submission which students tried.

---

## The written analysis

Not a summary. **The plot is not the analysis.** Nobody is grading whether you can describe what happens.

Your analysis must:
- Use shot terminology correctly and specifically
- Identify **at least three deliberate choices** and argue what each one *does*
- Name the sequence's structure
- Identify **at least one moment where the director broke a convention**, and say why it works

**Strong:** *"The sequence holds a single line of action for the first nine shots, then crosses it deliberately on the reveal — the disorientation lands on the audience at the same moment it lands on the character."*

**Weak:** *"The director uses many different camera angles to make the scene interesting."*

The difference: the first one names a decision and says what the decision does. Every paragraph should survive the question **"and so what?"**

---

## Terminology is graded strictly

This is where the course assesses film vocabulary, and vocabulary is the one thing here with objectively right answers.

- "It zooms in" when the camera **trucked** is wrong, and costs points
- "A medium shot" for something clearly a **medium close-up** is imprecise, and costs fewer
- "It's a cool shot" is not terminology

**H2 is open-book.** There is no reason to lose these points.

---

## You may disagree with the director

You may argue that a choice was a mistake. If the argument is specific and supported, it scores exactly the same as praise. What doesn't score is an unsupported opinion in either direction.

---

## What is not graded

**Drawing quality of the reconstruction.** These are observation boards. Stick figures in the correct positions outscore beautiful figures in the wrong ones, every time.

---

## Why this assignment exists

Two reasons.

The obvious one: it assesses whether you can read a sequence in the vocabulary this course teaches.

The less obvious one: **analytical reading is how you get better without a teacher.** After this course, every film you watch is available as instruction, but only if you have the habit of watching for decisions instead of watching for plot. This assignment is where that habit gets installed.
