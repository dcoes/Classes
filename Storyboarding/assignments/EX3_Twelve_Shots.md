# EX3 — Twelve Shots
**30 points · Assigned Session 3 · Due Session 5**
**Rubric:** [`rubrics/EX3_Twelve_Shots.md`](../rubrics/EX3_Twelve_Shots.md)
**Handout:** H2 (Shot Vocabulary) · **keep H2, the midterm is graded against it**

---

## The assignment

Twelve shots. Thirteen panels. **One subject, one location, twelve cameras.**

Pick a subject and a place and commit to them: *one character at a bus stop.* *One character in a kitchen.* *One character on a stairwell.* Then shoot it twelve ways without moving the subject or changing the location.

---

## The constraint is the assignment

You could illustrate twelve shot types with twelve unrelated drawings. Most people do, and it teaches almost nothing, because when everything changes at once you can't tell what any one change did.

**Hold the subject constant and the only variable left is the camera.** Now the twelve panels are comparable to each other, and the difference between a medium shot and an extreme close-up of *the same person in the same room* is visible in a way that twelve different subjects can never show you.

This is also what coverage actually is on a set: one scene, shot repeatedly, from positions chosen on purpose. You are doing the real task, at the smallest possible scale.

---

## The twelve

**Eight required:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **XLS** | Extreme long shot |
| **MS** | Medium shot |
| **ECU** | Extreme close-up |
| **Low angle** | Camera below eyeline |
| **High angle** | Camera above eyeline |
| **Dutch** | Canted horizon |
| **OTS** | Over the shoulder |
| **POV** | Point of view |

**Four free.** Any four more from **H2** — LS, MLS, MCU, CU, bird's-eye, worm's-eye, two-shot, insert, whatever you want. Name each one under the panel.

The eight are required so the assignment is gradeable against a fixed standard. The four are free because "as you understand them" is part of the point, and because H2 has more in it than eight entries.

> **OTS and POV need a second person or a looked-at object.** Plan for that when you pick your location. It's the most common reason a student gets to panel nine and has to start over.

---

## The captions — this is where the points are

Under every panel, **one line saying what the shot is for. Not what it is.**

- ❌ *"Low angle. The camera is below her."*
- ✅ *"Low angle — she has the power in this room now."*

- ❌ *"ECU on the hands."*
- ✅ *"ECU on the hands — we need to see the tremor before she speaks."*

The label is the vocabulary. The caption is the **judgment**, and judgment is what this course grades. A student who names all twelve correctly and writes twelve captions describing the camera position has demonstrated they can read a glossary.

This is also rehearsal for the midterm, where you do the same thing at length about somebody else's sequence.

---

## Panel 13 — the wrong shot

One more panel: **the wrong shot for this moment**, with one line saying why it's wrong.

An extreme close-up where the audience needed to see the room. A wide where the whole point was somebody's face. A Dutch angle in a scene with nothing wrong in it.

This is the first time in the course you defend a choice, and it is easier to argue against a bad shot than for a good one — which is why it's here and not later.

---

## What is not graded

**Drawing.** The subject can be a stick figure in an empty room. What is being assessed is whether the *camera* is where the label says it is.

**Ambition.** Twelve shots of a person waiting at a bus stop is a correct and complete answer.

---

## The one that fails most often

**Low angle and high angle drawn as "looking up at the face" and "looking down at the face."**

The camera has to actually be down there, or up there. That means the horizon line moves, the perspective changes, and things in the foreground get very large. If your low angle and your eye-level shot have the same horizon, you drew the head tilted, not the camera moved.

Session 6 is entirely about why. You're getting the problem two sessions before you get the tool, which is deliberate — you'll recognise it when it arrives.
