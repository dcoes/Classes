**Rubric:** [`rubrics/PR1_City_Park_Sequence.md`](../rubrics/PR1_City_Park_Sequence.md)

---

## Start here

> **What experience do you want the audience to have?**

Answer that in one sentence before you draw anything. Write it at the top of your beat sheet. Every decision below gets easier once it exists, and every decision you make that contradicts it is a decision you should reconsider.

---

## The assignment

Board a short sequence set in a city park. Two characters. One location. 15–25 panels.

Something happens between them. What happens is up to you, but it has to be something an audience could follow with the sound off.

---

## Why it's this boring on purpose

This assignment assesses **continuity and staging under constraint.** One location, few characters, nothing exotic. What's being tested is whether you can keep a space coherent across a cut.

Adding a car chase does not earn points, and it usually costs them — because continuity is harder to hold across a complicated sequence, and continuity is the entire subject.

The constraint is the assignment. Take it seriously and it is genuinely difficult.

> **Each milestone gates the next. A stage submitted without the preceding stage is capped at 50% of that stage's available points.**
>
> The cap cascades. Skipping thumbnails costs you the 20 points *and* caps your roughs at 12.5 — that's 32.5 points to save one afternoon. The arithmetic is not an accident.

---

## What each stage is

**1 · Beat sheet.** Every event in the sequence, in order, one line each, with how many panels you intend to give it. This is a list, not prose. It is also the direct assessment of a course objective, so do not treat it as a formality.

**2 · Thumbnails.** The whole sequence, small and fast, on your **EX2 working sheet** — the small-panel one, many to a page.

At least two beats staged **two different ways.** A second version only counts if it changes one of: **shot size · camera angle · blocking · which character the frame is on.** Redrawing the same idea more neatly is not a second version.

For each of those beats, submit both, mark your choice, and write **one line on what the rejected version did better.** That line is worth points, and you cannot write it honestly without having actually considered both.

What's graded here is the exploration, not the drawings. Nothing on the rubric rewards a polished thumbnail — and a thumbnail drawn at final-panel size is out of spec, because the point of a thumbnail is that you can throw it away.

**3 · Rough boards.** First full-size pass. Block the action. Establish the line, hold your screen direction, match your eyelines. Poses must read in silhouette.

**4 · Final boards.** The full sequence on your EX2 presentation sheet, notated per H1, with the H3 and H4 checklists actually run.

## The two things that fail most often

**Figure/ground separation.** Mid-value figure on a mid-value background. It is the number one reason student panels fail and it has nothing to do with drawing ability. Squint at your work. If the subject disappears, fix the value, not the drawing.

**Crossing the line without noticing.** Draw the floor plan. Mark the line. Put the camera on one side of it. This takes four minutes and prevents the most common continuity failure in the assignment.

---

## What is not graded

**Rendering.** Not once, anywhere on the rubric. A crude panel that stages the beat clearly outscores a polished panel that doesn't. Drawing in this course is treated as *notation.*

**Ambition.** See above.

## Critique

We critique this in Session 8. **Giving notes is graded** — peer notes are evidence for a course objective you can't meet any other way.

The rule in critique, here and for the rest of the term: **the note is data, not a verdict.** You will not defend your work while notes are being given. You may ask a clarifying question. You may respond after all the notes are in.
