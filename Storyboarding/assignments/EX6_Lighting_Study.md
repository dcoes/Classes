## Read this part first

**You choose the medium. All of them are worth the same.**

- Draw or paint over the supplied six-skull line-art `.psd`
- **Photograph one real object with one movable light** — a phone and a desk lamp is a complete kit
- Render one 3D object in any package
- Value-study from your own photographs

Nobody has to ask permission, nobody is being accommodated, and nothing on the rubric rewards one of these over another. The reason is in the objective: this assignment assesses whether you can **identify and control light**, which is not the same skill as drawing, and it should not be graded as though it were.

## The assignment

Two parts.

### Part 1 · The Read — 12 pts

Eight film stills are posted. For each one, name:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Key direction** | Where is the dominant light coming from? Front, side, back, above, below. |
| **High key or low key** | Which side of the shadow question is this on? |
| **Contrast** | High, medium, or low — how big is the gap between lit and shadow? |
| **Hard or soft** | Sharp shadow edges, or gradual? |
| **Motivated or stylized** | Is there a source in the frame that explains it? |

No drawing. No making. These have right answers, **H7 is open-book**, and this is the section that assesses the objective most directly.

### Part 2 · The Six Setups — 28 pts

Six images demonstrating:

1. **Three-point** — key, fill, back
2. **Side lighting** — key at roughly 90°
3. **High key**
4. **Low key**
5. **Underlighting** — key from below the eyeline
6. **Low contrast**

## The three constraints

These are what make a photograph, a render and a drawing gradeable against the same criteria. Skip one and the assignment stops working.

### 1 · One subject, six frames

**Same object. Same framing. Same medium. All six.** The only thing that changes is the light.

This is the whole experimental design. Six different subjects would let you cherry-pick whichever image happened to look good, and you would learn nothing about what the light was doing — because you'd have changed two variables at once.

### 2 · A light plan for every setup

A small overhead diagram: the subject, the camera, and every light with an arrow showing direction. Use the **floor-plan key from Session 4** — the same notation you use for camera positions, because it is the same problem. You are placing something in a space and recording where you put it.

**The diagram has to match the image.** If your plan says the key is camera-left and the shadow falls to the left, one of them is wrong.

### 3 · A three-value reduction for every setup

Submit each image **twice**: as you made it, and reduced to **three values only** — black, mid-grey, white. Posterize, threshold, or squint and repaint it. Any method.

> **The reduction is the graded artifact. The original is context.**

This is the trick that makes the assignment fair, and it's worth understanding rather than just complying with. A posterized photograph, a posterized render and a posterized pencil drawing are *the same class of object* — three shapes on a page. Asking "is the lit shape the subject, and is the light coming from where the plan says" is a question that does not care what made the image.

You already own this discipline. It is the three-value section of **H3**, which you have been running on your own panels since Session 5.

## The naming test

This is how Part 2 is actually scored, and you can run it yourself before you submit:

> **Cover the labels. Hand the six images to someone who is not in this class. Can they sort them to the six names?**

If they can, the setups read. If they can't, they don't — and no amount of rendering quality fixes that, which is exactly why rendering quality isn't on the rubric.

It's the same test as the silhouette test from week one, pointed at light instead of shape. Deliberately independent of skill level, which is what makes it fair.

---

## The one distinction people get wrong

**High key is not "bright." Low key is not "dark."**

They describe **how much shadow there is**, not how much light. You are working two dials, and they are independent:

- **Overall level** — how bright is the image
- **Contrast** — how big is the gap between the lit parts and the shadowed parts

High key is low contrast with the level pushed up: bright, open, few dense shadows. Low key is high contrast: most of the frame dark, small areas lit.

Which is why **low contrast** is a separate item on your list of six. Low contrast can happen at *any* overall level — a dim, flat, foggy image is low contrast and nowhere near high key. If your #3 and your #6 look like the same image, you have collapsed two ideas into one, and that costs you both.

## What is not graded

**Rendering. Photographic quality. Render quality. Resolution. How good the object looks.**

None of it. Not once, anywhere on the rubric. A phone photo of a coffee mug on a desk, lit six ways with a lamp and diagrammed correctly, scores full marks.

## Why this assignment exists

Every shot you will ever board has light in it, and most students board as though it doesn't — flat panels with no value structure, where the figure and the background sit at the same value and the panel dies.

You cannot direct light you can't name. Part 1 is the naming. Part 2 is proving the naming was real.

And the connection worth carrying forward: **the three-value reduction is the same tool as the silhouette test and the squint test.** One discipline, three names, appearing in composition, in staging, and now in light. When you see it a fourth time, you'll recognise it.
