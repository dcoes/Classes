# EX5 Continuity Strips
**4% of the course grade · Assigned Class 10 · Due Class 12**
**Rubric:** [`rubrics/EX5_Continuity_Strips.md`](../rubrics/EX5_Continuity_Strips.md)
**Handouts:** H4 (Continuity Rules) · H1 (Notation)

---

## The assignment

Six short sequences. Six different ways of getting from one shot to the next.

Take your **1.85: 1 template** and use it in strips: **each row is one sequence, read left to right.** Six rows, six devices, three or four panels each. Twenty panels total.

You are not telling a story here. You are demonstrating that you can execute six specific pieces of grammar on demand, which is the difference between having read about them and being able to use them.

---

## The six

### 1 · Reverse across a height difference: 3 panels

A short character and a tall character, talking.

| Panel | |
|---|---|
| **1** | Two-shot. Establish who is where. |
| **2** | The short character's view: low angle, looking up. |
| **3** | The reverse: high angle, looking down. |

**Required:** a floor plan in the margin, with the line marked and **both cameras on the same side of it.**

The trap here is that reversing the angle feels like it should mean reversing everything. It doesn't. If your tall character is frame-left in panel 2, they are still frame-left in panel 3.

### 2 · Match on action: 3 panels

Someone gets up from a park bench.

**Two requirements**, and they are what separate a match cut from two drawings of a person standing up:

- **The frame size must change noticeably** across the cut. A match cut between two identical framings is a jump cut.
- **The cut lands mid-movement**, not at the end of it. Draw the same instant of the rise from two distances. The action carries the eye across the cut and hides it.

### 3 · Shot / reverse shot, over the shoulder: 4 panels

| Panel | |
|---|---|
| **1** | Two-shot establishing the geography. |
| **2** | OTS on character A. |
| **3** | The reverse OTS on character B. |
| **4** | **Labeled BROKEN.** The same reverse, with the line deliberately crossed. |

Panel 4 is not a mistake you're being asked to avoid. It is a mistake you're being asked to **produce on purpose**, and it is the only proof available that you can see it. Everyone can nod at the 180° rule. Not everyone can break it deliberately and point at what went wrong.

### 4 · Cut-in: 3 panels

A bus speeds toward a stop. The driver hits the brake. The bus stops.

| Panel | |
|---|---|
| **1** | Wide: the bus, moving. |
| **2** | ECU: the foot hitting the pedal. |
| **3** | Wide: the bus, stopped. |

**Required:** on panel 1, **circle where the cut-in detail lives** in the wide shot.

That circle is the whole definition. A cut-in moves *closer into the same space*: the detail was always there, and you're going in to get it. If you can't circle it in the wide, you haven't drawn a cut-in. You've drawn a close-up of something else.

### 5 · Cut-away: 3 panels

A character waits for a bus.

| Panel | |
|---|---|
| **1** | The character checks the time. |
| **2** | **Cut away to something that is not in that space.** A clock in a shop window across town. An empty bay at the depot. The driver, somewhere else entirely. A dog asleep on a porch. |
| **3** | Back to the character. **The light has changed.** They are still waiting. |

**Required: name the job.** Write one of these two under the strip:

> **"Buys time."** The cutaway lets you skip twenty minutes with no title card and no dissolve. You left, something else happened, you came back, and the audience accepted it.
>
> **"Gives the audience what the character doesn't have."** We saw the empty depot. We know the bus isn't coming. He doesn't. That gap is the whole point of the shot.

**A cutaway leaves the main action.** That is the definition, and it is the entire difference between this strip and strip 4. Cutting to a *closer view of the same event*, or to someone watching that event from inside the same scene, is not a cutaway. It's a cut-in or a reaction shot. Both are useful. Neither is this.

### 6 · Cross-cutting: 4 panels

Two things happening in two places at the same time, alternating: **A / B / A / B.** They should be converging: heading toward the same moment, or the same collision.

**Required:** a floor plan for each location.

This is the one device on the list that only exists because of the strip. You cannot demonstrate cross-cutting in a single panel, and you cannot demonstrate it in two. It needs the rhythm, and the rhythm needs four.

## How this is scored

**Cover the labels. Hand the six strips to someone outside this class. Can they sort them to the six names?**

Same test as the silhouette test, same test as the lighting study. If a stranger can't tell your cut-in from your cut-away, the distinction isn't on the page, and that distinction is most of what the assignment is for.

---

## Format

- **1.85: 1 panels** on your EX2 template, one strip per row, read left to right
- Strips labeled by device name
- **H1 notation** throughout: shot numbers incrementing by ten
- Floor plans in the margin on strips **1, 3 and 6**
- One or two pages

---

## What is not graded

**Drawing.** Stick figures in correct positions beat beautiful figures in wrong ones, and this assignment is almost purely about positions.

**Story.** These are six unrelated fragments. Trying to connect them into a narrative will cost you clarity and earn you nothing.

---

## Why this assignment exists

Continuity is a system, and systems are learned by running them, not by hearing about them.

You have had the rules since Class 4 and the cut since Class 10. This is where you find out which of them you actually have. It is also deliberately placed **after** City Park rather than before, so that the rules arrive once as instruction and once as retrieval, which is how they stick.
