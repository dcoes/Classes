# H6: Acting & Staging Checklist
**CA 140 Storyboarding · Issued Session 9, at final-project launch**

> "It's stiff." "I don't believe them." "I can't tell what they want." These sound like drawing notes. They're almost never drawing notes.

---

## Before you call a pose done

- [ ] **What does this character want in this panel?**
- [ ] **Is there a line of action?**
- [ ] **Does the silhouette read?**
- [ ] **Is the weight believable?**
- [ ] **Is anything symmetrical that shouldn't be?**
- [ ] **Did I draw the face before the body?** (If yes, redo it.)

---

## The core question

> **What does this character want, right now, in this shot?**

If you can't answer that for a panel, the panel is decoration. Write the answer in the margin if you have to.

Better still, from Ed Hooks: **play an action, in pursuit of an objective, while overcoming an obstacle.** That forces you to board a *verb.*

---

## The body carries 80% of it

**Line of action.** One curve through the whole figure, drawn before anything else. It's the first mark you make; every other mark serves it.

**Straight against curve.** Rigid on one side, flowing on the other. A figure made only of curves is mush. Made only of straights, it's a mannequin.

**Silhouette.** Fill it black. Can a stranger name the action? This is the same test from week one and it is the single most useful thing in this course.

**Negative space in the pose.** Gaps between arm and torso, between legs. Closed shapes read; overlapping mush doesn't. If the arm is in front of the body, we lose it: move it off the silhouette.

**Weight.** Where's the center of gravity, and what's carrying it? Characters without weight look like stickers. It's usually one line: the contact of the feet.

**Asymmetry.** Shoulders and hips opposed. Head tilted off the spine axis. Nothing parallel. Symmetrical poses read as dead, formal, or robotic. Sometimes you want that. Usually you don't.

**Gesture over anatomy.** A loose gestural figure with clear intent beats a carefully constructed anatomically-correct stiff one, and takes a fifth of the time. Anatomy is a nice-to-have. **Intent is mandatory.**

---

## The face

**Faces last, not first.** Students draw the face first and then discover the pose doesn't work. Reverse it.

**Expression economy.** Three marks: brow · eye line · mouth. That's a complete expression at board scale.

**The brow does the work.** Same eyes, same mouth, four different brows, four different emotions. If you learn one facial feature, learn the brow.

**When the face doesn't matter.** Long shots, silhouettes, back-of-head, obscured faces. Sometimes withholding the face *is* the performance. Don't draw a face just because there's a head.

---

## Two or more characters

**The relationship is the composition.** Before you draw either character, decide what the relationship is. The staging follows.

- **Height and power**: who's higher in frame, who's looking up. This stacks with camera angle.
- **Distance and intimacy**: intimate, personal, social, public. Audiences read the gap instantly without knowing why.
- **Open vs. closed body language**: legible at thumbnail size, which makes it more useful to you than facial subtlety.
- **Three or more**: triangular staging, groupings as "sides," one character isolated. Who's grouped with whom is the story.
- **Staging the listener**: beginners board the person talking. **The performance is usually on the other face.**
- **Blocking across a sequence**, if your characters stand in the same places for twelve panels, ask why.

---

## Getting it right

**Reference is not cheating.** Every professional uses reference. The ones who don't have simply memorized more of it.

**Act it out.** Get up, do the action, notice what your body actually does. You own the best reference model available and it's free.

**Shoot it.** Phone video. Scrub for the extreme. Draw from that frame.

**Mirror.** For faces and hands specifically.
