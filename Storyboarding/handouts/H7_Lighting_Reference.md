# H7 — Lighting Reference Card
**CA 140 Storyboarding · Issued Session 11 · Open-book for EX6 Part 1**

> Five questions, six setups, and one distinction that half the class gets wrong. This is Deck 07 on a piece of paper, and it is what EX6 Part 1 is graded against.

---

## Reading any lit frame — five questions

- [ ] **Where's the key?** — front · side · back · above · below
- [ ] **High key or low key?** — how much shadow is there
- [ ] **Contrast?** — high · medium · low
- [ ] **Hard or soft?** — sharp shadow edges, or gradual
- [ ] **Motivated or stylized?** — is there a source in frame that explains it

Answer those five and you have described the lighting of any shot ever made. There is no sixth question.

---

## The two dials — the thing to actually understand

**Level** — how bright is the image overall.
**Contrast** — how big is the gap between the lit parts and the shadowed parts.

**These are independent.** You can have any combination of them, and that is the whole system.

|  | Low contrast | High contrast |
|---|---|---|
| **High level** | **High key** — bright, open, few dense shadows. Sitcoms, comedy, most animation. | Bright and harsh. Noon desert. Interrogation. |
| **Low level** | Flat and murky. Fog, dusk, rain. **Low contrast — but nowhere near high key.** | **Low key** — most of the frame dark, small areas lit. Noir, horror, thriller. |

### The error this table exists to prevent

**High key does not mean "bright." Low key does not mean "dark."** Both terms describe *how much shadow is present*, not how much light.

That is why **low contrast** is a separate item from **high key** on your list of six. Low contrast happens at any level. If your high-key setup and your low-contrast setup look like the same image, you have collapsed two ideas into one — and you're working one dial when there are two.

## The six setups

**Three-point** — key, fill, back. The default. Key does the modelling, fill lifts the shadow so it isn't black, back light separates the subject from the background. Learn this one and the other five are deviations from it.

**Side (90°)** — key at right angles to the camera. Half lit, half in shadow, maximum modelling. Reads as division, conflict, or a character with two sides. The most legible setup in a board because the value split is a shape.

**High key** — level up, contrast down, shadows open. Reads as safe, light, comic, or clinical.

**Low key** — contrast up, most of the frame in shadow, small lit areas. Reads as danger, secrecy, interiority. **Shadow is doing the composition here** — you're drawing with what you don't light.

**Underlight** — key below the eyeline, shadows travelling upward. Reads as wrong, because faces are almost never lit this way in nature. Horror uses it for exactly that reason.

**Low contrast** — small gap between lit and shadow, at whatever level you like. Reads as flat, listless, hazy, memory, or dream.

## Key direction, and what it does

| Direction | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Front** | Flattens. Removes modelling. Hides age and texture. Also removes drama. |
| **Side** | Maximum form. Splits the subject into two shapes. |
| **Back / rim** | Separates subject from ground. Silhouettes if there's no fill. The cheapest depth in lighting. |
| **Top** | Eyes go dark. Reads as oppressive, institutional, or holy, depending on how soft it is. |
| **Below** | Reads as wrong. See above. |

## Hard and soft

**Hard** — small source relative to the subject. Sharp-edged shadows. Bare bulb, direct sun, a phone torch.
**Soft** — large source relative to the subject. Gradual shadow edges. Overcast sky, a window, anything bounced or diffused.

The single controlling variable is **apparent size of the source**, which is why moving a lamp closer makes the light softer, not harder.

---

## Motivated or stylized

**Motivated** — the frame contains, or implies, a source that explains the light. A window, a lamp, a fire, a screen.

**Stylized** — it doesn't, and the film is asking you not to care. A key light with no possible source in a realist drama reads as a mistake; the same light in a musical or a horror film reads as intent.

Neither is better. But **know which one you're doing**, because an unmotivated light in a naturalistic sequence is the lighting equivalent of crossing the line by accident.

## The naming test

**Cover the labels. Can a stranger sort your images to the setup names?**

Run it on the **three-value reductions**, not the originals — black, mid-grey, white, nothing else.

That reduction is why this is fair. A posterized photograph, a posterized render and a posterized drawing are the same class of object: three shapes on a page. Whether the lit shape is the subject, and whether the light comes from where your plan says, are questions that do not care what made the image.
