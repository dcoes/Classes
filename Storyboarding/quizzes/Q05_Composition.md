# Q5: Composition
**Opens Class 6 · Covers Class 5 (Deck 05) and H3 · 5 drawn from 15**

---

### Q5.01 · MC
The single most common reason a student panel fails to read is:
- * Figure and ground sit at the same value
- The perspective is inaccurate
- The anatomy is wrong
- The panel is too small
> Source: Deck 05. H3, the checklist.

### Q5.02 · MC
Figure and ground separation has almost nothing to do with drawing ability because:
- * It is a value decision, and value can be fixed without redrawing anything
- It only matters in colour work
- Software handles it automatically
- It is a matter of taste
> Source: H3. PR1 rubric, the composition criterion.

### Q5.03 · MC
The squint test asks you to:
- * Shrink, blur or squint at the panel and check whether the subject and action are still legible
- Compare the panel against the one before it
- Check the panel at full print size
- Count the number of elements in frame
> Source: H3, "does it read at thumbnail size."

### Q5.04 · MC
When an instructor says a panel "doesn't read," the usual cause is:
- * Two elements are competing to be dominant
- The drawing is too rough
- The panel is the wrong aspect ratio
- There is not enough detail
> Source: H3, "is there ONE dominant element."

### Q5.05 · MC
Lookroom, sometimes called nose room, is:
- * Space in the direction the character is looking
- Space above the character's head
- Space in the direction the character is moving
- The gap between two characters
> Source: H3. Deck 05.

### Q5.06 · MC
Leadroom is:
- * Space in the direction the character is moving
- Space in the direction the character is looking
- Space above the head
- Space behind the character
> Source: H3. Deck 05.

### Q5.08 · MC
Two consecutive panels have nearly identical compositions. What is the consequence?
- * The cut is invisible in the bad way
- The sequence feels smoother
- Continuity is strengthened
- The pacing improves
> Source: H3, "does it contrast with the panel before it." Deck 05.

### Q5.09 · MC
This course treats composition as a question about:
- * Two frames, not one
- The single best arrangement of a frame
- The rule of thirds
- Balance and symmetry
> Source: Deck 05, composition for the cut. CURRICULUM.md boundary with Digital Painting.

### Q5.10 · MC
Black, mid-grey and white. Why does the three-value discipline get so much weight?
- * Value contrast does more work than line, shape, direction, size and texture combined, and the eye resolves it first
- Three values are faster to draw
- Printers reproduce three values most reliably
- It is a requirement of the template
> Source: H3, the three-value discipline.

### Q5.11 · MC
Most student panels feel flat regardless of how accurate the perspective is. The usual cause:
- * Only one plane is used, with no foreground or background
- The values are too dark
- The panels are too small
- The horizon is in the wrong place
> Source: H3, "depth, cheaply." Three planes.

### Q5.12 · MC
Value as depth means:
- * Near is darker and higher contrast, far is lighter and lower contrast
- Dark objects appear closer regardless of contrast
- Depth requires accurate vanishing points
- Value has no effect on perceived distance
> Source: H3, "depth, cheaply." Works even when the perspective is wrong.

### Q5.13 · MC
"Ballistics," in Deck 05, describes:
- * Information having momentum across a cut
- The speed of a camera move
- The arc of a thrown object
- How fast a sequence should be paced
> Source: Deck 05. PROJECT.md section 3.

### Q5.14 · MC
The "magician frame" describes design as:
- * Misdirection: controlling attention while concealing the mechanism
- A rigid set of compositional rules
- The illusion of depth on a flat surface
- A frame within a frame
> Source: Deck 05. PROJECT.md section 3.

### Q5.15 · MC
Applying the headroom, lookroom and leadroom line alone is worth doing first because:
- * It is objectively checkable and can fix half your panels in ten minutes
- It is the only one on the rubric
- It requires no drawing
- It matters more than figure and ground
> Source: H3, the checklist commentary.
