## 1. The course

**CA 140 — Storyboarding.** Computer Animation (CA) program.
Block schedule: 16 sessions × 2.5 hours, 4 days/week, one month.
Class size 8–20. Mixed majors: animation (majority), film, game production.
Not a capstone course.

**Conditions that shape every decision:**
- The Photoshop prerequisite is not reliably enforced. Assume some students have never opened it.
- Students do not buy the textbook. Tuminello is *recommended*, not required. All required content must live in these files.
- Many students arrive with no design, art, or film background at all.
- Attendance decays during work sessions. Every session ends in a posted exit ticket.

## 3. Pedagogy — non-negotiable

**1. Grade clarity and judgment, not rendering.**
The question is never "is this well drawn." It's "does this communicate, and did you decide it on purpose." Drawing is reframed throughout as *notation*.

**2. Silhouette readability is the recurring standard.**
Introduced Session 1, referenced in composition, staging, and every critique. Deliberately independent of skill level, which is what makes it fair.

**3. Milestone grading over long windows.**
Beat sheet → thumbnails → rough → final, each graded. Also the structural counter to AI-generated submissions, because process is what's assessed.

### Supporting commitments
- **Show, don't tell** is the through-line. Lectures model it, not just assert it.
- **Every concept lands on a decision** the student has to make.
- **The magician frame** (Deck 05): design is misdirection — controlling attention while concealing the mechanism.
- **Ballistics** (Deck 05): information has momentum across cuts.
- **Play an action, in pursuit of an objective, while overcoming an obstacle** (Hooks, Deck 09): forces students to board a verb.
- **Forward-looking by default** — see §7.

## 6. Style

- Slides carry short bullets. Speaker notes carry the prose.
- Speaker notes in spoken register, second person, contractions fine — what you'd actually say, including stage directions ("do this live," "let them answer wrong").
- Diagrams are native vector shapes on the deck's theme palette — editable, not embedded images.
- Copyrighted film stills are marked `[ADD IMAGE: ...]` in speaker notes and sourced by the instructor. Never generate or substitute copyrighted characters or frames.

## 7. The forward-looking lens

A declared commitment, not a unit. Appears as framing inside existing content.

- **Technological change:** the "make a picture of a thing" half of this job is under real pressure; the judgment half is durable. Say it plainly; do not reassure.
- **Societal change:** what artists make shapes culture, and that's legitimate to raise in a craft course.
- **Where it lives:** Deck 12 closing slide, Deck 10, Deck 11's job-market slide, and the pitch — the skill that survives every shift on this list.

## 8. The Pitch — gate model

Adapted from the CMU model: students pitched self-directed projects in place of assigned ones and had to convince faculty the time was worth spending.

- **Default path:** every student receives an assigned script for the final. Nobody is stranded.
- **Pitch path:** a student may pitch their own premise to replace it.
- **The gate is real.** A pitch can be declined; a declined pitch costs nothing but the swap. A gate that always opens teaches nothing.

**Boundary with Game Design:** that course owns the *business* pitch — market, platform, profitability, client POV. CA 140 owns the *sequence* pitch — performing boards aloud, pitching the timing, the *Snow White* precedent. A business pitch sells the idea; a story pitch makes the room see the film. State this distinction in class.

**Assessed on:** setup, performance, timing, identifying what the piece is about, receiving notes without defending. **Nerves are not a deduction. Defending instead of hearing the note is.**
