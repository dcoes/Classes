## You are the magician

A magician doesn't hide the trick by being fast. They hide it by controlling where you look. That's the entire job description, and it's exactly what you're doing when an audience watches a sequence you've boarded. They're not supposed to be thinking about the frame, the cut, or the staging. They're supposed to be inside the story.

The audience will look wherever you send them. If you don't send them somewhere, they choose, and they choose badly: value contrast, movement, a face, a line pointing somewhere, these aren't suggestions, they're instructions the eye follows automatically. An unplanned frame still directs attention. It just directs it somewhere useless.

Good composition is invisible. Bad composition is the only kind anyone notices, and "it looks weird" usually means "I looked at the wrong thing." That's your diagnostic: translate a vague complaint into where the eye went wrong, and you have a fixable problem instead of a feeling.

Design here isn't decoration or making things pretty. It's a set of tools for controlling where attention goes and how it moves. You already respond to all of it, from thousands of hours of watching film, television, and games. Today is about using it on purpose.

## Design for motion is not graphic design

A poster has one frame and unlimited time. You have many frames and about one second each, and your composition has to survive being replaced by the next one. Every principle in this lesson has two meanings: the version inside one frame, and the version across a sequence. The first half of this lesson covers single frames. The second half expands every rule into motion.

## The six elements

Six raw materials do almost all the compositional work: **line** (the mark between two points), **shape** (a self-contained area, geometric or organic), **direction** (every line has one, horizontal, vertical, or oblique), **size** (the area one shape occupies relative to another), **texture** (rough, smooth, soft, hard), and **value** (lightness or darkness). Notice color isn't on the list. It matters in a finished film and returns in the lighting lesson, but the overwhelming majority of storyboards are grayscale.

**Value does most of the work.** It's the first thing the eye resolves, before detail, before color, before a face reads. If the values are right, a crude drawing communicates instantly. If they're wrong, no amount of rendering saves the shot.

**Line** is mostly invisible, because most lines in a shot aren't drawn as lines: they're the edge of a building, the angle of an arm, a horizon, the direction of a gaze. Every line has a direction, and every direction carries a feeling. Horizontal lines read calm, stable, at rest, echoing the horizon and a body lying down; break a calm frame by breaking its horizontals. Vertical lines read strong and formal, standing up against gravity, but a row of verticals in front of a character is a cage, same element, opposite meaning depending on where the character sits relative to it. Diagonal lines read as movement, instability, danger, because they're literally what something looks like mid-fall or mid-run; this is why the canted angle works, rotating a stable frame turns every vertical into a diagonal at once. Curved lines read as natural and organic, and matter to you most as the line of action running through a figure: a pose built on a curve is alive, a pose built on a straight vertical is a mannequin. Jagged lines read as chaos, anxiety, damage, and they're tiring to look at; a frame that's entirely jagged has no resting place and the audience can't find the subject.

**Shape** is the next level up. When you thumbnail, you're working almost entirely in shape, big masses, no detail, and that's the correct order of operations: if the shapes are wrong, detail can't fix it. A triangle gives stability and a focal point at once; put whoever holds power in a scene at the apex and the composition states the relationship before anyone speaks. Circles and arches enclose: a group staged in a circle reads as a unit, and a character left outside it reads as excluded, instantly. Shapes also carry meaning on their own: round reads safe and friendly, square reads stable and reliable, angular reads dangerous and sharp. Look at almost any animated cast and the villain has sharper shapes than the hero. This applies to environments and props too, not just characters.

## You have about one second

A panel in an animatic may hold for twelve frames. The audience can't study your frame; whatever they get, they get immediately or not at all. Composition isn't a refinement added at the end. It's the only reason the shot communicates at all.

There's a rough priority order to where the eye goes: strongest value contrast first, then movement, then faces (especially eyes), then detail and texture, then everything else. Use this two ways: put what matters at the top of the list, or deliberately bury something low on it, which is how you plant a detail the audience only notices on a second viewing. Reading order matters too: left to right for most Western audiences, foreground before background, light before dark. You can violate all of it, but only on purpose; violating it by accident just leaves a shot that feels uncomfortable for no traceable reason.

**The squint test.** Squint at a panel until the detail disappears; what's left is roughly what the audience actually receives in half a second. If the subject vanishes when you squint, the panel has failed, no matter how well it's drawn. Shrinking a panel to thumbnail size is the same test.

## Figure, ground, and three values

**Figure and ground.** A subject has to separate in value from what's behind it: dark figure on light ground, or light on dark. The failure case, mid-value figure on mid-value ground, is the number one reason student panels don't read, and it has nothing to do with drawing ability. It's a decision, correctly makeable on the very first panel anyone ever draws.

**Three values is enough.** Black, mid-gray, white is a complete storyboard rendering system, and it's what most professional boards actually use. A fourth or fifth value is almost never because the shot needed it; it's because rendering is comfortable and deciding isn't. Limit yourself to three and you're forced to make the decision that matters: what's dark, what's light, what's neither.

**The silhouette test**, the standard this whole course grades against: fill the subject solid black, and ask whether a stranger can still name the action. If not, the pose has failed regardless of drawing quality. It's been used in animation for a century and is a hiring criterion for character design in games, and it's completely independent of skill level, which is why it keeps coming back. A badly drawn figure with a strong silhouette communicates; a beautifully drawn figure with a weak one doesn't.

**Value as depth.** Things farther away are lighter and lower in contrast, because there's air in the way. This atmospheric perspective is the cheapest depth cue available, and it works even when your linear perspective is wrong, which makes it an escape hatch worth knowing about, not a compromise; professionals rely on it too.

**Contrast** is the range between your lightest and darkest, and it does two jobs at once: it sets mood (high contrast reads dramatic, tense, aggressive; low contrast reads soft, flat, dreamy, or just dull), and it directs attention, since the highest-contrast area of a frame is where the audience looks whether or not you chose it. So choose it.

## Placement and balance

**Rule of thirds**: put the subject on an intersection, not dead center. Useful, and a little overrated, treat it as the answer when you don't have a better one, not a rule you must obey. Sometimes centering is exactly right: symmetry reads formal and controlled, which is why it shows up for thrones, altars, and villains, and it also reads instantly at speed, which is why fast action sequences sometimes center nearly every shot on purpose.

**Symmetrical balance** (equal weight on both sides of an axis) reads stable, deliberate, often institutional, and tells an audience that nothing is about to happen, which makes it a good setup for something happening. **Asymmetrical balance** (one dominant form offset by several smaller ones) carries more visual tension and is what most shots use most of the time, because it feels observed rather than arranged.

**Dominance.** Every frame needs a hierarchy: one dominant element, some sub-dominant, the rest subordinate. When a panel "doesn't read," it usually means two things are competing to be dominant and the eye can't choose. One dominant per frame. If two things matter equally, that's probably two shots.

**Positive and negative space.** The empty space in a frame is doing work too. Two characters at opposite edges of a wide frame with nothing between them: the empty middle is the entire content of the shot. It's the relationship, and it's what the scene is about even though nobody drew it.

**Headroom, lookroom, leadroom.** Three specific, checkable rules. Leave space in front of a look and in front of a movement; a character walking frame-right with all the space behind them feels like they're about to hit a wall. Give the eye somewhere to go. And when discomfort is the goal (trapped, cornered, about to be hit), take that room away on purpose. The audience feels it before they can name it.

## Framing, lines, and where the eye lands

**Frame within a frame**: doorways, windows, mirrors, arches, gaps, foreground shapes that surround the subject. It isolates the subject, creates depth by giving you a foreground, and can carry meaning, a character framed in a doorway is contained by it. It's also cheap: two dark shapes at the edges of a panel will do it.

**Leading lines**: roads, rails, fences, shadows, architecture, but also arms, gazes, weapons, outstretched hands. The eye follows a line to whatever's at the end of it, and the most useful leading lines aren't the obvious roads and rails, they're the ones built out of characters, lines you can add to any shot without changing the environment at all.

**Eyelines.** Where a character looks, the audience looks, almost involuntarily. This is why look-see-react works so reliably: someone looks, we see what they see, we see them react, three panels, complete storytelling. An eyeline is a composition tool and a continuity contract at once: show a character looking off-screen, and the audience now expects to be shown what they're looking at. That's where composition hands off to continuity.

## How the brain organizes a frame

**Gestalt.** The brain groups things automatically: by proximity, by similarity, by continuation. Put two characters close together in a frame, and you've said they're on the same side, without dialogue, and you can't easily undo it.

**Closure.** The brain fills in missing information automatically. This is the single most reassuring idea in the course: it's why a board made of rough shapes and three marks for a face communicates perfectly, the audience finishes it for you. Suggestion often beats depiction; the thing half-seen in shadow is more frightening than the thing fully rendered, because the viewer's own brain builds something worse than you could draw.

**Foreground, midground, background.** Three planes create depth instantly through overlap. Most student panels use one, which is why they feel flat regardless of how accurate the perspective is. The fastest fix available: add a dark shape in the foreground, a branch, a shoulder, a doorframe. Ten seconds, instant depth, and it frames the subject at the same time.

## Now the rules expand: across the cut

Everything above applies to one frame, and would be true in a painting class. You don't make frames, you make sequences, and every principle above gets a second meaning across time. This is where design for motion separates from graphic design.

**Rhythm has two meanings.** In space, it's repetition inside a single frame: a row of columns, a line of streetlamps, a crowd. Regular rhythm (even intervals) reads ordered or mechanical; flowing rhythm (curving, organic) reads natural; progressive rhythm (changing as it repeats) creates direction, which makes it a leading line too. In time, it's the pattern of your cuts, how long each shot holds: fast, slow, regular, syncopated, interrupted, a held silence where a cut was expected. This second meaning is closer to music than to design, and it's doing the same job beat and tempo do: creating expectation, then satisfying or denying it. Align both rhythms (a character running past evenly spaced columns, cut on an even rhythm) and a sequence feels inevitable. Break one against the other, and it feels like something's wrong, without a single drawing changing.

**Ballistics: information has momentum across a cut.** The audience doesn't reset at a cut. They carry what they just learned, and their eye is still physically pointed where the last shot left it. The question is never just "does this frame work." It's "where is the audience arriving from, and where am I sending them next." That trajectory, not any single frame, is what's actually being designed.

**Composing across the cut.** Consecutive shots should differ enough that the cut registers: contrast size, value, or density. Two similar compositions in a row leave the audience unsure whether anything changed, and the cut reads as an error instead of a decision. **Continuity of position** works the opposite direction: place the new shot's subject at the point where the audience's eye already landed, and the cut feels seamless; place it on the opposite side, and they have to hunt, felt as a small shock. Both are useful, smooth for conversation, jolting for a scare, but the choice has to be deliberate.

**The graphic match.** A shape in one shot echoed by the next ties two moments together across any distance in time. The classic is *2001*: an ape throws a bone into the air, and the cut lands on a spacecraft with the same shape in the same position. Four million years, one cut, and it works on composition alone.

**Motif.** A shape, object, or composition that recurs and accumulates meaning. In *E.T.*, the bicycle is established as the ordinary world, kids, suburbs, the everyday, so when it lifts off the ground and crosses the moon, the image is doing far more than showing flight: it's the ordinary transformed, and the audience understands instantly because the film taught the vocabulary first. This costs nothing but planning: establish something, then transform it.

**The visual echo** is the motif's most powerful form: repeat a composition exactly, and let what happened in between change what it means. *Star Wars* does it with the binary sunset, same framing, and Luke is a different person standing in it. If you take one thing from this lesson into your final project, take this one: plan your last shot to echo your first. It costs nothing but a decision made early, and it's the difference between a sequence and a story.

## The checklist

Six questions to run on every panel before calling it finished, none about drawing ability: Does it read at thumbnail size? Does the figure separate from the ground? Is there exactly one dominant element? Are headroom, lookroom, and leadroom correct? Does it contrast with the panel before it? Where does the eye enter, and where does it leave? Handout H3 has this printable. Every question on it is a decision you can make correctly on the first attempt, and every one improves the work more than another hour of rendering would.
