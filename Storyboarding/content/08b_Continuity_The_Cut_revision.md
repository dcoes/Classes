## What editing actually controls

Continuity editing compresses or stretches time depending on what the narrative needs, and it links the action of an individual shot to the bigger picture of the story. If it works, the audience never sees the manipulation, they see one continuous whole. Editing can compress three hours into ninety seconds, jump four million years in a single cut, or hold a moment past comfort. None of that is an effect layered on afterward. It's structural, decided at the board, and invisible when it works, which is why editing is one of the least appreciated crafts in film and one of the most decisive.

## Cutaway and cut-in

A **cutaway** is a shot related to the main action but not part of it: two characters talking, and the camera cuts away to a singer performing across the room. She isn't in the conversation, but she's in the world, and the cut adds context. Mechanically, a cutaway buys time: remove ten seconds of a conversation, cover the join with two seconds of somebody else, and nobody notices the surgery. Beyond the mechanical use, a cutaway can work psychologically: cutting away during a conversation can carry hesitation, avoidance, or a wandering attention, without a line of dialogue. Cutting away at the wrong moment tells the audience the moment didn't matter. Cutting away at the right moment tells them the character couldn't stay in it. Same technique, opposite meanings, and the difference is entirely where it's placed.

A **cut-in** is the opposite move: rather than cutting away from the main action, it narrows the view to a smaller portion of it, for dramatic emphasis. The main action is the game; the cut-in is the bat meeting the ball. Cutaway goes outside, cut-in goes closer. One buys time, the other buys emphasis, and they solve different problems.

## Cross-cutting

**Cross-cutting** (parallel editing) intercuts two actions happening at the same time in different places, manipulating both time and space at once, and it's the standard way to build suspense: the bomb ticking, the hero driving, the bomb, the hero. It also makes an argument: putting two things next to each other says they're connected, whether or not anyone in the film says so.

The clearest example is *The Godfather*'s baptism sequence: Michael Corleone stands as godfather at his nephew's christening while, intercut with it, his men murder the heads of the five families. He renounces Satan at the altar while the murders he ordered are carried out. Six minutes, and it moves fast. Neither sequence works alone: a baptism isn't dramatic, and five murders are just violence. The meaning is manufactured entirely by the intercutting, and not one shot had to be redrawn to make it happen. That's the argument for storyboarding a sequence rather than a shot: the meaning lives in the order.

## Transitions are narrative devices

A transition links two shots that differ in time, place, or focus, and it contributes to pace, lyrical or abrupt. It tells the audience what kind of gap they're crossing: same place a second later, or the other side of the world a year later.

**Cut**: over 90% of all transitions in film, and the audience doesn't notice a single one. Unobtrusive, and the best tool for compressing time. The default is correct almost always; reaching for something else needs a reason that can be said out loud. "It looked nice" isn't one, and it's the reason most student animatics have three dissolves too many.

**Dissolve**: one image superimposed over another, the first fading out as the second fades in, any length. Conventionally signals a time lapse or location change. A long dissolve is also an emotional device: holding two images on screen together asks the audience to consider them at the same time, a montage idea wearing a transition's clothes.

**Fade**: the screen goes to or from black (to white is a "white-out"). Denotes a passage of time and shouldn't be used in the present tense; a fade-in eases into a setting, a fade-out usually ends a scene or a film. A fade is the strongest punctuation available. Using one mid-scene tells the audience the scene ended, and they have to un-learn that when it hasn't.

**Wipe**: one image pushes the other off screen, functionally a cut, cosmetically a performance. Popular in the 1930s and 40s, out of fashion after the war, resurrected almost single-handedly by *Star Wars*, deliberately evoking the serials Lucas grew up on. A wipe carries a period association now, so using one is a reference whether intended or not; few techniques stay neutral forever.

**Jump cut**: the deliberate opposite of continuity. Same background, character position jumps; or same framing with the camera moved only slightly, so the subject snaps. It disrupts the illusion of continuous action on purpose, a broken rule used deliberately, exactly like crossing the line. The difference between this and a mistake is entirely whether the audience believes it was intended: one jump cut reads as an error, a sequence of them reads as a style.

## Montage: meaning in the gap

Cinematic **montage** is a series of images juxtaposed to create meaning by evoking an emotional response, shots that share a single idea or theme rather than a continuous action. This is one of the strangest and most important ideas in editing theory: meaning appears in the gap between two shots. Nobody drew it and nobody photographed it, the audience manufactures it.

Take two unrelated shots: students slumped over an exam in a classroom, and somebody asleep in bed, different times, different places. Put them together and an audience will read boredom, exhaustion, an exam so dull it put someone to sleep, or a student who stayed up all night. Every one of those readings comes from the audience, not the footage. This is the closure principle from the composition lesson, operating across a cut instead of inside a single frame: the brain completes what's left out. Same principle, different scale.

**Soviet montage**, developed in the 1920s by Sergei Eisenstein and Dziga Vertov, theorized montage as collision, two shots striking each other to produce an idea neither contains on its own. The Odessa Steps sequence from Eisenstein's *Battleship Potemkin* is the demonstration: soldiers' boots descending, a mother screaming, a pram on the steps. No single shot contains the massacre. The sequence does.

**Music video montage** is the modern everyday version: assembled around a song rather than a story, with cutting rhythm dictated by the track and shots relating to a theme rather than continuous action. It's the clearest possible demonstration that cutting rhythm is a separate variable from content: take the same footage, cut it to a different track, and it's a different piece. That's exactly what the animatic exercise proves: same panels, different timing, different film.

## The graphic match, again

A graphic match echoes a shape in one shot with a shape in the next, so a cut bridges visually rather than logically. *2001*'s bone-to-spacecraft cut has now appeared in three sessions this term for three different reasons: in composition it was about shape, earlier in continuity it was about the leap in time, and here it's about montage. Composition, continuity, and montage are the same subject looked at from three sides. If one structural idea survives the whole course, it's that one.

## Contrast and position across the cut

Consecutive shots should be different enough that the cut registers: wide to close, light to dark, empty to full. Two panels with near-identical composition don't read as a cut, they read as a glitch: the audience senses something happened but can't see what, which is the jump-cut effect arriving by accident. This is also the practical reason behind the shot-size progression from the fundamentals lesson: wide to medium to close guarantees consecutive shots differ enough to cut cleanly.

**Continuity of position** is subtler, and it's what separates competent sequences from good ones. When a cut lands, the viewer's eye is already fixed wherever the previous frame directed it. Put the next subject in that same screen area and the cut is seamless; put it somewhere else and the eye has to travel, costing a beat. Both are legitimate: matching position gives smoothness, deliberately displacing gives a jolt. What's not legitimate is not knowing which one happened. Check where the eye is at the end of a panel and where it's being sent at the start of the next.

## Motif and the visual echo, once more

A motif is a composition repeated on purpose so the repetition carries meaning; the second time the audience sees it, they measure the difference. The binary sunset in *Star Wars* returns with a different character standing in the same frame. Same frame, changed meaning: the whole trick, and it costs nothing but planning, which is exactly why it has to be planned at the boarding stage. An echo can't be added in the edit if the original was never shot. For a final project: if there's a shot worth echoing, decide it now, and place both panels in the same sequence deliberately.

## What this all comes down to

Continuity editing joins shots together to create meaning. Done well, the audience never notices how shots of different sizes and angles were spliced together; they see one continuous whole of uninterrupted action. That's what continuity means, and everything in this lesson, the cutaway, the cut-in, cross-cutting, transitions, montage, contrast, and position, is a way of deciding what happens in the gap between two panels before anyone else has to.
