# Lesson: What Is a Storyboard?

**CA 140 Storyboarding · Class 1 notes**

---

## The thesis of this course

A storyboard's only job is to make a sequence understandable and engaging, before anyone spends money making it. It gets asked one question: will this work? Beauty is optional. Clarity is not.

That distinction runs the whole term. This class grades clarity, not rendering and not draftsmanship. The question on every panel you turn in is whether the sequence communicates, not whether it's well drawn.

## What professional boards actually look like

Real production boards are rough. Scratchy, arrows everywhere, three marks for a face or no face at all. They're drawn fast and thrown away. A panel is disposable thinking, not a finished artwork, and thinking gets revised.

Board artists draw fast and loose on purpose. If "I can't draw" is the thing standing between you and this class, the honest answer is that neither can most of the people who do this professionally, in the sense you mean it. What they can do is notate an idea clearly enough that someone else can act on it. That's the actual skill, and it's covered later in this lesson.

## Visual storytelling is never accidental

We're surrounded by visual media constantly, and almost all of it is constructed. Somebody decided what you would see and in what order. That decision-making is the subject of this course.

Every piece of visual media is trying to do something to you: sell you something, make you feel something, or make you understand something. None of that happens without staging.

**Storyboarding**, formally, is the visual representation of the script: a number of panels showing the visual action of a sequence in logical narrative order. Its closest visual cousin is the comic strip, but a comic is the finished product and a board is the plan, which is why boards are allowed to be rough and comics aren't.

Stories entertain. Storyboards are production tools used to sell an idea. Hold onto that distinction all term: your board is not the thing the audience eventually sees. It's the thing that convinces people to build the thing the audience sees.

### Linear vs. nonlinear

Film, animation, and comics are **linear**: the audience receives events in a fixed order, and this is the grammar most of the course assumes. Games, interactive applications, and some forms of theater are **nonlinear**: the audience also manipulates the experience.

The grammar is the same in both cases. What changes is who controls the camera. Deck 10 spends a full session on exactly what breaks when you hand that control to a player.

## Show, don't tell

The oldest rule in visual storytelling, and the one this course grades against. When you tell a story, you ask the audience to imagine the content. When you show it, you hand it to them directly. Prose can do either. A board can only do the second.

Compare two descriptions of the same character:

**Telling:** *Jake, who was considered the bully of the neighborhood, stood on the corner of 10th and Main in his blue jeans, black T-shirt, and a cigarette pack rolled up in his sleeve. The rest of the kids avoided the corner where Jake stood.*

**Showing:** *Jake, a burly teen with pock marks on his cheeks and a skull tattoo scrolled across his bicep, kicks a scuffed military boot in the dirt. His eyes dart to several schoolchildren standing in a single-file line waiting for their bus. He settles his gaze on a pint-size boy with a space-rangers backpack, smirks, and takes a long drag from his cigarette.*

Ask a room to picture the first version and you get a dozen different images. Ask them to picture the second and the answers converge. That convergence, everyone building roughly the same picture in their head, is the entire value of a storyboard: it's a document a crew can build the same thing from.

The same principle scales down to a single image: a mother absently touching frayed toys in an empty child's room says more than "the child no longer lives at home," and says it faster. Leave enough in the frame to inform, and let the audience decide how they feel about it. An audience that works slightly for the meaning holds onto it. An audience that's told, forgets.

## Structure: the shape underneath every sequence

Most stories start as an idea with no structure at all: a dream, an image, a fragment. That's normal. Structure gets added afterward, deliberately, which means it's a craft skill rather than a talent.

The classic shape is beginning, middle, end: act one, act two, act three. A more useful modern version comes from game design. Jesse Schell's **interest curve** holds that the quality of an experience can be measured by the pattern of interest it creates over time. Attention isn't constant. It rises and falls, and managing that rise and fall is your job, shot by shot. This maps directly onto animatic pacing, which you'll work with directly later in the term.

Every sequence needs a **hook**: an opening beat that raises a question and withholds the answer. A hook doesn't have to be big. A phone ringing that nobody answers is a hook. A suitcase being packed is a hook. What matters is that the audience now wants something they don't have.

## The monomyth as a staging tool

Joseph Campbell's *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949) noticed that hero myths from cultures with no contact with each other keep repeating the same shape. He called it the monomyth.

This isn't a screenwriting lesson. Every stage of the monomyth is a staging problem: knowing which stage of the story you're in tells you where to put the camera.

**Ordinary World.** "Normal" has to be established before it can be disrupted, and normal is genuinely hard to make interesting. Use wide establishing shots at a neutral eye level, repetition as visual rhythm, and frame the hero slightly out of place (off-center, wrong scale, alone in a crowd). You're not telling the audience the hero is restless. You're staging them so the audience concludes it.

**Call to Adventure.** The audience has just been trained on a visual rhythm; the call is where you break it, and the break should register physically before it registers intellectually. Change something structural (shot size, camera height, cutting rate), not just the content of the frame. A call that arrives in the same visual language as the ordinary world doesn't feel like a call.

**Refusal.** Showing someone *not* doing something is harder than showing action. Two tools: blocking that physically moves the character away from the goal, and held shots, since reluctance reads as duration. This is the first stage where a timing decision carries story weight.

**Crossing the Threshold.** The most reliably visual moment in the whole structure, because it usually arrives pre-visualized: a door, a gate, a tunnel, a horizon, the water's edge. Every other stage asks you to invent a visual equivalent for an abstract idea. This one is already a physical action in a physical space. If your story has a threshold moment and you haven't built a literal threshold into the frame, you've left the easiest shot in the film on the table.

**The New World.** The audience has been dropped somewhere unfamiliar and needs re-orienting, fast. New establishing shots, and a visual language that deliberately contrasts Act One: different camera height, different value range, different lens behavior. Scale shots help too, a hero who was competent in the ordinary world should look small here. The mistake is making the new world look like the old one, which wastes the threshold you just built.

**Tests, Allies, Enemies.** The middle of a story is a series of obstacles, and they have to feel progressively worse. Escalation is a compositional job, not a writing job: repeat a shot setup and change exactly one variable (same framing, closer; same framing, darker). The audience reads the difference without being told. If shot 40 looks like shot 10, it doesn't feel harder.

**The Ordeal.** The climax needs its own grammar. Your widest and your tightest shots probably both live here, the cutting rate changes, and this is where you spend your best drawing. Most student boards are rendered evenly from the first panel to the last, which means nothing is emphasized. Budget your effort the way you budget your shots.

**The Return.** How do you show someone has changed? Repeat a shot from Act One and let everything that happened in between change what it means. Same framing, different person standing in it. This is the single most useful tool in the whole lecture, and it costs nothing but planning ahead: plan your last shot to echo your first.

## Who uses storyboards, and how the job changes

Storyboards show up in advertising, video games, animation, feature films, television, and previs, a large and growing department on most big productions. The underlying skill transfers across all of them, but the job itself isn't identical everywhere:

- **Animation:** the board *is* the film. Everything downstream follows from it.
- **Live action:** boarding is targeted at anything complex or expensive enough that guessing is unaffordable.
- **Advertising:** boards are highly finished, key frames only, made to sell a client rather than instruct a crew.
- **Games:** cinematics work like film, but there's a whole additional category of problem that comes from a player controlling the camera. That gets its own session later in the course.

In almost every professional context, you're boarding someone else's idea: a director's, a production designer's, an animation director's. That sounds limiting, and it isn't. The skill being hired is turning a description into something clearer than the description was, and then noticing the thing the director hadn't thought of and offering it. That second part is the difference between a board artist people request by name and one they don't.

## "I can't draw"

This objection is in the room whether or not anyone says it out loud, so it's worth answering directly: you aren't being asked to draw. You're being asked to **notate**. The bar is whether someone else can understand what you put down, and that's a learnable skill with a low floor.

**The three-shape figure.** Draw the line of action first, one curve describing what the body is doing, then hang three shapes on it: head, ribcage, pelvis. The order matters. Build the shapes first and try to add energy afterward, and you get a mannequin.

**The silhouette test.** This is the grading standard for the whole term, and it's the first of three fairness checks you'll meet in this course (the others come later, in continuity and lighting). Fill the pose in solid black. If the action doesn't survive being reduced to a silhouette, the pose has failed, no matter how well it's drawn. This test has been used in animation for a century, and it's completely independent of skill level: a badly drawn figure with a strong silhouette communicates, and a beautifully drawn figure with a weak one doesn't. In this class, the first one gets the better grade.

**Economy.** One curve for the spine. Hands are mittens. Faces are three marks, or nothing at all. Detail belongs only where it carries the story; everywhere else, suggest and let the audience fill it in, which they will do automatically. The classic beginner mistake is a beautifully rendered face on a pose that doesn't read, an hour spent on the least important part of the panel.

**Notation.** A motion arrow shows what the subject does. A camera arrow shows what the camera does: PAN, TRUCK IN/OUT, TILT, CUT, HOLD. Never use one arrow style for both; they mean completely different things and a crew will read them literally. Number every panel, always. Learn this shorthand properly now and you'll never again write a paragraph where an arrow would do.
