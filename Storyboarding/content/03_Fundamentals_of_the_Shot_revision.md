## Shot, scene, sequence

Stories break down into three nested units. A **shot** is a continuous view from one camera position: camera on, camera records, camera off, that's a shot, and moving the camera to a new position starts a new one even if the action continues. A **scene** is a group of shots sharing one location: when the location changes, you owe the audience a new establishing shot. A **sequence** is a group of scenes sharing one dramatic purpose, and it can move between locations and still be one sequence because it's one piece of story doing one job.

Think sentence, paragraph, chapter. Your final project is a sequence, not a film and not a scene. That's the unit this course works in, and it's the unit the industry hires for.

A typical feature runs around 1,200 shots: twelve hundred separate decisions about where the camera goes and how long it stays. You aren't going to invent each of those from scratch. A shared vocabulary is a set of pre-solved problems, so your actual judgment goes toward the ten shots that matter and the other 1,190 get handled cheaply.

## What the director decides

The job is to find the viewpoint that's both the most visually interesting and the most legible. When those two pull against each other, clarity wins, which is the standard this course grades against. From there, three questions in order: how is it framed, what angle expresses the mood, does the camera move. Framing first because it's the biggest decision; movement last because it's the most expensive.

Genre changes the shot list before you've read a word of the script. Drama moves slower and sits closer: more medium shots and close-ups, longer holds, fewer moves. Action goes wider and faster, with more movement and more cuts. Where a thing gets watched matters too: commercials, games, and industrial video have historically played on smaller screens, favoring closer framing, while features can hold a broad, panoramic shot. It's the same argument as vertical video from the aspect ratio lesson: delivery size changes the shot, and it always has.

The default progression for a scene is wide to medium to close-up and back to wide or medium to reorient. It works, which is exactly why it's also what every student board looks like in week two: a sign nobody made a decision. Learn it so you can notice when you're on autopilot, and then ask what the scene gains if you refuse it: opening on the close-up, or never giving the audience the wide at all.

## Shot size: framing by how much of the subject fills the frame

**Extreme Long Shot (XLS/ELS).** The establishing workhorse: a skyline, a landscape, a castle in the distance. Scale is the point, and scale needs a human-sized reference in frame, a horse next to the castle, a figure on a ridge, or a big landscape reads as texture rather than place. A gesture is invisible at this size; anything a character does here has to be enormous (a fall, a wave, a silhouette against the sky).

**Long Shot / Wide Shot (LS).** Full figure, head to toe, environment still doing work around them. This is the most important shot in a sequence because it sets the geography: where we are, who's there, what's happening. If an audience gets lost later, it's usually because this shot was skipped or unclear. Games sit at roughly this distance almost permanently, since third-person and isometric cameras keep the player character full-figure so the player can read the space. At this size, movement has to be exaggerated to read at all, which is why game animation looks broader than film animation: a craft decision, not a limitation.

**Full Shot (FS).** The entire height of a person, head near the top of frame, feet near the bottom. Clipping either end reads as a mistake rather than a choice, and it's the single most common framing error in student work. Decide whether the feet are in or out; don't let it happen by accident.

**Medium Shot (MS).** Waist up. The conversation default and the most common shot in narrative film, because it's the compromise: enough body for gesture and posture, enough face for expression. It's also, because it's the default, the least interesting choice available. Use it, but know that you chose it. With two characters in a medium shot, staging starts to matter more than framing, since now there's a relationship to compose: who's higher, who's closer to camera, who's turned away. Be deliberate about what moves in frame; the eye goes to motion automatically, so movement anywhere but your subject steals attention you needed.

**Close-Up (CU).** Shoulders to the top of the head. The performance shot, where the movements are small: a brow, an eye line, a swallow. Everything the long shot needed exaggerated, the close-up needs restrained. Same actor, same beat, completely different amount of movement reads correctly: shot size is a direction to the animator.

**Extreme Close-Up (ECU/XCU).** Fills the frame with a detail: an eye, a mouth, a hand, a doorknob. It does two jobs at once: raises tension by withholding context, and tells the audience this specific object matters, which is a promise you now have to pay off. If you show the knife that close, the knife had better be used.

**By population, not size:** a **single** isolates one person, usually a medium or close-up; cutting from a two-shot to a single separates characters and the audience feels it before they think about it, which is how an editor makes a room feel like it's splitting apart. A **two-shot** puts two characters in frame together (facing each other, one behind the other, side by side) and states that they're together; holding it through a whole conversation refuses to let the audience take sides. It's also the shot the wide ratios from last class buy you: in 2.39:1 you can hold two people at opposite edges of the frame with the whole world between them. An **insert** is a close detail cut into a wider master, a photo on a mantelpiece, a hand on a doorknob, and the cheapest way to give the audience information the characters don't have.

## Camera angle: where the camera sits relative to the subject

Angle is where the camera stops being a recording device and starts being a point of view. It carries feeling, and it creates depth: a three-quarter view separates foreground from background in a way a flat frontal shot can't.

**High angle**, camera above and tilted down, diminishes: the character is smaller, the world bigger, and they're being looked down on by the camera and therefore the audience. It's also a practical staging tool, since a high angle shows the floor, and therefore where everyone is standing.

**Low angle**, camera below and tilted up, suggests dominance and scale. It works hardest when the character is also positioned higher in frame, the two effects stack. Which means you can also make them contradict: shoot a physically small character from a low angle and you've said something specific about how another character sees them.

**Eye level** is neutral: the camera meets the character's eyes, and the audience meets them as an equal. Neutral isn't the same as boring or as no decision. In a scene where everything else is tilted and canted, eye level is the loudest thing in the room.

**Bird's eye**, directly overhead, is the god view: the audience sees the whole board and the character can't. Useful for geography, and for making a person look like a piece on a table. It also destroys faces, so use it when position matters more than performance.

**Canted / Dutch angle** tilts the frame off its axis so the horizon runs diagonally: unstable, unhinged, wrong. It works because the audience's sense of level isn't negotiable, you're lying to their inner ear. It's also the most conspicuous angle available, which makes it the easiest to overuse. One canted shot in a sequence is a statement; six is a style, and usually an annoying one.

**Tilt** is a fixed camera pivoting on its vertical axis, up or down. Note it does double duty as both an angle and a camera move; as a move, tilting up a building reveals its height by withholding it one story at a time. A reveal is a tilt with a reason.

**Three-quarter (45°)**, between frontal and profile, is the strongest general-purpose angle: it separates foreground, subject, and background into distinct planes. If you don't know what angle to use, use this one. It's the rule of thirds of camera angles, a default that's almost never wrong and almost never remarkable.

**Frontal**, camera straight on, is flat since it loses the depth a three-quarter gives you, but in close-up it turns intimate, and it's the angle for subjective shots, a character addressing the camera directly.

**Profile**, directly from the side at eye level, is observational and formal. It refuses you the eyes, and therefore most of the performance, which is sometimes exactly what you want: a character withholding, or a moment the audience watches rather than shares. Two profiles facing each other is the most neutral possible staging of a conflict; nobody has the advantage, including the audience.

**Over the shoulder (OTS)** places the camera behind one character, over their shoulder, focused on the other. It's the workhorse of dialogue coverage, and unlike a two-shot, it puts you in a position: you're standing with this person, looking at that one. It's also a continuity trap. The moment you shoot an OTS, you've committed to a side of the line, which comes back in the continuity lesson.

## Camera movement: a character in its own right

Before adding a move, ask what it adds, and what it costs. A moving camera is expensive in every medium: a crane, a dolly, a track and a crew in live action; a background rebuilt wide enough to move across in animation; artwork drawn larger than the frame in an animatic. Every move you board is a move somebody has to build.

Notating movement on a flat page needs two different arrows, and they aren't interchangeable. A **subject arrow** sits on the thing that moves and follows its path. A **camera arrow** sits outside or across the panel and says the frame itself is moving. Conflate them once and an animator builds the wrong shot: the character walks left instead of the camera panning left, and nobody catches it until it's animated. Label them (CAM and SUBJ) any time there's doubt.

**Crane**, a camera on a crane or jib moving vertically through space, covers distance and produces angles otherwise unreachable, reading as soothing, exhilarating, or menacing depending on context. In 3D, a crane is free, a virtual camera has no weight, no crew, no risk, which is why animated films and games use moves that would be impossible or lethal in live action. Just because the camera can go anywhere doesn't mean it should.

**Pan**, a fixed camera pivoting horizontally, turns but doesn't travel, and directs attention from one part of a location to another. It's the cheapest way to connect two things without cutting. A fast pan is a **swish pan**, fast enough to blur, used as a violent transition. For an animatic, a pan means drawing wider than the frame: plan the move before drawing the panel, or the artwork won't be there.

**Dolly / truck** moves the camera itself toward or away from the subject. This is the distinction students get wrong most often: a dolly changes your relationship to the space, foreground and background shift against each other and the world gets more three-dimensional. A zoom does none of that.

**Tracking** is a dolly move that runs alongside the subject rather than toward or away from it, the walk-and-talk shot, making the audience feel like they're accompanying the characters rather than observing them. It's also the most demanding move to board, since the background changes continuously: effectively you're drawing a very wide panel and choosing which slice of it the frame sits on at each moment.

**Zoom** keeps the camera exactly where it is; the lens changes focal length. Zooming in gets "tighter," zooming out gets "looser." A zoom is a crop, not a move: it magnifies what was already there and flattens the space while doing it. Audiences read it as an effect rather than travel, which is why zoom-heavy footage dates so visibly.

**Steadicam**, a camera in a body-mounted harness, lets an operator walk, run, and climb stairs while the frame stays smooth, giving film the ability to follow a character anywhere with the fluidity of a dolly and the freedom of handheld. It's a boarding problem because it has no cuts to hide behind: board an unbroken two-minute take and every composition in it has to work, and every transition between them has to be motivated by the character's own movement.

**Zolly (dolly zoom)** dollies one direction while zooming the other, so the subject stays the same size in frame while the background expands or collapses behind them. Hitchcock invented it for *Vertigo*; the famous modern example is Brody on the beach in *Jaws*, the moment he realizes what he's looking at. It's the only camera move with no equivalent in human vision, which is exactly why it feels like the floor dropping out, and why it only works once per film.

## Camera perspective: whose eyes the audience is borrowing

**Objective** places the camera in a neutral, unbiased position: the camera is nobody, the audience observes from the sidelines. This is the default in most film, and like every default in this course, it's only a good choice when you know you made it.

**Point of view (POV)** sits the camera near a specific character's viewpoint, roughly where their eyes are, seeing roughly what they see. It increases involvement, since the audience is borrowing somebody's position, and it commits you: once a POV is established, cutting away from it means something. POV is not the same as subjective. POV is near the eyes; subjective is in them.

**Subjective** trades places with the character entirely: the audience sees through their eyes. It's the most personal and demanding option, since it collapses the distance completely, which is why it's used sparingly in film and permanently in first-person games. Everything covered today about choosing an angle assumes you own the camera. Deck 10 covers what happens when a player owns it instead.

## In conclusion

Shot, scene, sequence: three nested units. Framing is the distance from camera to subject. Angle is where the camera sits relative to the subject, and it carries the feeling. Movement adds dynamism, and costs somebody real work. Perspective is whose eyes the audience is borrowing.

Handout H2 puts every size, angle, movement, and perspective from this lesson on one page, and it's open-book for the midterm: there's no reason to lose those marks. But be honest about what it does and doesn't do. That page will tell you what a shot is called. It will not tell you which shot to use. That decision is the rest of the course.
