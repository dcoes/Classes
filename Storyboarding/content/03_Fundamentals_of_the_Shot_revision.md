# Lesson: Fundamentals of the Shot

## Shot, scene, sequence

A **shot** is a continuous view from one camera position. Camera on, camera records, camera off, that's a shot. Moving the camera to a new position starts a new one, even if the action keeps going. A **scene** is a group of shots that share one location: change the location and you owe the audience a new establishing shot. A **sequence** is a group of scenes sharing one dramatic purpose. It can move between locations and still count as one sequence, because what makes it a sequence is that it's one piece of story doing one job.

Think sentence, paragraph, chapter. Your final project is a sequence, not a film and not a scene. That's the unit this course works in, and it's the unit the industry hires for.

A typical feature runs around 1,200 shots or so. That's twelve hundred separate decisions about where the camera goes and how long it stays. Nobody invents each from scratch. A shared vocabulary is a set of pre-solved problems, so your judgment can go toward the ten shots that matter while the other 1,190 get handled cheaply.

## What the director decides

The job is to find the viewpoint that's both the most visually interesting and the most legible. When those two pull against each other, clarity wins. That's the standard this course grades against. From there, three questions, roughly in order: how is it framed, what angle expresses the mood, does the camera move. Framing comes first because it's the biggest decision. Movement comes last because it's the most expensive one.

Genre changes the shot list before anyone's read a word of the script. Drama tends to move slower and sit closer: more medium shots and close-ups, longer holds, fewer moves. Action goes wider and faster, with more movement and more cuts. Where a thing gets watched matters too. Commercials, games, and industrial video have historically played on smaller screens, so they favor closer framing, while a feature can hold a broad, panoramic shot and trust the screen to carry it. It's roughly the same argument as vertical video from the aspect ratio lesson: delivery size changes the shot, and it always has.

The default progression for a scene is wide to medium to close-up, then back out to wide or medium to reorient. It works, which is why it's also what every student board looks like around week two, a sign nobody made a decision. Learn it well enough to notice when you're on autopilot, and then ask what the scene gains if you refuse it: opening on the close-up, say, or never giving the audience the wide at all.

## Shot size: framing by how much of the subject fills the frame

**Extreme Long Shot (XLS/ELS).** The establishing workhorse: a skyline, a landscape, a castle in the distance. Scale is the point, and scale needs a human-sized reference somewhere in frame, a horse next to the castle, a figure on a ridge, or the landscape reads as texture instead of a place. A gesture is basically invisible at this size, so anything a character does here has to be enormous: a fall, a wave, a silhouette against the sky.

**Long Shot / Wide Shot (LS).** Full figure, head to toe, with the environment still doing work around them. This sets the geography: where we are, who's there, what's happening. Games sit at roughly this distance almost permanently, since third-person and isometric cameras keep the player character full-figure so the player can read the space around them. At this size, movement has to be exaggerated to read at all, which is why game animation tends to look broader than film animation.

**Full Shot (FS).** The entire height of a person, head near the top of frame, feet near the bottom. Clipping either end reads as a mistake rather than a choice, and it's the single most common framing error in student work. Decide whether the feet are in or out. Don't let it happen by accident.

**Medium Shot (MS).** Waist up. The conversation default and the most common shot in narrative film: the compromise between body and face. It's also the default, which makes it the least interesting choice available. Use it, but know that you chose it. With two characters in a medium shot, staging starts to matter more than framing: who's higher, who's closer to camera, who's turned away. Be deliberate about what moves in frame. The eye goes to motion involuntarily, so movement anywhere but your subject steals attention you need.

**Close-Up (CU).** Shoulders to the top of the head. The performance shot, where the movements are small: a brow, an eye line, a swallow. Everything the long shot needed exaggerated, the close-up needs restrained. Same actor, same beat, a completely different amount of movement reads as correct. Shot size is a direction to the animator.

**Extreme Close-Up (ECU/XCU).** Fills the frame with a detail: an eye, a mouth, a hand, a doorknob. It does two jobs at once: it raises tension by withholding context, and it tells the audience this specific object matters, which is a promise you now have to pay off. If you show the knife that close, the knife had better get used.

**By population, not size:** a **single** isolates one person, usually in a medium or close-up. A **two-shot** puts two characters in frame together (facing each other, one behind the other, side by side) and states that they're together. Holding it through a whole conversation refuses to let the audience take sides. The wide ratios from last class buy you this: in 2.39:1 you can hold two people at opposite edges of the frame with the whole world between them. An **insert** is a close detail cut into a wider master, a photo on a mantelpiece, a hand on a doorknob, a way to give the audience information the characters don't have.

## Camera angle: where the camera sits relative to the subject

Angle is where the camera stops being a recording device and starts being a point of view. It carries feeling, and it creates depth: a three-quarter view separates foreground from background in a way a flat frontal shot can't.

**High angle**, camera above and tilted down, diminishes the subject: the character gets smaller, the world gets bigger, and they're being looked down on by the camera, and therefore by the audience. It's also a practical staging tool, since a high angle shows the floor, and therefore where everyone in the scene is standing.

**Low angle**, camera below and tilted up, suggests dominance and scale. It works hardest when the character is also positioned higher in the frame, since the two effects stack. Which means you can also make them contradict: shoot a physically small character from a low angle and you've said something specific about how another character sees them.

**Eye level** is neutral: the camera meets the character's eyes, and the audience meets them as an equal. Neutral isn't the same as boring, and it isn't the same as no decision either. In a scene where everything else is tilted and canted, eye level ends up being the loudest thing in the room.

**Bird's eye**, directly overhead, is more or less the god view: the audience sees the whole board and the character on it can't. Useful for geography, and for making a person look like a piece on a table. It also destroys faces, so use it when position matters more than performance.

**Canted / Dutch angle** tilts the frame off its axis so the horizon runs diagonally: unstable, unhinged, wrong. It works because the audience's sense of level isn't negotiable, you're lying to their inner ear. It's also the most conspicuous angle available, which makes it the easiest one to overuse. One canted shot in a sequence is a statement. Six is a style, and usually not a good one.

**Tilt** is a fixed camera pivoting on its own vertical axis, up or down. It does double duty as both an angle and a camera move. As a move, tilting up a building reveals its height by withholding it one story at a time. A reveal is a tilt with a reason.

**Three-quarter (45°)**, between frontal and profile, is probably the strongest general-purpose angle: it separates foreground, subject, and background into distinct planes. If you don't know what angle to use, use this one. It's something like the rule of thirds of camera angles, a default that's almost never wrong and almost never remarkable.

**Frontal**, camera straight on, is flat, since it loses the depth a three-quarter view gives you, but in close-up it turns intimate, and it's the angle for what's called a "subjective" shot: a character addressing the camera directly.

**Profile**, directly from the side at eye level, is observational and formal. It refuses you the eyes, and therefore most of the performance, which is sometimes exactly what you want: a character withholding something, or a moment the audience watches rather than shares in. Two profiles facing each other is about the most neutral possible staging of a conflict there is. Nobody has the advantage, including the audience.

**Over the shoulder (OTS)** places the camera behind one character, over their shoulder, focused on the other. It's the workhorse of dialogue "coverage," and unlike a two-shot, it puts you in a position: you're standing with this person, looking at that one. It's also something of a continuity trap. The moment you shoot an OTS, you've committed to a side of the line, and that comes back in the continuity lesson.

## Camera movement: a character in its own right

Before adding a move, ask what it adds, and what it costs. A moving camera is expensive in every medium: a crane, a dolly, a track and a crew in live action, a background rebuilt wide enough to move across in animation, artwork drawn larger than the frame in an animatic. Every move you board is a move somebody has to build.

Notating movement on a flat page needs two different arrows, and they aren't interchangeable. A **subject arrow** sits on the thing that's moving and follows its path. A **camera arrow** sits outside or across the panel and says the frame itself is moving. Conflate the two once and an animator builds the wrong shot: the character walks left instead of the camera panning left, and nobody catches it until it's already animated. Label them (CAM and SUBJ) any time there's doubt.

**Crane**, a camera on a crane or jib moving vertically through space, covers distance and produces angles otherwise unreachable, and can read as soothing, exhilarating, or menacing depending on context. In 3D, a crane is essentially free: a virtual camera has no weight, no crew, no risk, which is part of why animated films and games use moves that would be impossible or lethal in live action. Just because the camera can go anywhere doesn't mean it should.

**Pan**, a fixed camera pivoting horizontally, turns but doesn't travel, and directs attention from one part of a location to another. It's about the cheapest way to connect two things without cutting. A fast pan is called a **swish pan**, fast enough to blur, used as a violent transition. For an animatic, a pan means drawing wider than the frame, so plan the move before drawing the panel, or the artwork won't be there.

**Dolly / truck** moves the camera itself toward or away from the subject. This is probably the distinction students get wrong most often: a dolly changes your relationship to the space, foreground and background shift against each other, and the world gets more three-dimensional. A zoom does none of that.

**Tracking** is a dolly move that runs alongside the subject instead of toward or away from it, the "walk-and-talk" shot, and it makes the audience feel like they're accompanying the characters rather than observing them. It's also probably the most demanding move to board, since the background changes continuously. Effectively, you're drawing one very wide panel and choosing which slice of it the frame sits on at any given moment.

**Zoom** keeps the camera exactly where it is. The lens changes focal length. Zooming in gets "tighter," zooming out gets "looser." A zoom is really a crop, not a move: it magnifies what was already there and flattens the space in the process. Audiences read it as an effect rather than travel, which is probably why zoom-heavy footage dates so visibly.

**Steadicam**, a camera in a body-mounted harness, lets an operator walk, run, and climb stairs while the frame stays smooth, giving film the ability to follow a character almost anywhere with the fluidity of a dolly and the freedom of handheld. It's a real boarding problem, though, because it has no cuts to hide behind. Board an unbroken two-minute take and every composition in it has to work on its own, and every transition between them has to be motivated by the character's own movement rather than a cut doing the work for you.

**Zolly (dolly zoom)** dollies in one direction while zooming the other, so the subject stays the same size in frame while the background expands or collapses behind them. Hitchcock more or less invented it for *Vertigo*. The famous modern example is Brody on the beach in *Jaws*, the moment he realizes what he's looking at. It's the only camera move with no equivalent in human vision, which is exactly why it feels like the floor dropping out, and why it only works once per film.

## Camera perspective: whose eyes the audience is borrowing

**Objective** places the camera in a neutral, unbiased position: the camera is nobody in particular, and the audience observes from the sidelines. This is the default in most film, and like every default in this course, it's only a good choice once you know you made it.

**Point of view (POV)** sits the camera near a specific character's viewpoint, roughly where their eyes would be, seeing roughly what they'd see. It increases involvement, since the audience is borrowing somebody's position, and it commits you: once a POV is established, cutting away from it means something. POV isn't the same as "subjective." POV is near the eyes. Subjective is in them.

**Subjective** trades places with the character entirely: the audience sees through their eyes. It's the most personal and demanding option, since it collapses the distance completely, which is probably why it's used sparingly in film and almost permanently in first-person games. Everything covered today about choosing an angle assumes you own the camera. Deck 10 covers what happens when a player owns it instead.

## In conclusion

Shot, scene, sequence: three nested units. Framing is the distance from camera to subject. Angle is where the camera sits relative to the subject, and it carries the feeling. Movement adds dynamism, and it costs somebody real work. Perspective is whose eyes the audience is borrowing.

Handout H2 puts every size, angle, movement, and perspective from this lesson on one page, and it's open-book for the midterm, so there's no reason to lose those marks. But be honest about what it does and doesn't do. That page will tell you what a shot is called. It will not tell you which shot to use. That decision is the rest of the course.
