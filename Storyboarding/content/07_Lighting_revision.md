## Nothing is accidental

Every element in a frame has a purpose, and light is one of the most important of them. You already do this instinctively with composition; lighting is the same argument with a different tool.

Lighting started as a technical necessity: film needed enough light to expose properly. Film noir, in the 1930s and 40s, turned it into a storytelling language, and most of the vocabulary in this lesson is eighty or ninety years old and still completely current. That's not nostalgia. Those problems got solved early, and the solutions held.

Lighting design works the same way whether the lights are physical (on a film set) or virtual (in 3D and animation software, mimicking real-world behavior). Either way, decide the mood before touching a light, or you're just pushing sliders.

## Three-point lighting

Key, fill, and back became the standard setup in Hollywood in the 1930s and 40s, and it's still the default starting point on most productions.

**Key light** is the main source that defines the subject, usually placed around 45 degrees off to one side of the camera. It illuminates the subject and creates the shadows that bring out texture and form. (The sun is a key light, though direct sun produces harsh shadows at certain times of day.)

**Fill light** fills in or softens the shadows the key created. It's an indirect, softer source, and it lowers the contrast between light and dark. On a strongly key-lit face, the shadow side isn't pure black, because a fill is putting a little light back into it.

**Backlight** sits behind the subject and creates a rim of light that separates them from the background, giving form to hair and clothing that would otherwise fade into it. This is the same separation problem value contrast solves in composition: different tool, same job, the figure has to read against the ground.

## High-key and low-key

A subject's look is mostly defined by its key light: outside, that's the sun or moon; inside, a lamp or a window. High-key and low-key are the two basic families, and they're worth being able to name on sight.

**High-key lighting** is overall brightness with minimal shadow. It simulates daytime, and it's standard in comedy, upbeat adventure, sitcoms, news, and corporate video. It can also do something less obvious: too much light, in the right context, reads as transcendent rather than just bright. A character who appears out of nowhere in real trouble, blasted with high-key, low-contrast light, reads as something close to divine without anyone saying a word about it.

**Low-key lighting** produces an uneven distribution of light: an abundance of shadow, concentrated pools of light, and large areas of the frame left genuinely dark. This is the film-noir signature.

## Contrast

Overall contrast is the difference between the lightest and darkest parts of a frame, and it directs attention, the same job value contrast does inside a single panel. If everything is equally lit, nothing is emphasized.

**High contrast** emphasizes the extreme difference between light and dark, setting one part of the frame hard against another. Thrillers, horror, suspense. **Low contrast** has no pure whites or blacks, just a range of grays, and the viewer takes in the whole composition rather than its individual parts. Low contrast gets called boring because it can read as flat, the same trap as an evenly rendered storyboard panel where nothing is emphasized. It's only a problem when it's an accident.

## Hard and soft light

Go back to the sun as key light to understand quality of light. A sunny day creates harsh edges and hard shadows because the light is directional. An overcast day creates soft, diffuse shadows because light arrives from every direction at once. Everything else about hard and soft light follows from that.

**Hard light** emphasizes the dramatic, illuminating everything, texture and imperfections included, with clearly defined, sharp-edged shadows. Almost no fill means the eyes go dark; that's a deliberate choice and it's most of why a hard-lit character reads the way they do. **Soft light** softens everything it touches, and it's why actresses in the 1940s were routinely shot in soft, diffused light: it evens out skin tone and hides imperfections. The choice between hard and soft is partly a choice about how honest an image is willing to be about the face in it.

**This is the slide where cinematography becomes storyboarding.** The quality of light decided on determines how a shadow gets drawn: hard light means sharp-edged shadow shapes, soft light means soft gradients. Drawing the hard edge takes confidence; drawing the soft one takes restraint. What matters is that one was picked on purpose.

## Direction changes the mood

Light can come from the front, side, back, above, or below. Same light, five completely different results, because direction is what casts the shadows that reveal texture and detail.

**Frontal lighting** creates a flat image with barely any separation between foreground and background, minimal shadow and texture. On its own it's a boring composition, but it's genuinely useful when color relationships need to read clearly without shadows interfering. Comedy uses this constantly: bright, flat, nothing hidden, exactly right for the genre.

**Side lighting** creates strong contrast, one side lit, one side in shadow, giving a three-dimensional effect and bringing out detail and texture. It's often used to convey internal conflict, a character staged literally half in light and half in dark.

**Backlighting** places the source behind the subject, separating background from subject and creating depth, the back light's edge-definition job from three-point setup, promoted to the dominant light. Push it far enough and the subject goes to pure silhouette, which connects straight back to the silhouette standard from composition.

**Underlighting**, from below, produces unnatural-looking shadows: it lights the bottom half of a figure and shades the top, which reads as sinister. Horror and thrillers use it constantly, and once you've noticed it, you can't unsee it.

## Realistic vs. stylized

Most productions default to **realistic lighting**: light that could plausibly exist in the space. Candles and firelight sell a real setting; games lean on realistic lighting to make characters feel grounded in their environment.

**Dramatic / stylized lighting** makes the image more dramatic on purpose, with roots in early cinema's German Expressionism. Games use it to steer a player's focus toward what matters. The choice between realistic and dramatic is itself a storytelling decision. Realism is not the required default.

## What color does in a story

Color establishes time, place, and mood, and it reads differently from person to person, partly taste, partly culture. Keep that caveat in view for everything below.

The real principle isn't a color wheel, it's that **contrast is a tool of emphasis**, full stop. Value contrast, color contrast, sharpness, scale, motion: all the same job. Color is just one of many ways to bring attention to something, or take attention away from it. The question in front of a panel is never "what colors go together." It's "what am I emphasizing, and what's the cheapest way to emphasize it." Sometimes that's color. Often it isn't.

The obvious move to make a character stand out is to make them brighter, more saturated, higher contrast. That works, sometimes. Often the better move is the opposite: leave the character alone and lower the contrast of everything else. Keep a background analogous (colors sitting next to each other on the wheel, close in value), then put the character in something that contrasts against it. The character pops without anything being done to the character at all. Games do this constantly, because they have to guarantee a player can find their character and their objective. You can subtract attention as easily as you can add it, and subtracting is usually cheaper and looks better.

**The wheel, briefly**: primary, secondary, and tertiary colors on the standard 12-color wheel. You don't need to memorize scheme names (triadic, split-complementary, tetradic). What matters: colors opposite each other on the wheel create contrast, tension, energy; colors adjacent to each other create harmony and calm. Those two facts do almost all the work.

**Warm colors** (red, orange, yellow) evoke fire, sunsets, heat: energy, passion, aggression, urgency. **Cool colors** (blue, green, purple) evoke night, water, distance: calm, reserved, isolated, heavy. Yes, warm-equals-energetic and cool-equals-sad is a cliché. It's also load-bearing when used well, and the interesting question isn't whether it's true, it's what you can do with it once you know the audience already reads it that way.

**Color meaning is cultural, not universal.** Red reads as danger and passion in a lot of Western media, and as luck, prosperity, and celebration elsewhere. White reads as purity and weddings in some places, and as mourning or ceremony in others. Genre convention shifts it too: red in horror isn't red in a romance. Use these associations as a starting point, not a rule you're obligated to follow, and know who you're making the decision for.

## A language still being written

Lighting as storytelling took roughly a century of cinema to become a language audiences read fluently without noticing they're reading it. Games are visibly building their own version of that grammar right now, in public, while you're a student rather than long before you arrived. That's a rare position to learn from, and worth knowing while it's happening.
