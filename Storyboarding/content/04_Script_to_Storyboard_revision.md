## Script format, briefly

The language of film is visual, but the input is almost always words. If a screenplay says a little boy is pretending to be a pirate, every reader pictures something different: a ship, a bedroom, a stick, a bathtub. All valid, all different. Your job is to collapse that into one picture, so that two hundred people build the same film. That's what a board is for, and it's why "show, don't tell" is a production requirement, not an aesthetic preference.

Screenplay format uses **Courier, 12 point**, a fixed-width typeface, so a page holds a predictable number of characters. That's what makes the rule "one page equals roughly one minute of screen time" work: a producer can pick up a script, feel its thickness, and estimate a running time and a budget without reading a word. Every convention on the page (scene heading, action, character, dialogue, always in that order; margins specified to a sixteenth of an inch) exists so the stack of paper can be measured. Once you know that, the format stops being arbitrary rules and starts being a tool. If you're handed three pages for the final, that's about three minutes of film, and that scope is knowable before you draw anything.

A **slug line** looks like `INT. WAREHOUSE - NIGHT` and carries three facts. Interior or exterior tells you whether you're dealing with controlled light or the sky. The location tells you what the space is. The time tells you what the light is doing. Two of those three are your problem to solve: "night" is a lighting decision about where the light is motivated from, and "warehouse" is a staging decision about size, emptiness, sight lines, and where a character could hide. A slug line is three words long and contains most of a day's work.

Screenplay format isn't the only convention you'll meet. **Two-column / AV format** puts picture down the left side of the page and sound down the right, running in parallel, and it's standard in advertising, corporate video, instructional media, and a lot of game work.

## The gap that's your entire job

A script describes action and dialogue. A well-written one does not describe camera: it won't tell you this is a close-up, or that the camera cranes down, or that we cut to a reaction. That gap is the job. It's also why two board artists given the same page produce two different sequences, and why both can be right. If a script *does* specify the shot, you're reading a shooting script, meaning the director has already made that call.

How much a board needs to resolve depends entirely on who's reading it. Scorsese blocks scenes in simple diagrammatic drawings, stick figures and arrows, just enough to hold the geography. Fincher's boards for *Fight Club* are exhaustive and near-final, effectively a pre-edit of the film. Neither is more professional than the other. A board is a communication tool, and the right amount of finish is the least that communicates.

## The shooting script and the shot list

The **shooting script** is the final, green-lit version of a screenplay, the one that's actually going to be made. Once it locks, the director's job is to translate it into camera: analyzing each scene and breaking it into components. Notice the order. Story is decided, then money is committed, then camera is decided. If you're boarding, you're downstream of two enormous decisions made without you, and knowing that is most of what makes a board artist easy to work with.

What gets added at this stage: camera angles, camera framing, lighting notations, character blocking, and a scene number on every scene so that schedule, budget, and boards all refer to the same units. Nothing gets specified beyond what the production can afford. A shot you can't afford isn't a shot, it's a note. That sounds cynical and it's actually liberating: the constraint is what makes the problem solvable.

A **shot list** is the written version of the same information: for every scene, every shot that needs to exist, with framing, angle, and a line tying it back to the script. On most jobs somebody else writes it and you board from it, and that's real work. On the good jobs, you're in the room while it's being written, and getting into that room means knowing the vocabulary cold, having read the script properly, and having an opinion you can defend in one sentence.

## Choosing a shot: three filters

Every shot decision runs through three filters, and students usually only use one. **Practical**: whose viewpoint is this, what does the audience need to see, can we afford it. **Aesthetic**: does it look right, does it match the visual language of the piece. **Psychological**: how should the audience feel about what they're looking at. In *Ferris Bueller's Day Off*, the character talks directly to the camera, so the shot is frontal, a purely practical call. Most student boards stop at the practical question. The other two are where the work gets good.

An over-the-shoulder shot puts the audience in somebody's position: you're standing with this character, looking at that one. It's not literally their POV (you can see the back of their head), but it aligns you with them, which means alternating OTS shots in a conversation quietly asks the audience to switch sides every few seconds. Holding on one side instead is also a choice, and a stronger one.

## The establishing shot is orientation

Required any time a new location appears. It tells the audience where they are and what's happening before you ask them to follow anything. Skipping it is the most common structural mistake in student sequences: the audience spends the next four panels working out the geography instead of watching the story, and misses the beat that mattered.

You can *delay* an establishing shot, though, and delay is a real tool. Take one scene: a villain plants a bomb under a car in a parking lot. Open wide, and you've given the audience the geography and made the scene about anticipation. Open close on the bomb first, then pull out to discover where and who, and you've made it about revelation. Open on the villain, and you've made it about a person. Three legitimate choices, three different films, and nothing in the script changed. Hitchcock wrote about exactly this scenario: showing the audience the bomb early buys fifteen minutes of suspense, while surprising them with it buys fifteen seconds.

There's a debt attached to withholding, though. An audience will tolerate not knowing where they are for a while, and then they'll get lost, and once they're lost they stop following the story and start doing geometry. Withhold deliberately, and pay it off before anyone notices. If you haven't given an establishing shot within a few panels of a new location, you're not building suspense anymore, you're just being unclear.

Framing and angle change how an audience feels about what they're looking at, independent of content: this is Deck 03's angle taxonomy, applied rather than listed. The clearest example is the *Psycho* shower scene. Hitchcock gives you a run of subjective shots, Marion in the shower, comfortable, unobserved, the water, the showerhead from below, and the audience is inside her point of view and her sense of safety. Then the curtain is pulled back, and the POV is interrupted, taken away from her and from the audience at the same instant. The horror is structural, not gory: shot selection did what no amount of blood could.

## The floor plan

The **floor plan** (or overhead diagram) shows camera placement and character movement within a scene, drawn from directly above. It catches crossed lines and mismatched eyelines before a single panel is drawn, while they're still free to fix. It's probably the most underused tool in the discipline. Nobody makes you do it; everybody who does it has fewer problems. Nobody does it because it doesn't look like a drawing, so it doesn't feel like progress, but ten minutes of floor plan routinely saves an afternoon of redrawing.

A floor plan is a map and needs a key. Camera is conventionally a V with a line through it (the V is the field of view, the line is the axis); characters are circles, sometimes with a line for the direction they're facing. The convention matters less than the consistency: pick a set of marks, put a key on the page, use the same marks every time. Every camera position gets drawn and numbered according to its shot, using the same numbers as your panels and your shot list. Three documents, one numbering scheme: when somebody says "let's look at 40 again," everybody should find it in under five seconds, whichever document they're holding.

You'll meet the floor plan again: in the continuity rules next class, in perspective's camera heights, in the lighting plan, and in staging. Every appearance is the same tool solving the same class of problem before it becomes expensive to fix on paper.

## Notating movement, and expanding a panel

Illustrating movement on a flat, still page has two tools. **Directional arrows**: an arrow on the subject for subject movement, an arrow on or outside the frame for camera movement, two different marks, and confusing them is the classic error (Handout H1 has the full notation). And **expanding a panel into a succession**: draw a single panel wider or taller than the delivered frame, with two or three boxes marked inside it showing where the frame sits at the start, middle, and end of a move. This is also exactly what you'll do for the animatic. Want a pan? Draw wider than the frame. Want a push? Draw bigger. Plan the move before you draw the panel, or the artwork won't exist when you need it.

## The chain

The professional process, and almost exactly the grading structure of this course:

**Read the script.** All of it, before anything else, including the meeting. Many artists thumbnail in the margins as they read and arrive with questions rather than just attention. Turning up having read only the pages you were assigned, rather than the whole script, is visible within about ninety seconds.

**Meet the director.** Come prepared: read the script, look up anything unfamiliar, bring thumbnails (even bad ones), bring a short written list of questions. Meeting time is the most expensive time on a project and it isn't for catching up. Worth bringing to that meeting: what's the emotional goal of the story, what are the characters' personalities, what's the overall mood, what's the mood of each scene, what colors dominate, how does the action unfold, who's in charge of each scene. And the one that matters most: how do you want the audience to feel? If you can answer that in one sentence, the rest of the decisions get easier.

**Research.** You'll be asked to draw things you've never seen and can't picture accurately. That's not a gap in your training, it's the permanent condition of the job. The answer is a reference collection built continuously, before you need it: locations, vehicles, architecture, period clothing, weather, crowds, machinery. Anything you've had to look up twice belongs in it.

**Thumbnails.** Small, rough, fast: stick figures and shapes, meant to be tried and thrown away. They're also a note-taking tool during meetings, a way to leave with a record of what was actually decided. Rendered thumbnails are marked down in this course, because the moment a thumbnail is precious, you've stopped exploring and started defending, and exploring is the entire function of the stage.

**Rough draft.** The first full-size sketches, drawn to proportion, blocking action, dialogue, characters, and establishing continuity: the line, screen direction, eyelines. Then they go for approval. This is the stage where problems are still cheap: a staging error found in roughs costs an afternoon; the same error found in finals costs the week.

**Approval, and final storyboard.** Every stage in this chain is a graded submission in your projects, and skipping one caps the assignment. That's not administrative preference. The process is what's being assessed, because the process is what makes the work reliable, and a chain of dated stages that each follow from the last is very hard to fake and very easy to grade honestly.

## Working with a director

There will be problems on any project. That's arithmetic, not pessimism: a sequence has hundreds of decisions and some will be wrong. The only variable is when you find out, so fail faster: discover problems sooner so you can solve them sooner. There's no such thing as a dumb question, if you've done the homework and still don't understand the director's vision, ask. The expensive version of that question is the one asked in week four, after building on the misunderstanding.

Rough sketches don't have to be perfect. They do have to be understandable, and that floor isn't negotiable. The director has given you a problem to solve; solve it. Ask questions to make sure you understand the vision, then don't ask them for the solution, because the solution is the thing you were hired for. There's a version of "asking questions" that's really asking somebody else to make your decision, and everyone can tell the difference immediately.

Make sure you're on the same page as the director or client: it's their vision you're bringing to life, so understand it. That's not the same as having no authorship. Your judgment is all over the work, which shot, which angle, which moment to hold. But the target is somebody else's picture of the film, and the skill is getting there faster and better than they could describe. If you never want to serve somebody else's vision, that's a legitimate position, and it means you need to be the one with the vision, which is what the pitch session is about later in the term.

## Final board guidelines

Every board you hand in for the rest of the term follows this spec: each panel adheres to the production's aspect ratio; panels are numbered to the corresponding scene in the screenplay; each panel carries a shot number; camera angle and action are described beneath each panel; directional arrows appear wherever there's significant movement. Formats vary by studio; these are the constants. A board delivered with no shot numbers can't be discussed in a meeting, and a board nobody can talk about has failed at its only job.

Increment shot numbers by ten, not by one (010, 020, 030). When a director asks for a shot between 020 and 030, and they will, you write 025 instead of renumbering the entire sequence. Notation exists so that somebody who wasn't in the room can build your shot without asking you a question. If a mark on your board needs you standing next to it, it isn't notation yet.
