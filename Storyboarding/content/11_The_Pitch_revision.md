# Lesson: The Pitch

**CA 140 Storyboarding · Class notes**

> This is the part of the job that requires no drawing ability at all, and it will probably matter more to your career than anything else in this course. Nerves are not a deduction. Nobody loses a mark for being nervous. What costs marks is something else, covered below.

---

## The thing nobody tells you

You will spend more of your career explaining boards than drawing them. Meetings, reviews, pitches, interviews, sitting next to a director going through a sequence page by page: that's the job, most days. Almost nobody teaches it, which is exactly why being decent at it is an advantage.

## Where the format came from

Disney pinned the *Snow White* boards to a wall, then acted the entire film out for the crew, performed it, did the voices, walked the room through the picture before a frame existed. Pinning drawings to a wall so they could be performed isn't something that happened to storyboards later. It's the origin of the format. The artifact exists because somebody needed to pitch.

## What a pitch is, and isn't

A pitch is standing in front of boards and performing the sequence: reading the action, doing the voices, indicating the timing, so the room *experiences* the film before it exists. Not understands it, not learns about it. Experiences it. That bar is higher than describing what happens, which is what almost everyone does the first time.

It is **not** a defense; nobody in the room is hostile. It is **not** an apology; don't open with what isn't finished, everyone's is unfinished, and that's expected. And it is **not** reading captions aloud; if a pitch just narrates what's already visible on the boards, the boards have failed and the pitch won't save them.

## Where this happens

Story meetings, constantly. Client presentations. Pitching a project to somebody who might fund it. Festival pitch sessions. Grant applications, a written pitch with the same structure. Interviews and portfolio reviews, a pitch about you. Crowdfunding. For an independent artist, that list isn't a set of opportunities, it's the entire funding pipeline. Nobody funds a folder of drawings. They fund a person who can make them see the film.

## How to pitch a sequence

**Know it cold.** Perform, don't read. Looking at your own boards to remember what happens next is the tell that you're not ready: eyes belong on the room, and the boards are there for them, not for you. It's twelve to twenty panels of your own work; if you made them, you know them. Rehearse until that's trusted.

**Set it up in one sentence.** Where we are, who we're with, what just happened. *"It's the last night of a school year, and she's been waiting outside for an hour for someone who isn't coming."* That's it, then start. Everything between that sentence and the sequence is stalling: explaining process, describing how the idea came about, apologizing.

**Perform the action in present tense, active voice.** *"She turns. She sees it. She runs."* Not *"in this shot we see a character who is turning."* The second version puts the room outside the film, watching a document get described. The first puts them inside it. Same information, completely different experience, and the only difference is grammar.

**Do the voices.** Badly is fine; silently is not. Nobody is assessing acting range, but the room needs to hear dialogue timed against the cut, and reading it flat says nothing about rhythm. Everyone feels ridiculous doing this. The people who look comfortable have simply done it enough times that the feeling stopped mattering.

**Pitch the timing, not just the content.** Pause where the film pauses. Speed up where it speeds up. If a shot holds four seconds on somebody's face, actually stop talking and let four seconds pass. This is what separates a pitch from a description: performing the edit rather than summarizing it. The room should feel the shape of the sequence in the rhythm of the voice alone.

**Match the energy of the thing.** A chase pitched calmly is a chase that doesn't work; a quiet, sad scene pitched loudly is a scene nobody believes. The room's read on a sequence follows the presenter's read on it, not manipulation, just the same information delivered with the confidence the finished thing would have.

**Know the one thing.** Every sequence has one moment it exists for, one beat everything else sets up or follows from. Know which one, build toward it, slow down when it arrives, let it sit. Without that, the room decides what the sequence is about for themselves, usually picking something unintended, and then all their notes aim at the wrong thing.

**The physical part.** Stand beside the boards, not in front of them; it's surprisingly easy to block the thing being pitched. Point deliberately, not constantly; a hand that's always moving stops meaning anything. Finish, then stop talking. The silence that follows will feel enormous. It isn't a problem to solve. Let it sit.

**Length.** Three to four minutes for a sequence, which is close to the professional length too. Rehearse out loud, timed, at least three times. Reading it silently doesn't work, because an inner voice reads at roughly twice speaking speed, which is why people who rehearse silently reliably run over.

## Receiving notes

The room will have notes, always, including on excellent work, especially on excellent work, because good work is worth engaging with. Notes are not a verdict. Notes are the job.

**Don't defend.** The response to a note is "got it," or a clarifying question. Not an explanation of what was meant, not the reasoning behind the choice, not the constraint that was in play. This feels unnatural and it's non-negotiable, for one reason: if a choice has to be explained, the boards didn't say it, and that *is* the note. Explaining in the moment confirms exactly what the room was telling you. Responses come after all the notes are in; during, they get written down.

**Write everything down**, including and especially the notes that provoke disagreement. Practically, notes half-remembered three days later are worse than no notes. And the ones that sting are usually the ones worth revisiting: a note that lands immediately is easy, a note that annoys is usually pointing at something already suspected and not yet dealt with.

**Listen for the note behind the note.** People are good at noticing something is wrong and unreliable at diagnosing why. "Make the monster bigger" often means "I'm not scared here," and the fix might be a bigger monster, or it might be a longer hold before the reveal, a tighter shot on a reaction, or cutting a shot that gave the reveal away too early. The job is to diagnose, not comply. Solving the stated note when the real problem sits elsewhere wastes everyone's week, and the note comes back.

Ask about intent, not solutions. Good: "What's the feeling you want here?" Bad: "So should I make it a low angle?" The second question asks somebody else to do the job they hired you for, and it usually gets a yes, which means implementing somebody else's guess.

**Disagreeing well** is allowed; being precious is not. "Let me try both" is a professional answer that usually ends the argument, since one version will obviously read better side by side. "Can I show you why I went the other way?" is also fine, then let the work make the case. What doesn't work is defending in the moment, at volume, with certainty as the only evidence.

## Pitching yourself

Everything above applies to pitching yourself, which happens far more often than pitching a sequence: portfolio reviews, interviews, a reel (a pitch with no narrator), an artist statement (a pitch in writing). Same structure every time: set it up in one line, perform rather than describe, know the one thing, stop talking when finished. People who are good at interviews aren't more confident, they've simply noticed an interview is a pitch and prepared it like one.

Going independent means this is the whole machine: grants are pitches, crowdfunding is a pitch delivered once that runs for a month without you, festival pitch sessions are literally this with a timer, and finding collaborators, rarely mentioned, is a pitch too, since talented people choose the projects somebody made them see.

## The honest version

Entry-level 2D board roles have contracted. That's real. Generative tools produce panels quickly and studios are testing them widely, and some of that testing will stick. What's holding its value: judgment, taste, art direction, and the ability to sit in a room and defend a choice. Viable paths run through 2D/3D hybridity, previs, games, strong generalism, and fluency across more than one industry.

A machine can generate a panel. It cannot sit in a room, understand what a director is actually worried about, and propose the fix. That's the job. It always was. The drawing was only ever how it got delivered. Standing up and making a room see something that doesn't exist yet is the part of this work that nothing on the horizon touches.
