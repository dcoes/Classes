## Part 1: Origins

### Before there was a plan

The first split in cinema, documentation versus fiction, happened in about seven years. The Lumière brothers screened *Workers Leaving the Factory* and *A Train Pulling In* in 1895: film as record. Audiences were astonished by the recording itself; the Lumières thought of their camera as a scientific instrument.

Georges Méliès, a stage magician who owned a theater, looked at the same machine and saw a way to do a trick. *A Trip to the Moon* (1902) is a made-up story with built sets and effects. The moment film stopped recording and started inventing is the moment somebody had to plan a shot in advance, and that's the whole reason this job exists.

### Directors who drew first

Sergei Eisenstein sketched sequences before shooting *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), working out how action would move inside a frame and across a cut. Cecil B. DeMille hired illustrator Dan Sayre Groesbeck to visualize *The Ten Commandments* (1923) before committing a budget to it. Neither man had the word "storyboard." Both were doing a version of the thing. The practice existed before the word did, which means the job was invented because somebody needed it, not because somebody theorized it. That's true of almost everything in this course.

### McCay and Gertie

Winsor McCay, already famous for the newspaper strip *Little Nemo in Slumberland*, made *Gertie the Dinosaur* (1914): around 10,000 drawings on rice paper, by hand. It premiered as a vaudeville act on February 8, 1914, at the Palace in Chicago. McCay stood beside the screen in evening dress with a bullwhip, spoke to her, and she answered. At the end he appeared to walk into the frame and ride off on her.

The reason this matters for this course isn't the animation. Gertie was a stage act, and McCay understood that the performance was the point. In a few weeks you'll stand beside your own work and perform it for a room. He did it first, in 1914, with a dinosaur.

One correction worth carrying forward: the live-action prologue where McCay wagers he can make a dinosaur live was added later, for general release; the vaudeville premiere didn't have it. The friends in that prologue include cartoonist George McManus, who drew *Bringing Up Father*. It was never Ed McMahon, who was born in 1923, nine years after the film came out. If you see McMahon's name attached to this story, it's wrong.

### The counterexample: Fleischer

Max and Dave Fleischer (*Koko the Clown*, *Betty Boop*) were, in the 1920s, arguably the most successful animation house in America, and they ran with no script, no story department, and nothing written down. Dave walked the room each morning, collected gags from the animators, and the film accumulated. It worked, for five-minute shorts built out of gags where nothing has to pay off later because there is no later. The question that breaks this method: what happens when the film is eighty minutes long and something in reel one has to pay off in reel six?

### Webb Smith pins it to a wall

Disney split story development into its own department, under Ted Sears, in the early 1930s. That alone changed what was possible, but the sketches piled up faster than anyone could follow. Story man Webb Smith's solution was almost stupidly simple: pin every sketch to the wall, in order, beginning to end. Stand back and look at the whole thing at once. Move pieces around until it works. *Three Little Pigs* (1933) was the first cartoon built this way.

That's the actual invention: not a drawing technique, but a way of seeing a sequence all at once, laid out in space, so it can be rearranged before any money is spent on it. Everything since, including whatever software you use, is a refinement of that one idea. And it's still exactly how a pitch works: pinning your work up so a room can walk along it while you talk them through it.

### Live action takes notice

Once animation studios proved it, live action adopted the practice selectively, boarding only the expensive parts: effects, stunts, complex action. *Citizen Kane* (1941) boarded its complex sequences in advance. Hitchcock called his films storyboards come to life, and said he found actual shooting boring because the film was already finished on paper.

The *Psycho* shower scene is the one to know. Saul Bass drew 48 storyboard panels; the finished scene runs about 45 seconds across roughly 78 camera setups and 52 cuts. That's a lot of decisions for 45 seconds, all made on paper first. Bass later implied he had directed the scene, not just boarded it; Janet Leigh (who was in it) and assistant director Hilton Green both said Hitchcock directed every frame. Nobody disputes that Bass drew the boards. The dispute is about how much authorship drawing the boards gives you, which is a real question in this job.

### Why the industry actually adopted it

Boards did not spread through the industry because they're beautiful. A big film has multiple units shooting simultaneously in different places (*Bond*, *Star Wars*, *Raiders of the Lost Ark*), and somebody has to guarantee the footage will cut together. A board is the cheapest available guarantee: it's cheaper than fixing a mistake later, and it keeps two hundred people making the same movie instead of five different ones. That's still the argument for why this job exists.

## Part 2: The shape of the frame

### What an aspect ratio actually is

An aspect ratio is a proportion, not a size. The first number is the width, the second is the height, and the height is almost always normalized to 1: 1.85:1 means 1.85 units wide for every 1 unit tall. A 1.85:1 frame is the same shape on a phone and on an IMAX screen. You aren't drawing at the size of the film; you're drawing at the shape of it.

The ratio is the first decision on a board, before composition, before staging, before a single line goes down. Every compositional decision you'll make is a relationship to an edge: headroom is a relationship to the top edge, lead room is a relationship to the leading edge, a rule-of-thirds intersection is defined entirely by where the edges are. Change the shape and every one of those relationships moves. Draw twenty panels and then discover the ratio was wrong, and you haven't made a small error, you've composed twenty frames for a rectangle that doesn't exist.

### A short history of the rectangle

**1.33:1** wasn't a designed choice. It's what came out of the film stock Edison and Dickson settled on (four perforations per frame, four units wide by three tall, also written 4:3), and it was cinema's default from the 1890s until sound arrived.

**1.37:1**, the Academy ratio, is a two-step story. When sound arrived, the optical soundtrack needed space on the film strip, and it took that space from the image, narrowing the frame to a nearly-square 1.19:1 that looked wrong to everyone. In 1932 the Academy standardized a fix: shave the frame height, restore a sensible proportion, call it 1.37:1, close enough to 1.33 that no theater had to be rebuilt. The shape of every film for the next twenty years was set by where the industry decided to put the audio, a storytelling format changed by an engineering constraint. Watch for that pattern; it's happening again right now, with phones.

### The peak, and the collapse

Around 1929-30, American cinemas sold roughly 100 million tickets a week, in a country of about 123 million people. Not a hobby: closer to a habit, roughly one visit per person per week on average. Attendance held near 90 million a week through 1946. Then television arrived, and it adopted the identical 1.33:1 rectangle: not a different product competing with cinema, but the same shape, delivered to the sofa for free.

Hollywood couldn't compete on price or convenience, so the only axis left was the shape itself. Attendance collapsed accordingly: about 90 million a week in 1946, 60 million by 1950, 45 million by the mid-1950s, 20 million by 1970. By 1960 roughly 90% of American homes had a television.

### Bigger, in which direction

**Cinerama** (1952) was the maximalist answer: three synchronized cameras, three projectors, one deeply curved screen, about 2.60:1. The opening program wasn't a story but a demonstration, led by a roller coaster shot that made people scream and some of them sick. It died of economics: every theater needed three projectors, three synchronized operators, and a new screen, and you could see the seams where the panels met. The pattern worth remembering: the technically spectacular format loses to the cheap one that's nearly as good. It happens again, twice, with 3D.

**CinemaScope** (1953) solved the same problem sideways. An anamorphic lens optically squeezes a wide field of view onto standard 35mm film (everything looks tall and thin on the negative); a matching lens on the projector stretches it back out. Same film stock, same projector body, same theater, two pieces of glass. That's the difference between a format that dies in five years and one still in use seventy years later. *The Robe* (1953) was the first feature out; *Lady and the Tramp* was the first animated feature in the format, meaning Disney's board artists had to learn to compose for a shape more than twice as wide as tall after twenty years in 1.37.

CinemaScope's lenses had real problems (faces near the frame edge stretched, and a focus artifact cinematographers called "the CinemaScope mumps"), so **Panavision** built better anamorphics in the late 1950s and took the market. One precise point: the anamorphic standard was 2.35:1 until a 1970 change to the projection aperture made it 2.39:1. Films from before 1970 are genuinely 2.35; films after are 2.39. Almost everyone in the industry still says "two-three-five" for both, and either number is understood to mean the same thing.

### The two ratios you'll actually use

**1.85:1** is the workhorse: comedy, drama, most animated features, most of what you'll ever be asked to board. **2.39:1** is the wide, epic one (*Gladiator*, *The Lord of the Rings*, *Braveheart*), and it's not just decoration. It changes what's possible in a single frame: two people at opposite ends of a composition with landscape between them, no cut required, or a figure in the far corner with the emptiness carrying meaning. What it loses is height, which is why 2.39 is bad at tall things: staircases, towers, anyone standing up quickly.

### The mismatch, and what it costs

A cinema frame is much wider relative to its height than a 1.33:1 television. Show a 2.39:1 film on a 1.33:1 screen unaltered, and most of the picture has nowhere to go. There are exactly two options: throw away the parts that don't fit, or shrink the whole image and accept empty space above and below.

**Pan and scan** (20th Century Fox, early 1960s) is the first option: crop the wide frame down to 1.33:1, and pan within the original image to follow whatever a technician judges to be the subject. This can add camera movement to a shot that was locked off, and can turn a single composition holding two actors into what looks like a cut between them. The arithmetic makes the cost concrete: a 1.85:1 frame cropped to 1.33:1 loses about 28% of its width; a 2.35:1 frame loses about 43%, nearly half the image. Someone composed that frame on purpose, and someone else re-composed it later for a rectangle it was never made for.

**Letterboxing** is the opposite trade: keep the whole frame, shrink it, accept black bars. It was unpopular for years (audiences felt cheated by empty strips on a television they'd paid for), but directors pushed for it and eventually won, helped by widescreen sets arriving and making the bars smaller. The bars aren't a defect and they aren't a border. They're the film declining to be re-cropped.

### Why this history still matters

Every fight in this history is the same fight: a mismatch between the shape something was made in and the shape it gets watched in. That fight never ended, it moved. Today's landscape: **16:9 (1.78:1)** is the negotiated settlement for streaming and broadcast, close enough to 1.85 that a feature loses almost nothing. **2.39:1** still signals "feature." **9:16 vertical** is a genuinely new shape, native to phones and short-form, not a crop of anything; it inverts most of the shot vocabulary in this course, losing the horizontal (most two-shots, most establishing shots) and gaining a vertical stacking axis instead. **4:3** is now a deliberate choice again, used for period feel or claustrophobia. And some films run IMAX sequences that change ratio partway through.

Games face a version of this problem that never resolves: the same title runs on a 16:9 laptop, a 21:9 ultrawide, a 4:3 projector, and a phone, and the aspect ratio can change mid-session when a player plugs into a television. Games solve it structurally, with safe areas for anything that must be readable, interface anchored to the edges rather than laid out for one rectangle, and cameras that adjust field of view by ratio. Cinematics get the film treatment; gameplay does not. Deck 10 covers this properly.

There is no correct aspect ratio. There's only the shape a thing will actually be watched in, and either you composed for it or you didn't.

### The arithmetic

There are two directions, and only two.

**Ratio to frame size:** pick the height you want to draw at, multiply by the ratio, and that's your width. A 4-inch-tall panel at 1.85:1 is 7.4 inches wide; at 2.39:1 it's 9.56 inches wide. Pick height first, because height is what limits how many panels fit down a page. Sanity check: the width must always come out bigger than the height for anything wider than 1:1. If it doesn't, the numbers went in the wrong order.

**Frame size to ratio:** divide width by height. A 6-inch by 4-inch frame gives 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5, so 1.5:1. This is the direction you use when you're handed a template and want to verify what it actually is rather than trust its label, which on a real production is sometimes wrong.

Four mistakes account for almost every problem on a ratio-dependent assignment: eyeballing a panel instead of calculating it (a rectangle that "looks about right" is often 5-15% off, enough to change every edge relationship on the page); panels that don't match each other down a page (duplicate the first box, don't redraw it by hand); a frame that's the wrong ratio for the sequence it's meant to hold; and working so small you can't draw comfortably inside the box. All four are avoidable with about a minute of arithmetic.

The shape of the frame is the first decision, and it's the one every other decision sits inside.
