# Lesson: Staging and Acting

**CA 140 Storyboarding · Class 9 notes**

> Handout H6 carries the acting checklist for this material. This page is the reasoning behind it. This is the one lesson in the course that isn't about where the camera goes. For eight classes, the subject was the outside of the shot: where to place a camera, how to hold a line, how to compose a frame. This one is about what's happening inside it: what the character is doing, what they want, and how to draw a person who is clearly in the middle of wanting something.

---

## The notes you'll get, translated

"It's stiff." "I don't believe them." "I can't tell what they want." These sound like drawing notes. They almost never are. The instinct when a pose is called stiff is to redraw it: cleaner lines, better anatomy, more time. That fixes it approximately never, because the problem usually isn't execution. No decision was made about what the character was doing, so there was nothing for the drawing to express. All three notes are acting notes.

## The core question

**What does this character want, right now, in this shot?** Not what they want in the film. What they want in this panel, in this second. Those are different questions, and the second one is the one that gets drawn. If the question can't be answered for a panel, the panel is decoration: a picture of a person in a place, maybe a nice picture, but not a shot. Write the verb in the margin if it helps.

Two identical panels, same character, same framing, same amount of skill in the hand that made them, can read as completely different things: one as waiting, one as dreading. Nothing about that difference is rendering. It's weight, it's where the head is, it's what the hands are doing. It's a decision.

## Acting in animation vs. acting in boards

An animator acts across time: they own the arc, the overlap, the settle, the way a hand gets to where it's going. That's performance as motion. A board artist acts in single held moments: the job is to look at a beat that lasts three seconds and pick the one frame that contains the performance, then draw that. That's harder than it sounds and it's a genuine specialty of its own. An animator handed a great key pose can make it live. Nobody can rescue a key pose that was the wrong moment.

## Building the pose

**Line of action.** One curve, drawn through the whole figure, before anything else, before the head, before the hands, before knowing what the character is wearing. A straight spine reads as standing there; a C-curve reads as doing something. This is the first mark on a panel, and every other mark serves it. If a pose isn't working, the fix usually isn't the arms, it's whether there was ever a line of action to begin with.

**Straight against curve.** Every limb, every torso, needs one side doing something the other side doesn't: if the outside of an arm is a long straight, the inside is a curve. A figure made only of curves is mush, nothing holds; made only of straights, it's a mannequin. The opposition between the two is what makes the eye read a body under its own power.

**The silhouette test, again.** Fill the pose in solid black and ask if a stranger can name the action. This is the third time this test has come back this term (week one, composition, and now here), because it's the single most useful thing this course teaches, and the only standard completely independent of drawing skill. A beautifully rendered figure that reads as a blob when filled in has failed. A stick figure that reads instantly as reaching for something on a high shelf has succeeded.

**Negative space is part of the pose.** The gaps between an arm and the torso, between the legs, between head and shoulder, are shapes too, and the eye reads them as fast as it reads the figure. An arm held in front of the body merges into the torso and disappears into a solid silhouette; rotate the figure a few degrees, lift the elbow, turn the shoulder, whatever opens a gap between limb and mass. This is the cheapest fix available: no skill required, and it saves more panels than anything else in this lesson.

**Weight.** Where's the center of gravity, and what's holding it up? Balanced reads neutral, someone waiting. Weight shifted onto one leg reads relaxed, casual, thinking. Off balance, mid-fall or mid-lunge, reads as committed to something, a body that has moved past its own center of gravity has already decided something, which is exactly the state a board wants to catch. Characters without weight look pasted onto the background rather than standing on it, and the fix is usually one line: where the feet actually contact the ground, and whether the mass above is over that contact point.

**Asymmetry.** If a line in a figure is parallel to another line in the same figure, one of them is probably wrong. Shoulders tilt one way, hips the other; the head sits off the spine's axis; weight sits on one leg, not shared. Symmetrical poses read as dead, formal, or robotic, which is sometimes exactly right (a soldier at attention, a judge), but most of the time it should be broken. This is the same principle as one dominant element from composition: the eye needs somewhere to go, and perfect balance gives it nowhere.

**Gesture over anatomy.** A carefully constructed pose with correct proportions, checked anatomy, and twenty minutes of work reads as "a person exists." A loose, gestural pose with approximate proportions, an unmistakable line of action, and four minutes of work reads as "a person wants something." Given a choice between anatomically correct and unreadable, or anatomically wrong and instantly legible, the second one ships, every time. That's not an argument against learning anatomy, which genuinely makes you a better artist. It's an argument that intent is mandatory and anatomy is a nice-to-have.

## Faces

**Faces last, not first.** The instinctive first mark on a panel is usually a head, and then a body gets built underneath it, so the pose ends up as whatever the head allows, a compromise made before anyone decided what the character was doing. Reverse the order: line of action, then mass, then weight, then last the head. By the time the face gets drawn, the body has already said what expression belongs on it. The body carries most of the performance anyway, especially at board scale, where a face is often four marks wide.

**Expression economy.** Three marks, brow, eye line, mouth, is a complete, readable expression at the size boards actually work at. More than that is rendering, and rendering isn't on the rubric. The brow does most of the work: the same eyes and mouth, with only the angle of the brow changing, can read as angry, worried, surprised, or skeptical. The mouth gets all the attention and does much less than it seems to.

**When the face doesn't matter.** In long shots, silhouettes, or a character shown from behind, withholding the face can be the performance itself. A character turned away at the moment they get bad news, a figure in silhouette in a doorway: the audience fills in an expression more specific to them than anything drawn could be. The failure is drawing a face out of obligation, just because there's a head in the frame. Ask whether the shot is better without one. Surprisingly often it is.

## Staging two or more people

**With two characters, the relationship is the composition.** Decide the relationship in the moment before drawing either character; the staging follows automatically. Facing each other, level, reads as equals, engaged, a negotiation between peers. One turned away reads as refusal, shame, or dismissal, instantly, without a word said. One higher reads as power. Distance is a dial: the physical gap between two people is one of the loudest signals available.

**Height and power** stacks with camera angle, since there are now two independent controls for it: where characters are in the space, and where the camera is. A low angle on a character who's already physically higher is redundant, saying the same thing twice. The interesting case is the contradiction: a high angle on the powerful character, a low angle on the one who's losing, a shot saying two things at once that audiences feel even without naming it.

**Distance and intimacy (proxemics)** breaks into four rough, cross-culturally reliable zones, read instinctively without anyone knowing the term. Intimate, out to about 18 inches: lovers, or threats, almost nothing in between. Personal, out to about 4 feet: friends, real conversation. Social, 4 to 12 feet: colleagues, transactions, strangers being polite. Public, beyond that: performance, address, isolation. Two characters at intimate distance having a work conversation reads as uncomfortable; two characters at social distance arguing about their marriage reads as cold. The zone doesn't need naming while boarding, but choosing one has to be a decision.

**Open and closed** body language, arms, shoulders, orientation toward or away, is a blunt instrument worth using anyway, because it's legible at thumbnail size. A subtle facial expression is invisible at the scale a thumbnail sprint actually works at (under an inch); open versus closed still survives.

**Three or more characters** is a different problem than two. Triangular staging is the default: characters at different depths and heights so nobody is hidden and nobody is decorative, since straight lines of people read as a chorus. Groupings read as sides: if two of three characters are near each other and the third isn't, the audience has already decided there's an alliance, whether it was meant or not. Proximity is the same principle from composition, things near each other belong together, applied to people. Who is grouped with whom is the story.

**Stage the listener.** Watch any well-directed dialogue scene and the screen time spent on the person not speaking is usually more than half, and often the most memorable shot in the scene is somebody listening. Beginners board the talker: line of dialogue, cut to whoever said it, repeat, which produces flat scenes. The information in a scene is usually the effect a line has, not the line itself, which means the reaction is the shot. When unsure where to cut, try cutting to the person who isn't talking.

**Blocking across a sequence.** Characters move during a scene, and where they move is dramatic information. Someone who starts across the room and ends up beside another character has performed the entire scene with their feet; the dialogue could be cut and the story would still be legible. A scene where two people hold fixed positions for twelve panels and only their faces change is radio with pictures. Check a floor plan for where people start and end. If it's the same place, that needs to be a decision, not an oversight.

## Using reference

Reference is not cheating, and it never was. Disney shot live-action test footage for its features: actors performed the scenes, the footage was projected, animators drew from it. That was the pipeline at the studio that invented the pipeline. Every professional uses reference; the ones who appear not to have simply looked at so much of it that it's already in their head, which is the same thing on a delay.

**Act it out.** Stand up and perform the action; the body usually does something unpredicted, a stop, a half-turn, a hand that never made it up, rather than the version imagined beforehand. That gap between an imagined body and an actual body is where stiff drawings come from, and the best reference model available is free and always on hand.

**Shoot it.** Record the action on a phone, scrub through frame by frame, and find the extreme, the moment where the pose is at its most legible, usually a bit earlier than expected. That frame is the panel. This is a videomatic: shooting reference, finding the key moment, and drawing from it, a real production technique, done here with a phone and no crew.

**Mirror.** For faces and hands specifically. Hands are the hardest thing in figure drawing to invent from memory and one of the easiest things in the world to look at.

## The acting checklist

What does this character want in this panel? Is there a line of action? Does the silhouette read? Is the weight believable? Is anything symmetrical that shouldn't be? Did the face get drawn before the body? Run these before calling a pose finished. The last question catches the most: if the face went down first, the pose was built around a decision that hadn't been made yet, and starting over costs two minutes but usually fixes the panel.
