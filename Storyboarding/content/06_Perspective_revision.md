# Lesson: Drawing in Perspective

**CA 140 Storyboarding · Class 6 notes**

> This is not a draftsmanship test. Five of the six ways to build depth work no matter how well you draw. This lesson teaches those five first, on purpose.

---

## Depth is an illusion you construct on purpose

We see in three dimensions; film, video, and paper are two. Depth on a flat surface is always a trick, and tricks can be learned. Your eye reconstructs space from a flat image using a handful of cues: overlap, relative size, atmosphere, texture, and converging lines. Most of them are free. You don't have to draw well to use them, you have to know they exist and decide to use them.

Perspective also isn't purely a drawing topic. Linear perspective is a property of where the camera is and what lens is on it, not a property of the drawing itself. Move the camera and every convergence in the frame changes with it. That means the perspective questions in this lesson are the angle questions from Deck 03, asked in a different vocabulary: where is the camera, how high, how far, how wide is the lens.

## Five free depth cues

**Overlap.** Put one object in front of another. The nearer object is complete; the farther one is interrupted, and the brain reads depth instantly, with no construction and no accuracy required. It's probably the cheapest depth cue there is, and it survives bad drawing completely intact: a crude shape in front of another crude shape still reads as depth. If a panel reads flat, the first question isn't "is my perspective wrong," it's "is anything in front of anything."

**Size and scale.** Closer is bigger, farther is smaller, and relative size does two jobs at once. It gives distance: the same figure drawn smaller reads as farther away. And a known-size object next to an unknown-size one tells the audience how big the unknown thing is, which is why an establishing shot needs a person in it. Without a human-scaled reference, a mountain and a rock look identical.

**Atmospheric perspective.** Air isn't transparent. Dust and moisture scatter light, so distant objects lose contrast, get lighter, and pick up a cool cast. On a black-and-white board you can't use the color shift, but the contrast shift alone does the work.

**Value as depth.** This is the same line from the composition lesson, worth repeating verbatim: it's the cheapest depth cue you own, and it works even if your perspective is wrong. Foreground dark, midground mid, background light, and three planes separate with no construction whatsoever. Composition, perspective, and lighting each hand you the same three-value tool for a different job, which isn't a coincidence. It's the sign of a system, not a list.

**Texture gradient.** Any repeating surface, cobbles, tiles, grass, waves, a crowd, compresses with distance: coarse and separated up close, fine and merged far away. Draw the near units big and separated and the far ones small and merged, and the surface recedes. This is the cheapest way to make a floor or road read as going somewhere, and it means you can suggest an enormous crowd with a handful of drawn faces and a gray tone behind them.

**Put three of these together and you have most of the depth you need.** Foreground, midground, background: something near, something at the subject's distance, something far, overlapped and separated by value. Most student panels use one plane, which is why they feel flat regardless of how accurate the perspective is. If you use nothing else from this lesson, use three planes and overlap them.

## The sixth cue: constructed linear perspective

**Picture plane.** Think of it as a window between you and the world: what you draw is what you'd trace on that glass, and your eyeline points through it at the subject. It's a useful mental model because it makes the geometry concrete: you're not drawing objects, you're drawing where they intersect a plane.

**The horizon line is your camera height.** Not a metaphor, literally what it encodes. If the horizon crosses a standing figure at chest level, the camera is at chest level. Move the horizon line up and you're looking down; move it down and you're looking up. That's the high-angle and low-angle shots from Deck 03, executed with a single line. You can find the horizon in any still and tell how tall the camera operator was standing.

**Vanishing points** are where parallel lines appear to converge on the horizon. Objects parallel to each other share one; objects at a different angle get their own. A scene can have several vanishing points at once, and a drawing can technically be "in perspective" and still look wrong, usually because two things that should share a vanishing point don't.

**One-point perspective**: one vanishing point, verticals stay vertical, horizontals stay horizontal, everything receding runs to the single point. Good for corridors, roads, tunnels, anything you're looking straight down. It's also the most conspicuous construction; perfect one-point symmetry almost never happens by accident, which is why it reads as formal and unnerving.

**Two-point perspective**: two vanishing points on the horizon, verticals still vertical, two sets of receding lines running to their own points. This is looking at a corner rather than a face, and it's the default for most exteriors, since it reads as a real place rather than a diagram. Moving the two points closer together exaggerates the corner and tightens the space; farther apart flattens it out.

**Three-point perspective**: two points on the horizon plus a third above or below it, so verticals converge too. A third point high above means looking up at something tall; far below means a bird's-eye view down onto something. This is what to reach for when the angle itself is the point, and it's the construction most worth practicing, because it's the one that can't be faked convincingly.

**Circles in perspective are ellipses.** Never draw the ellipse freehand: draw the square that contains it, in perspective, then inscribe the ellipse touching the middle of each side. The farther an ellipse sits from the horizon line, the more open it gets; on the horizon, it collapses to a straight line. That single rule fixes most wheel drawings, and it's worth the twenty minutes it takes to learn, since vehicles, cups, and doorways are everywhere and a wrong ellipse is instantly visible.

**Foreshortening** applies perspective within a single form: easy on a pipe or a box, hard on an arm or a leg. The near end enlarges and the length compresses, usually more than feels right. There's a legitimate professional workaround: if a foreshortened pose is fighting you, change the camera angle so it isn't foreshortened anymore. That's a real decision, not a cop-out, though it's worth noticing whether you're choosing the angle or avoiding the drawing.

## What the camera and lens add

**Relative motion (parallax)** is the one depth cue that needs actual motion: when the camera moves, near objects sweep past faster than far ones, and distant mountains barely move at all. It's an animatic tool rather than a board tool: board a pan across a landscape with something close in the foreground, and the sweep does the rest. Plan it at the boarding stage, since it means drawing that foreground element on its own layer.

**Depth of field** is how much of the frame is in focus, a shallow depth isolates a subject against a soft background; a wide one keeps everything legible. On a board, you indicate it rather than render it: a few soft, low-contrast lines behind a crisply drawn subject reads as shallow focus immediately. **Deep focus** keeps every plane sharp at once, which changes the relationship with the audience, instead of the camera telling you where to look, the composition has to. Welles and Toland staged *Citizen Kane* in deep focus so foreground, middle, and background all carried story at once; it's the harder way to shoot, and it's why those frames are still studied. The controlling factor is **aperture**: a small aperture lets in less light but gives greater depth of field (more sharp); a wide aperture lets in more light but gives shallower depth of field (less sharp). You don't need the f-numbers. You need to know a cinematographer choosing a shallow-focus look is trading light for isolation, and if you board a shot that depends on a soft background, someone has to be able to light it that way.

**Lens choice is a storytelling decision, not a technical one.** A **normal lens** renders spatial relationships approximately as your eye does, which makes it invisible, and invisible is often exactly right; most of most films is shot on normal-ish lenses for the same reason most shots are eye level, the conspicuous choices get saved for moments that deserve them. A **wide angle** lens exaggerates the space between near and far, making a room feel bigger or a corridor feel longer, while distorting faces and edges, especially near the frame's edges; for boarding, a wide-angle shot needs strong convergence, or it won't read as wide. A **telephoto** lens does the opposite, compressing depth so things at different distances look stacked together and flattened, the classic case is a character running toward camera who never seems to get closer. For boarding, a telephoto shot has almost no convergence: flatten the perspective rather than sharpen it. In *Murder on the Orient Express*, the main story runs on normal lenses and the flashbacks switch to wide, stretching the space and distorting faces just enough to make the memories feel untrustworthy. Nobody in the audience notices the lens changed; everybody notices the flashbacks feel different.

## Naming the mechanism

Given three film stills, the goal is to name the specific cue at work: "overlap and value," not "good perspective." Naming the mechanism is the skill, because it's the only version you can then go use yourself. A harder, more useful question: which cue is carrying the shot, and what would the frame lose without it? That's the question that turns recognition into judgment.

## In conclusion

Six ways to build depth: overlap, size, atmosphere, value, texture, and linear perspective. Five of them cost nothing but the decision to use them. The horizon line is your camera height, move it and you've moved the camera. If perspective has been intimidating before, notice what happened in this lesson: five of six tools never required construction. The sixth is worth learning, and it's not the price of entry.
