# DECK 09: STAGING & ACTING FOR BOARDS
**Target: 30 slides · Session 12, at final-project launch**

## Why this deck exists
Your course teaches where to put the camera very thoroughly and never teaches what the character is *doing* inside the frame. Every critique note that sounds like "it's stiff" or "I can't tell what they want" is an acting note, and there's currently no vocabulary for it. This is also the deck where your Marvel Way material finally has a proper home instead of being crammed into Session 1.

Timed for Session 12 deliberately: students are about to board a story where characters have to *perform*, and this is the last moment the lecture can still change their work.

---

### PART 1 ACTING IS A STAGING PROBLEM (1–5)

**1 · Title**: Staging & Acting: What the Character Is Doing

**2 · The note you'll get**
"It's stiff." "I don't believe them." "I can't tell what they want."
*Say: these sound like drawing notes. They're almost never drawing notes.*

**3 · Two panels**
Same character, same shot size, same rendering quality. One reads as *waiting*; one reads as *dreading*.
*Say: identical drawing skill. The difference is a decision, and the decision is what I'm teaching today.*

**4 · The core question**
> What does this character want, right now, in this shot?
*Say: if you can't answer that for a panel, the panel is decoration. Write the answer in the margin if you have to.*

**5 · Acting in boards vs. acting in animation**
Animators act in motion, across time. You act in **single held moments**: the extreme, the pose that carries the beat.
*Say: your job is to pick the one frame that contains the performance. That's a harder problem than it sounds and it's a genuine specialty.*

---

### PART 2 THE BODY (6–13)

**6 · Line of action**
One curve through the whole figure, drawn before anything else.
*Demo live: same figure with a strong C-curve and with a straight spine.*
*Say: this is the first mark you make. Every other mark serves it.*

**7 · Straight against curve**
Rigid vs. flowing on opposite sides of the figure. Creates energy and readability.
*Say: a figure made only of curves is mush. Made only of straights, it's a mannequin.*

**8 · Silhouette, again**
Callback to Session 1 and to composition.
The test: fill it black. Can a stranger name the action?
*Say: I will keep bringing this back all semester because it is the single most useful thing I can teach you.*

**9 · Negative space in the pose**
Gaps between arm and torso, between legs. Closed shapes read; overlapping mush doesn't.
*Say: if the arm is in front of the body, we lose it. Move it off the silhouette.*

**10 · Weight**
Where is the center of gravity? What's carrying it?
Diagram: balanced stance · shifted weight · off-balance / mid-fall.
*Say: characters who don't have weight look like stickers. This is one line, the contact of the feet, and it changes everything.*

**11 · Asymmetry**
Shoulders and hips opposed. Head tilted off the spine axis. Nothing parallel.
*Say: symmetrical poses read as dead, formal, or robotic. Sometimes you want that. Usually you don't.*

**12 · Gesture over anatomy**
Two versions: a carefully-constructed anatomically-correct stiff figure vs. a loose gestural figure with clear intent.
*Say: the second one is better boarding and it took a fifth of the time. Anatomy is a nice-to-have. Intent is mandatory.*

**13 · Where your Marvel Way material goes**
Construction, foreshortening, dynamic poses: offered here as **enrichment**, linked on Canvas, for students who want to push further.
*Say: if you want your figures to be genuinely good, this is the path. It is not required to pass this class and it is not what I'm grading.*

---

### PART 3 THE FACE (14–17)

**14 · Faces last, not first**
*Say: students draw the face first and then discover the pose doesn't work. Reverse it. The body carries 80% of the performance.*

**15 · Expression economy**
Three marks: brow · eye line · mouth. That's a complete expression at board scale.
*Grid demo: six emotions in three marks each.*

**16 · The brow does the work**
Same eyes and mouth, four different brows, four different emotions.
*Say: if you learn one facial feature, learn the brow.*

**17 · When the face doesn't matter**
Long shots, silhouettes, back-of-head, obscured faces.
*Say: sometimes withholding the face IS the performance. Don't draw a face just because there's a head.*

---

### PART 4 STAGING RELATIONSHIPS (18–24)

**18 · Two characters: the relationship is the composition**
Diagrams: facing · one turned away · one higher · distance close vs. far.
*Say: before you draw either character, decide what the relationship is. The staging will follow.*

**19 · Height and power**
Who's higher in frame? Who's looking up?
*Say: this stacks with camera angle from Session 3. Low angle on a character who is also physically higher is redundant. Sometimes you want the contradiction instead.*

**20 · Distance and intimacy**
Proxemics: intimate · personal · social · public.
*Say: the physical gap between two characters is a dial you control, and audiences read it instantly without knowing why.*

**21 · Open vs. closed body language**
Arms, shoulders, orientation toward or away.
*Say: this is legible at thumbnail size, which is why it's more useful to you than facial subtlety.*

**22 · Three or more characters**
Triangular staging · groupings as "sides" · one character isolated from a group.
*Say: proximity from the composition lecture, applied to people. Who's grouped with whom is the story.*

**23 · Staging the listener**
The reaction is often the shot.
*Say: beginners board the person talking. The performance is usually on the other face.*

**24 · Blocking across a sequence**
Characters move, and their movement is dramatic.
*Example: a character who starts across the room and ends beside the other. That's the scene, staged.*
*Say: if your characters stand in the same places for twelve panels, ask why.*

---

### PART 5 GETTING IT RIGHT (25–30)

**25 · Reference is not cheating**
Callback to Disney's live-action test footage.
*Say: every professional uses reference. The ones who don't have simply memorized more reference.*

**26 · Act it out**
Get up. Do the action. Notice what your body actually does.
*Run this live for 60 seconds: have the room stand and perform one beat.*
*Say: you own the best reference model available and it's free.*

**27 · Shoot it**
Phone video. Scrub for the extreme. Draw from that frame.
*Say: this is exactly what a videomatic is, at one-person scale.*

**28 · Mirror**
For faces and hands specifically.

**29 · The acting checklist**
Before you call a pose done:
- What does this character want in this panel?
- Is there a line of action?
- Does the silhouette read?
- Is the weight believable?
- Is anything symmetrical that shouldn't be?
- Did I draw the face before the body? (If yes, redo it.)

**30 · Exercise: One Beat, Three Performances**
Same character, same shot size, same moment, *she realizes she's been lied to*, staged three ways: contained, explosive, collapsing.
*Say: three panels. Nothing about the camera changes. Post them before you leave.*

# DECK 10: BOARDING FOR GAMES & INTERACTIVE
**Target: 28 slides · Session 13**

## Answering your question first
You're right, and the deck should say so out loud in the first two minutes: **a cutscene is a film and you board it exactly the same way.** If this deck were only about cutscenes it wouldn't need to exist.

What *is* genuinely different, and what this deck is actually about:

1. **You don't control the camera.** In gameplay, the player does. That breaks nearly every assumption in the continuity lecture. You can't guarantee screen direction, eyelines, or even that the player is looking at the thing.
2. **You don't control the timing.** A player may take four seconds or four minutes. Every timing tool from the animatic deck assumes a fixed duration you no longer have.
3. **You don't control the order.** Branching, optional content, and player choice mean the sequence isn't a sequence.
4. **The frame has furniture in it.** HUD, subtitles, reticles, and objective markers occupy composition space permanently.
5. **The document has a different job.** A film board communicates to a crew executing one fixed vision. A game board frequently has to communicate an *interactive* intention to programmers and designers, which is closer to a design document, exactly as you suspected.

So the deck's structure is: cutscenes (same as film, fast), then the four things that break, then the hybrid techniques the industry has developed to cope. It's also genuinely valuable to your animation and film majors. It's the clearest possible demonstration that shot grammar is a *system*, not a film convention, because they get to watch it survive contact with a medium it wasn't designed for.

---

### PART 1 WHAT'S THE SAME (1–5)

**1 · Title**: Boarding for Games & Interactive

**2 · Let's get this out of the way**
> A cutscene is a film.
*Say: shot sizes, angles, the 180° rule, match on action, eyelines. All identical. If you board a cutscene, board it the way we've been doing all semester. Nothing in the first half of this course gets thrown out.*

**3 · The receipts**
Stills from *God of War*, *The Last of Us*, *Arcane*, *Uncharted*: all readable with the exact vocabulary from Session 3.
*Say: name the shots. Out loud. You already can.*

**4 · So why a whole session?**
Because games contain a second thing that isn't a film, and nobody teaches you how to board that.

**5 · The four breaks**
You don't control: **the camera · the timing · the order · the whole frame.**
*Say: every one of those was a guarantee that the last eleven sessions depended on.*

---

### PART 2 BREAK ONE: THE CAMERA (6–11)

**6 · Who's holding it?**
Cutscene: you are. Gameplay: the player is. Cinematic gameplay: you're negotiating.

**7 · The camera archetypes**
First person · third person over-shoulder · fixed/static (classic *Resident Evil*) · isometric · side-scroll · top-down.
*Say: each one is a different set of compositional constraints, and boards have to know which one they're in.*

**8 · Boarding a camera you don't own**
You board the *space* and the *possibilities*, not a single view.
*Say: this is the real shift. Your panel becomes "what could be seen from here," not "what is seen."*

**9 · The overhead diagram comes back** ← callback to Deck 04
Floor plans are far more important in games than in film, because the camera moves through the space rather than being placed in it.
*Say: remember the floor plan I said was underused? This is where it stops being optional.*

**10 · Directing attention without a camera**
Lighting · color · motion · audio cue · architecture and sightlines · negative space.
*Examples: the glowing ledges in* Mirror's Edge*; the way* Journey *always frames the mountain on the horizon.*
*Say: this is composition, done environmentally. Level designers are compositing shots you'll never see.*

**11 · Player agency vs. authored moment**
The spectrum: full control → soft-locked camera → forced look → cutscene.
*Say: knowing where on this line you are tells you how much you're allowed to compose.*

---

### PART 3 BREAK TWO: TIMING (12–15)

**12 · Duration is unknown**
The player might sprint through in eight seconds or wander for six minutes.
*Say: everything from the animatic session assumed a fixed length. Throw that assumption away here.*

**13 · Boarding a state, not a shot**
Panels describe conditions: *before pickup / after pickup / on failure / on retry.*
*Say: this is where boards start looking like documentation, which answers the question about design docs: yes, partly.*

**14 · Loops and repetition**
Players see the same content many times. What reads as dramatic once is tedious the fifth time.
*Say: this is a real design constraint and it has no equivalent in film.*

**15 · The cutscene handoff**
Gameplay → cutscene → gameplay. The two most-noticed cuts in the medium.
*Say: match the camera position and character pose across the transition or the seam screams. Best-in-class productions hide the cut entirely: the* God of War *2018 one-shot is the extreme example.*

---

### PART 4 BREAK THREE: ORDER (16–19)

**16 · Sequences that aren't sequences**
Branching · optional content · missable beats · non-linear exploration.

**17 · Boarding branches**
Flowchart + panels. One node per beat, arrows for player choice.
*Show a simple two-branch diagram.*
*Say: this is genuinely a different document. Some studios call it a beat map or a narrative flow.*

**18 · What must every player see?**
Critical path vs. optional. Board the critical path fully; board optional content as key frames.
*Say: this is a resourcing decision, and being able to make it is a job skill.*

**19 · Environmental storytelling**
The story told by a room with nobody in it.
*Examples:* Bioshock*'s Rapture,* Gone Home*, any* Souls *level.*
*Say: composition with no camera, no character, and no cut. And it's still staging.*

---

### PART 5 BREAK FOUR: THE FRAME (20–23)

**20 · The frame has furniture**
HUD, health, minimap, reticle, subtitles, objective markers, prompts.
*Overlay a HUD on a well-composed film still.*
*Say: your rule-of-thirds intersections may already be occupied. Compose around what's permanently there.*

**21 · Safe areas and variable displays**
Handheld, TV at distance, ultrawide, phone.
*Say: this is aspect ratio and safe-title from Session 2, with more variables and higher stakes.*

**22 · Readability at speed and at scale**
Silhouette again, but now for enemies, interactables, and hazards, at small size, in motion.
*Say: character silhouette design in games is a hiring criterion. It's the same test I gave you in week one.*

**23 · Accessibility as composition**
Colorblind-safe signaling, subtitle space, contrast requirements.
*Say: increasingly a legal and commercial requirement, not a nicety. Knowing this in an interview is a differentiator.*

---

### PART 6 THE JOB (24–28)

**24 · What game boards actually look like**
Cinematic boards (film-identical) · gameplay previs · flow diagrams · beat maps · level layout + camera sketches.
*Say: your question about design documents was right. In games, the boards often live inside a larger design document, and you'll be writing as well as drawing.*

**25 · Who reads them**
Not just a director: designers, engineers, level artists, animators, audio, UI.
*Say: this is the biggest practical difference. A film board goes to people executing a fixed plan. A game board goes to people who will build something interactive from it, and they need to know your *intent*, not just your image.*

**26 · Roles**
Cinematic artist · previs artist · narrative designer · level designer · UX.
*Say: several of these hire people with exactly the skill set this course builds, and they're less crowded than feature animation.*

**27 · Adjacent, and worth knowing**
VR/360 (no frame at all. You're staging a room and hoping), AR, immersive theater, interactive film.
*Say: in VR the entire discipline of framing evaporates and staging becomes everything. Which is a good argument that staging was always the real subject.*

**28 · Exercise**
Take one beat from your final project. Board it twice: as a linear cutscene, and as an interactive moment where the player controls the camera.
*Say: what did you have to give up? What did you have to add? That's the whole lecture in one exercise.*

# DECK 11: THE PITCH
**Target: 26 slides · Session 14 · Paired with a graded assignment**

## Why this is a deck AND an assignment
You're right that it should be assessed. Pitching is the part of the job that survives everything. It requires zero drawing ability, it's how boards actually move through a studio, and for an independent artist it's the difference between having work and having a career. It's also the one skill in this course where a student with brilliant instincts and shaky hands can be visibly, unambiguously excellent.

It's placed at Session 14 so students pitch their final projects before finishing them, which means the pitch is also a milestone that catches problems while there's still time.

---

### PART 1 WHAT A PITCH IS (1–6)

**1 · Title**: The Pitch

**2 · The thing nobody tells you**
> You will spend more of your career explaining boards than drawing them.
*Say: and nobody teaches it, which is why being decent at it is such an advantage.*

**3 · The precedent** ← callback to Deck 02
Disney pinned the *Snow White* boards to the wall and **acted the whole film out** for his crew.
*Say: the storyboard was invented as a pitching tool. Pinning drawings to a wall so you could perform them IS the origin of the format. You've been doing a version of this in every critique since week one.*

**4 · What a pitch is**
Standing in front of your boards and performing the sequence (reading action, doing voices, indicating timing) so the room experiences the film before it exists.

**5 · What it isn't**
Not a defense. Not an apology. Not reading captions aloud.
*Say: if your pitch consists of explaining what I'm looking at, the boards have failed and the pitch won't save them.*

**6 · Where it happens**
Story meetings · client presentations · pitching your own project · festivals and grant applications · interviews and portfolio reviews · Kickstarter and crowdfunding.
*Say: for an independent artist this list is your entire funding pipeline.*

---

### PART 2 HOW TO DO IT (7–15)

**7 · Know your sequence cold**
You should not be reading. You should be performing.
*Say: if you're looking at your own boards to remember what happens, you're not ready.*

**8 · Set it up in one sentence**
Where we are, who we're with, what just happened.
*Say: one sentence. Then start. Everything else is stalling.*

**9 · Perform the action**
Present tense, active voice. "She *turns*. She *sees* it. She *runs*."
*Say: not "in this shot we see a character who is turning." Perform it.*

**10 · Do the voices**
Badly is fine. Silently is not.
*Say: you will feel ridiculous. Everyone feels ridiculous. The room needs to hear the dialogue timed to the cut.*

**11 · Pitch the timing**
Pause where the film pauses. Speed up where it speeds up. Say "hold" on a hold.
*Say: this is the part that separates a pitch from a description. You are performing the edit.*

**12 · Energy**
Match the sequence. A chase pitched calmly is a chase that doesn't work.
*Say: the room's read on your sequence will be heavily influenced by your read on your sequence.*

**13 · Know your one thing**
Every sequence has one moment it exists for. Know which one, and land it.
*Say: if you don't know what the sequence is about, the room will decide for you, and they'll be wrong.*

**14 · The physical stuff**
Stand beside the boards, not in front · point deliberately, not constantly · finish and stop talking.
*Say: the silence after you finish is not your problem to fill. Let it sit.*

**15 · Length**
Two to four minutes for a sequence. Rehearse it out loud at least three times, timed.
*Say: three times out loud, with a stopwatch. Reading it in your head does not count and does not work.*

---

### PART 3 TAKING NOTES (16–21)
> Arguably the most professionally valuable section in the entire course.

**16 · The room will have notes**
Always. Including on excellent work.
*Say: notes are not a verdict. Notes are the job.*

**17 · Don't defend**
The response to a note is "got it," or a clarifying question. Not an explanation of what you meant.
*Say: this is hard and it is non-negotiable. If you have to explain what you meant, the boards didn't say it, and that IS the note.*

**18 · Write everything down**
Even notes you disagree with. Especially those.

**19 · The note behind the note**
People are excellent at identifying that something is wrong and unreliable at identifying why.
"Make the monster bigger" often means *I'm not scared here.*
*Say: your job is to diagnose, not to comply. Solving the stated note when the real problem is elsewhere wastes everyone's week.*

**20 · Ask about intent, not solutions**
Good: "What's the feeling you want coming out of this?"
Bad: "So should I make it a low angle?"
*Say, and this is from your own Deck 04: be the solution, not the problem. They hired you to solve it.*

**21 · Disagreeing well**
"Let me try both." "Can I show you why I went the other way?" Then let the work argue.
*Say: you're allowed to disagree. You're not allowed to be precious.*

---

### PART 4 THE CAREER CONTEXT (22–26)

**22 · Pitching yourself**
Portfolio reviews · interviews · your reel · your artist statement.
*Say: all the same skill. Set it up in one line, perform it, stop talking.*

**23 · The independent path**
Grants · crowdfunding · festival pitch sessions · finding collaborators.
*Say: nobody funds a folder of drawings. They fund a person who can make them see the film.*

**24 · The honest slide** ← the job-market conversation, placed deliberately
- Entry-level 2D board roles have contracted
- Generative tools produce panels quickly and are being tested widely
- What's holding value: judgment, taste, art direction, and the ability to sit in a room and defend a choice
- Viable paths: 2D/3D hybridity, previs, games, strong generalism, adjacent-industry fluency

*Say, plainly: I'm not going to tell you the field is fine. What I'll tell you is that the part of the job that's under pressure is "make a picture of the thing," and the part that isn't is everything today's session was about. That's not a consolation. It's the reason this course grades clarity and judgment instead of rendering, and it's why pitching is on the syllabus at all.*

**25 · What the room is for**
A machine can generate a panel. It cannot stand in a room, understand what a director is actually worried about, and propose the fix.
*Say: that's the job. It always was. The drawing was only ever how you delivered it.*

## ASSIGNMENT: The Pitch (50 pts)

**Format:** 3–4 minutes, live, in front of your boards (projected or pinned). Session 14 or 15, scheduled.
**What you pitch:** your final project sequence, in whatever state it's in. Incomplete is expected and fine: pitch what exists and describe what's coming.

**Deliverable:** the live pitch. Optionally, a recorded version submitted to Canvas for students who want a second attempt or who are genuinely unable to present live.

### Rubric

| Component | Pts | Full credit |
|---|---|---|
| **Setup** | 5 | Establishes where, who, and what just happened in one or two sentences, then moves |
| **Performance of the sequence** | 15 | Present tense, active, performed rather than described; voices attempted; the room can follow without reading captions |
| **Timing** | 10 | Pacing of the pitch reflects the pacing of the sequence; holds are held, fast cuts are fast |
| **The one thing** | 10 | Can identify what the sequence is *about* and lands that moment deliberately |
| **Taking notes** | 10 | Receives at least two notes without defending; asks at least one clarifying question about intent; writes them down |

**Not graded:** nervousness, polish of speech, accent, drawing quality of the boards being pitched.

*Worth stating in the brief, verbatim:* **Nerves are not a deduction. Defending your work instead of hearing the note is.*

### Running it in class
- Schedule it: 3 to 4 min pitch plus 2 min of notes is roughly 6 min per student. For 20 students that's two full sessions; for 12 it's one plus change.
- Notes come from you AND from three assigned peers, rotating. Peers giving notes get process credit for it.
- Establish the rule out loud before the first pitch: **the artist does not respond during notes except to ask a clarifying question.** They can respond after all notes are given.
- Follow-up milestone: within 24 hours, each student posts the notes they received and one line on what they'll change. That converts the pitch from a performance into a revision mechanism.
