# Module M4 — VR, 360 & Immersive
**14 slides · Recommended host: Session 16 as a closer, or Session 15 · Spec only, not built**

## Why this module exists

Because it is the course's closing argument, delivered by a medium rather than by an instructor.

Every session in CA 140 has been about a rectangle: what goes in it, where its edges are, and what happens when you cut from one to the next. This module removes the rectangle and asks what's left.

**What's left is staging.** Which means staging was the real subject the whole time, and framing was one very successful implementation of it. That's a claim the course could simply assert in Session 1. Landing it in Session 16, after students have spent four weeks composing rectangles, is worth far more.

It's also short, genuinely strange, and a good last taste of the discipline.

---

### PART 1 — THE FRAME IS GONE (1–5)

**1 · Title** — VR, 360 & Immersive

**2 · The viewer is holding the camera and you can't take it back**
Not "the player controls the camera," as in Deck 10. There is **no camera.** The viewer's head is the camera and it is attached to a person.

**3 · What dies immediately**
Framing. Composition as you've practised it. The cut, mostly. Shot size. Off-screen space. Everything with an edge in it.

**4 · What survives, and gets more important**
Blocking. Sightlines. Light. Sound. Movement. Scale. **Proxemics** — the physical distance between the viewer and a character, which in VR is felt rather than read.

**5 · So the whole discipline collapses to one question**
> Where do you *want* them to look, and what makes them?

That question has been in this course since the composition session. It just used to have a frame to help answer it.

### PART 2 — DIRECTING ATTENTION WITHOUT A FRAME (6–10)

**6 · Light**
The brightest thing wins, and in a dark 360 environment it wins overwhelmingly. Callback to Session 11 — this is the lighting study's naming test with the stakes raised.

**7 · Motion**
Movement in the periphery turns heads involuntarily. It is the strongest tool available and the easiest to overuse.

**8 · Sound, and this is the big one**
Spatial audio is the primary staging instrument in VR. A sound behind the viewer turns them around, reliably, without a single visual cue. **Film has nothing that works this well.**

**9 · Architecture and sightlines**
You build the space so that most comfortable head positions land on something worth seeing. Level designers have known this for twenty-five years; it is composition done with walls.

**10 · The problem nobody solves cleanly**
The viewer looks the wrong way and misses the beat. Options: repeat it, wait for them, force a fade, or design so there is no wrong way. **All four are compromises.** Say so — the honest version is more useful than pretending it's solved.

### PART 3 — TIME AND THE BODY (11–13)

**11 · The cut is mostly gone**
Cutting in VR is disorienting because the viewer's body didn't move. Long takes dominate. Transitions tend to be fades, or moving the world rather than moving the viewer.

**12 · Comfort is a design constraint, not a nicety**
Acceleration the body doesn't feel causes real nausea. Which means **camera moves — the thing Session 3 taught as expressive — become a medical constraint.** That inversion is worth sitting with.

**13 · Presence and scale**
A character at conversational distance in VR is *present* in a way no close-up achieves. Scale is felt. A tall character is genuinely intimidating. This is the medium's one clear superiority and it is entirely a staging effect.

### PART 4 — THE ARGUMENT (14)

**14 · So what was this course actually about**
Take the frame away and framing goes with it. What remains is: where people are, where they're looking, what's lit, what moves, what you hear, and how close it is.

**That's staging. It was always staging.** The rectangle was a hundred-year-old convenience for delivering it — an extremely good one, which is why it lasted — but the underlying skill was never about edges.

*Which is also why the durable half of this job is the half that isn't rendering: the systems underneath outlive every specific frame they've ever been delivered in.*
