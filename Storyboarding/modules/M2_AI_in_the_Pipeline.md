# Module M2 — AI in the Pipeline
**12 slides · 45-minute insert into Session 15 or 16 · Spec only, not built**

## Why this module exists, and why it is deliberately small

The course already has a **position** on generative tools — the AI policy in `syllabus-language.md`, and the honest job-market material in Decks 11 and 12. What it doesn't have is a **craft** session: where these tools actually sit in a working board department, what they measurably cannot do, and how to direct one.

**This must not become a full session, and it must not become a tool tutorial.**

Two reasons. First, `PROJECT.md` §9's no-engine-specific-content rule applies by analogy — naming tools is fine, teaching workflows is out of scope. Second, and more practically: **this is the fastest-dating content in the repository.** A session built around specific tools is wrong within eighteen months. A forty-five minute block built around what the tools can't do, and why, survives much longer, because the limits are structural rather than technical.

The centrepiece is a live demo that makes the course's own argument better than any slide can.

---

### PART 1 — WHERE THEY ACTUALLY SIT (1–4)

**1 · Title** — Generative Tools in the Board Pipeline

**2 · What they're genuinely used for right now**
Mood and lighting exploration · environment and prop ideation · reference gathering · upres and cleanup · style tests before a look is locked.
All of it **upstream of boards**, or downstream of them. Very little of it is the boards.

**3 · What that means for the job**
The exploration phase got cheaper. The decision phase didn't. Those were always different phases; they're now priced very differently.

**4 · Disclosure, and why it's the policy**
Reference has never been cheating and generative ideation isn't either — but it gets disclosed. The reason is not suspicion; it's that a process you can't describe is a process you can't repeat.

### PART 2 — THE DEMO (5–8)
> This is the module. If time collapses, cut everything else and run this.

**5 · Generate a panel from one of the class's own beats**
Live, in front of everyone, from a beat in someone's final. Take the prompt from the room.

**6 · Now run H3 on it**
The composition checklist the class has been using since Session 5. Out loud, item by item:
- Does it read at thumbnail size?
- Does the figure separate from the ground?
- **Is there ONE dominant element?**
- Correct headroom / lookroom / leadroom?
- Does it contrast with the panel before it?

**7 · What fails, and it fails reliably**
Figure/ground and single-dominant-element are the two that break most consistently, because both are decisions about *what the frame is for* rather than about what's in it. There is no panel before it, so the fifth question can't even be asked.

**8 · The point of that exercise**
The class just evaluated a generated image against the same checklist they've applied to their own panels for three weeks, and the failure was in the same place their own panels fail — except that a student can fix theirs, because a student knows what the shot is *for*.

### PART 3 — DIRECTING ONE (9–10)

**9 · The prompt is a brief, and briefs are a skill**
Naming shot size, angle, lens feel, key direction, value structure, and mood — the vocabulary from Sessions 3, 5, 6 and 11 — gets dramatically better results than adjectives do. **The people who direct these tools best are the people who could direct a human artist**, and for the same reason.

**10 · What it still cannot do**
Hold a sequence. Keep a character consistent across forty panels. Answer a note. Know what the scene is about. Every one of these is a continuity or intent problem, which is what this course has been about since Session 4.

### PART 4 — THE HONEST PART (11–12)

**11 · The straight version, again**
The "make a picture of a thing" half of this job is under real pressure. The "decide what the picture should be, and know why" half is not. That is not reassurance — it is the reason this course grades clarity and judgment instead of rendering, and the reason the pitch is on the syllabus.

**12 · What the room is for**
A machine can generate a panel. It cannot sit in a room, understand what a director is actually worried about, and propose the fix.

## Boundary check

No tool tutorials. No workflow instruction. Named tools are examples, not curriculum. The policy content already lives in `syllabus-language.md` and should not be re-delivered here — this module is craft, not position.
