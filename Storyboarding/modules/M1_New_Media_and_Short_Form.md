# Module M1: New Media & Short-Form
**16 slides · Recommended host: Session 15, replacing Deck 10 · Spec only, not built**

## Why this module exists

Every frame in this course so far has been wider than it is tall. That is not a fact about storytelling; it is a fact about where films were shown for a hundred years, and it stopped being universally true around 2016.

This is also the only delivery format on the module list that a student is likely to be **paid for on a first job**. Vertical video, six-second pre-roll, title cards, and social cutdowns are entry-level work that exists in volume right now.

And pedagogically it is the cleanest possible callback to Session 2. The founding argument of this course is that **frame shape determines staging.** Vertical is that argument's extreme case, and it arrives thirteen sessions later when students have enough vocabulary to feel it.

Deck 02 already carries three slides on vertical, protect-and-deliver, and safe areas. This expands that beachhead into a session.

---

### PART 1: THE FRAME TURNED (1–5)

**1 · Title**: New Media & Short-Form

**2 · The frame rotated and nobody redesigned the grammar**
9:16, 1:1, 4:5. Roughly a decade old at scale, and still mostly boarded by people improvising.

**3 · What you lose**
The horizontal two-shot. Lateral composition. The reveal by pan. Most of what leadroom was for.

**4 · What you gain**
Verticality as a dial. Foreground/background stacking. Proximity: a face fills a vertical frame at a distance that would read as a medium shot horizontally.

**5 · The rule of thirds, rotated**
The intersections move. Everything you learned in Session 5 still applies; the geometry underneath it changed.

### PART 2: STAGING WITHOUT WIDTH (6–10)

**6 · Where does the two-shot go?**
Three answers: stack them (one nearer, one further), stagger them vertically, or stop using two-shots and cut. Diagram all three.

**7 · Stacking replaces lateral composition**
Foreground / midground / background does more work here than anywhere else in the course, because depth is the only axis you have left.

**8 · The 180° rule survives intact**
Screen direction still exists, eyelines still answer. **Nothing from Session 4 is thrown out.** Worth stating plainly. Students assume the whole system collapses.

**9 · Shot sizes shift one notch tighter**
A vertical "medium shot" reads closer than its horizontal equivalent. The vocabulary holds; the calibration moves.

**10 · The frame has furniture, again**
Captions, UI, the bottom third eaten by an interface. Callback to Deck 10's HUD slide if that deck ran; forward-reference if it didn't.

### PART 3: TIME (11–13)

**11 · Six seconds**
Pre-roll, bumpers, stings. One beat. No establishing shot. You open *inside* the moment.

**12 · The hook is the first frame, not the first shot**
There is no patience budget. Callback to H5's hold lengths: everything compresses, and the compression is not proportional.

**13 · Loops**
Content designed to repeat. The last frame has to hand off to the first. A genuinely different structural problem with no equivalent in film.

### PART 4: ONE ASSET, THREE CROPS (14–16)

**14 · Protect and deliver** ← callback to Deck 02
Compose for the crop you'll be *watched* in, protect for the one you'll be *delivered* in. Show one image with 16:9, 1:1 and 9:16 crop guides over it.

**15 · Where it doesn't work**
Some staging cannot survive three crops, and the honest answer is to board it twice. Knowing which is which is the skill.

**16 · Exercise: the three-crop test**
Take one beat from your final. Board it for 16:9, then re-board it for 9:16. *What did you have to give up? What did you have to move? Which crop is the story actually in?*
