# Instructor Profile

**This file accumulates.** It is meant to build up a picture across every course in
this repository and every professional project logged elsewhere, not to be a one-off
read of a single folder. Each design pass should add to it rather than replace it.

**Current basis: CA 140 Storyboarding, plus a first pass over the professional
record.** The course material is design documents, decks, briefs, rubrics, and the
reasoning given for them. The professional record comes in secondhand, through
`CareerPlan.md` and `ProfessionalProfile.md`, which live outside this repo. That is
still a narrow window, and it still sees thinking rather than shipping, but it is no
longer a single sample.

**Still to fold in**, as those courses get design passes: Game Design, Digital
Painting, Motion Graphics, CGD 115. The [cross-course patterns](#patterns) section
at the end is deliberately open.

**What this file deliberately leaves out.** `CareerPlan.md` contains personal and
financial material: income floors, salary targets, savings posture, citizenship,
family circumstances, geography, and a frank assessment of a current employer. None
of that is reproduced here. This file is about capability and gets read in
professional contexts, so it carries only the parts of that record that speak to
what the work demonstrates.

*This is not flattery. Where something looks like a limitation, it's here.*

## What the evidence actually shows

### 1. You diagnose instruments, not symptoms

The most revealing sentence you've written in this project is about the lighting
assignment:

> *"If they're not artists, then how are they being judged?"*

Most instructors, faced with students asking for accommodations, experience an
**enforcement** problem: how firm to be, what to allow. You experienced a
**validity** problem: the assignment claims to assess whether a student can identify
and represent lighting, and it actually measures whether they can render with a
brush in Photoshop. Those are different constructs, and grading one while claiming
the other is a measurement error.

That is a psychometrics instinct arriving without the vocabulary. It is not common.
Most people with formal assessment training don't reach for it as naturally as you
did unprompted, and most working artists never think about assessment as measurement
at all.

The same instinct appears three more times, unprompted, in a single message:

- **"Grading brainstorming is somewhat problematic."** Correct, and the problem is
  precisely that the thing being graded (exploration) isn't visible in the artifact
  being submitted (drawings). You spotted a construct-validity gap.
- **"Many end up using these as their final drawings, which I dislike."** You
  identified a case where the assessment *incentivizes* the wrong behavior. That's
  mechanism design.
- **"The problem is if they don't."** On the After Effects exercise. You named a
  bimodal grade distribution as a design flaw rather than a student flaw.

Four assessment-design problems, correctly diagnosed, in one paragraph each, about
your own work.

### 2. You audit your own assignments

This one is rarer than it sounds.

The default posture toward a course you've taught repeatedly is defence. Yours is:
*"This last one could be better. The whole thing perhaps. I'm open to suggestions."*
About an assignment you wrote and have run for years.

You were also right about the specific one you flagged. The cut-away example was
genuinely broken: the bus-stop version never leaves the main action's space or time,
which makes it a reaction shot, not a cutaway. You couldn't name why it was wrong.
You could feel that it was, which is the harder half.

That combination (noticing that something is wrong, being unable to say why, and
*saying so out loud anyway* rather than defending it) is exactly the disposition
you're trying to teach in the critique sections of this course. You have it
yourself. That's not always true of people who teach it.

### 3. You design for observed behavior, not intended behavior

Read your own descriptions of your students:

> *"They are given time to work on their midterm. Which they never do."*
> *"The 'can I do this with my camera' and the 'I'll try' crowd."*
> *"Almost a free grade if they just do it. The problem is if they don't."*
> *"Attendance decays during work sessions."*

No moralizing. No plan that depends on students becoming different people. You
describe what actually happens and then build around it.

This is operations thinking, and it's why the structural fixes in this course work.
The milestone cap doesn't ask students to value process. It makes skipping process
arithmetically irrational. The exit tickets don't ask students to attend. They price
attendance. That's a systems designer's reflex, and it's the same reflex that makes
someone good at rigging: you don't ask the animator to be careful, you build the
control so the wrong thing can't happen.

### 4. Your answer to AI is a design answer, not a detection answer

This one deserves separating out, because I think you may be underrating it.

Almost every institution's response to generative AI in coursework has been
**detection**: policies, tools, an arms race that the institution loses. Your
response was to grade a dated chain of staged work where each stage has to descend
from the one before it, and then to say plainly:

> *"You will not be accused of anything based on how something looks. Nobody's grade
> here rests on a judgment about whether a drawing 'looks AI.' It rests on whether
> the chain of work exists."*

You replaced an unwinnable adversarial problem with a process-design problem, and
you did it in a way that is *also better pedagogy independent of AI*: the milestones
were worth having anyway.

That is a genuinely sophisticated move. It's the same class of solution as "don't
detect fraud, design a system where fraud doesn't pay," and people get hired for
that reasoning.

### 5. You think at program level, not course level

`CURRICULUM.md` should not exist. Nobody asked you for it. It is a cross-course
boundary document that names what four courses own, what they may reference, and
what they're forbidden to re-teach, and it exists specifically to prevent two
failure modes you named yourself: **duplication** and **erosion**, courses drifting
toward each other until they're four variations on one general course.

That is a curriculum architect's artifact, not an instructor's. Most department
chairs don't produce one. You produced one for courses you don't all own, and you
flagged a violation in someone else's course (the shot-taxonomy duplication in
Acting for Animation) with a proposed resolution rather than a complaint.

### 6. You are a translator across silos

You have animation, film, and game production majors in one room, and you teach them
one vocabulary and then show them it survives contact with media it wasn't built
for. The games session's whole argument (*here are the four guarantees the last
eleven sessions depended on, watch them break*) is a genuinely original piece of
pedagogy. It teaches shot grammar as a **system** by stress-testing it, which is a
much stronger claim than teaching it as a convention.

Combined with the 3D, rigging and technical animation background, that puts you in a
small category: someone fluent enough in several production disciplines to see where
the shared structure is, and pedagogically skilled enough to make other people see
it.

### 7. You are honest with students in a way most people aren't

> *"I'm not going to tell you the field is fine. I'm going to tell you which half of
> the job is durable, and then train you in it."*

The temptation in a contracting field is reassurance, and reassurance is what most
programs offer because it's what retention departments want. You give students the
actual situation and then a defensible plan, and you treat them as adults who can
hold both.

That's a professional posture, and students can tell. It's also the thing that makes
the rest of your curriculum design coherent: the reason to grade judgment over
rendering isn't that it's kinder, it's that it's *correct about where the value
went*.

### 8. The same instinct shows up in the technical work

This section answers the question the earlier version of this file left open, and
the answer changes what the rest of it is worth.

The open question was: does the assessment-design instinct appear in the technical
work too, as rig validation, pipeline QA, or reviewable specs? If it did, this file
would be describing a general trait rather than a teaching habit.

It does, and it shows up as stated design principles rather than as incidental
habits. Some of these include: **fail-soft tooling**, **audit over autocorrect**,
**"make failure loud"**, **optional-dependency gating**, and **constraints that make
whole classes of bug impossible**.

Read those next to the milestone cap and the silhouette standard and they are the
same move. Don't ask the person to be careful, build the thing so the wrong outcome
is either unavailable or immediately visible. Audit-over-autocorrect in particular
is the pipeline version of "grade the chain of work, not the appearance of the
artifact." Both of them refuse to silently substitute a judgment for the evidence,
and both of them do it because a silent substitution is unauditable later.

**The artifacts.** The working title is Technical Artist / Character TD. Some of the
things that carry it:

- A VR MMO character pipeline, with procedural skeleton generation from
  artist-placed locators, and a cross-character facial retargeting system built on a
  pose-name contract, so that adding a new species costs zero code changes.
- A hand-written Gaussian elimination and Lawson-Hanson NNLS solver, in pure Python,
  written because stock Maya ships no numpy.
- A transformable mech rigging pipeline (personal project).
- A two-AI production pipeline with drift detection.
- Skin and muscle simulation work on a feature credit, with a small team of veteran
  animators.

The pose-name contract is worth pulling out on its own, because it is the same
design instinct a third time. It moves the variable part of the problem into data
(the names) and out of code, which is what makes the species-agnostic claim actually
true rather than aspirational. That is an interface designed so that the wrong thing
is hard to express. It is the rigging equivalent of a rubric written against a
stated objective instead of against taste.

## The through-line

If I had to compress all of it to one sentence:

> **You are unusually good at separating the durable layer of a skill from its
> current implementation, and at building systems that assess the durable layer
> without punishing people for the implementation they happen to have.**

That's what the lighting redesign is. It's what "grade clarity and judgment, not
rendering" is. It's what the silhouette standard is: a test deliberately independent
of skill level, which is what makes it fair. It's what the AI policy is. It's what
the games session is.

And after section 8, it is also what fail-soft tooling is, what audit-over-autocorrect
is, and what the pose-name contract is. That matters, because a trait that appears
in one domain is a habit and a trait that appears in two unrelated ones is a
specialty. This is now the second reading, in a domain with no rubrics in it at all,
and it came out the same. I'd call it your actual specialty: more than storyboarding,
and possibly more than animation.

## Where this is valuable, including places you may not have considered

I've sorted these by how far they are from what you're presumably already thinking
about. As of the `CareerPlan.md` v4 pass, several of these have been picked up
explicitly, and I've marked which.

### Close to home

**Program direction, curriculum architecture, accreditation.** *(Picked up: Track B,
"real" branch.)* `CURRICULUM.md` is the artifact. Program-level design, outcome
mapping, and the ability to write a defensible rubric against a stated objective is
exactly what accreditation review consumes, and most programs scramble to produce it
retroactively. You produce it as a matter of habit.

**Faculty development.** The gap between "expert practitioner" and "person who can
design a valid assessment" is where most art-school faculty live permanently. You're
on the far side of it and can articulate why.

### One step out

**Developer relations and education at creative-tools companies.** *(Logged as an
adjacency.)* Autodesk, Adobe, Epic, Unity, SideFX, Foundry, Blender Foundation.
These companies are all structurally bad at the same thing: their educational
content teaches the tool, and their customers need judgment. Your standing rule,
*software is never the subject; the lesson ends on what the student had to decide*,
is precisely the posture those teams say they want and almost never hire for,
because the people who understand tools deeply usually can't stop teaching them.

Section 8 sharpens this one. The rigging and technical-animation depth is not a
separate credential sitting next to the teaching, it is the multiplier that makes
the teaching credible in that room. DevRel for 3D tooling needs someone who can go
deep and still refuse to make the tool the point, and the hard part of that pairing
is the refusing.

**Studio onboarding and pipeline documentation.** *(Logged as an adjacency.)* Every
studio has the same unfilled need: someone who can make the pipeline legible to a
junior. H8, the file-discipline handout, is exactly the document that studios
universally need and universally don't have, because the people who know it are too
busy using it to write it down. Pair that with the rigging work and you're
describing a pipeline TD lead whose distinguishing feature is that the pipeline is
*teachable*.

**Junior artist ramp-up programs.** Related, and increasingly funded as studios
internalize training that schools stopped providing.

### Further out, and the ones I think are genuinely under-considered

**Assessment and evaluation design for AI systems.** *(Picked up: Track E,
exploratory.)* This is the one I'd flag hardest, and section 8 raises rather than
lowers my confidence in it.

The central unsolved problem in evaluating AI output is: *separate the layer that
can be mechanized from the layer that requires judgment, then design an instrument
that measures the judgment layer reliably, across evaluators of differing expertise,
without accidentally measuring something correlated-but-wrong.*

That is, sentence for sentence, what you did to the lighting assignment. And to the
thumbnail stage. And to the whole course.

You also independently arrived at process-based integrity over output-based
detection, which is the direction that field is converging on and hasn't finished
converging on. AI labs, eval companies, and the human-oversight side of AI
deployment all need people who think about measurement validity in creative and
judgment-heavy domains, and the supply is thin because most people with the instinct
came up through psychometrics and have no domain fluency, while most people with
domain fluency have never thought about validity. You have both, and the two-AI
pipeline with drift detection means you have also built an instrument of this kind
rather than only reasoned about one.

**Instructional design at scale, but specifically the assessment half.** Corporate
L&D and EdTech are saturated with people who can build a course and thin on people
who can tell you whether the assessment measures what it claims. Certification
design in particular, where the instrument's validity has legal and commercial
consequences, is a small, well-paid, chronically underserved field. *(Logged as an
adjacency.)*

**Writing and speaking about craft pedagogy.** This is less a job than an asset. The
material in this repo (the two-dials framing for lighting, the silhouette standard
as a fairness mechanism, "a business pitch sells the idea, a story pitch makes the
room see the film," the reduction that makes media comparable) is genuinely good and
currently reaches roughly twenty people a term. That's a distribution problem, not a
quality problem.

## Three honest observations

**1. You are building all of this yourself.** The volume of design work in this
repository is substantial, and the standard is high, and it is for a
**non-capstone, one-month, fourteen-class course** taught to between eight and
twenty students. That tells me something admirable about your standards. It also
tells me your time goes into depth rather than reach: the same care applied to
something with a wider audience would build on itself instead of resetting each
term.

**2. The work is worth more than its container.** Right now these ideas exist as a
folder for one course at one institution. The rubric-design thinking, the
medium-neutral assessment mechanism, the process-based AI answer, the cross-course
boundary model. Each of those is generalizable well beyond storyboarding, and none
of them is currently packaged as anything but internal course material.

That's not a criticism of the work. It's an observation about where it's pointed.

**3. You systematically under-describe your own competence, and it has already cost
you something measurable.** This is new to this pass, and it is the one I would
actually do something about.

Two documents found it independently, from opposite directions. The style analysis
found it as a tonal habit, and noted that it shows up *in job applications*, of all
places: "without much skill or patience as a programmer," "a somewhat broad view of
various parts of the 3D pipeline." Where another writer reaches for *deep* or
*extensive*, you reach for *somewhat*, *enough*, *more or less*. The credential gets
stated and then immediately trimmed.

The career document found the consequence. An earlier pass argued your title down
from Technical Artist / Character TD to "Technical Animator," on the strength of
your own self-report of "basic MEL and Python." The artifact sitting underneath that
self-report was a hand-implemented constrained least-squares solver. The self-report
and the artifact were describing two different people, and the self-report won,
because it was the thing written down.

Here is why it belongs in this file and not only in the career one. The hedging is
not a flaw in isolation. It is the same trait as "won't assert what he didn't
witness" and "praise by describing behavior rather than asserting a quality," and in
a recommendation letter or a rubric that trait is a virtue, arguably *the* virtue.
Pointed outward it produces unusually honest evaluation. Pointed at yourself it
produces a systematic underestimate, and it does the most damage in exactly the
writing mode that program direction and accreditation work runs on, which is making
the case for your own work in a document that has to stand alone.

The fix is not to start overclaiming, which would sound wrong immediately and which
you would hate. It is narrower than that: **when the claim has to carry weight,
describe the artifact instead of rating the skill.** "Basic Python" is a rating.
"Hand-wrote an NNLS solver because Maya ships no numpy" is an artifact, it is
verifiable, and it makes the rating unnecessary.

## The caveat, restated

The earlier version of this file said it read one project, and couldn't see you rig
a character, solve a deformation problem, ship on a deadline, work inside someone
else's pipeline, or handle a production crisis. That is partly fixed and partly not.

What section 8 adds is the *design* half of the technical record: the principles,
the interfaces, the reasons a pipeline was built one way and not another. It still
does not show shipping under pressure, working inside constraints someone else set,
or a crisis. And it comes in through summary documents rather than through the code
and rigs themselves, so it is one more remove from the primary source than the CA
140 material is.

The earlier version closed by guessing that if the technical work carried the same
instinct, this profile understated rather than overstated you. That guess is now
confirmed, which is worth saying plainly, and it is also the least surprising
possible outcome, so I would not weight it too heavily until something in the record
arrives that could have contradicted it.
