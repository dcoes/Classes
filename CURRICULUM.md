## Purpose

This file exists to prevent two failure modes:

1. **Duplication** — the same material taught twice, in two courses, uncoordinated, with neither instructor-of-record aware.
2. **Erosion** — courses drifting toward each other until they're variations on one general "visual media" course instead of four strong, discipline-specific ones.

The table below is the enforcement mechanism. It says what each course **owns**, and — more usefully — what each course is **not allowed to re-teach**.

## The pitch split — resolve this before building Deck 11

You already teach pitching in Game Design, with a working lecture and an assignment ("come up with a pitch for a game you would like to build"). That deck is good and it covers ground Deck 11 was going to duplicate.

**What Game Design owns and Storyboarding must not repeat:**
- Get in the door / be prepared / be organized / be passionate
- Market research, profitability, comparable titles
- "Start by stating platform, audience, genre — do not begin by telling the story"
- Assume the client's point of view
- The business framing generally

**What Storyboarding owns exclusively:**
- Performing a sequence aloud — present tense, active voice, doing the voices
- Pitching the *timing*: pausing where the film pauses, saying "hold" on a hold
- The Disney *Snow White* precedent — boards pinned to a wall and acted out, which is literally the origin of the format
- Standing beside boards and reading a sequence to a room
- The note-taking discipline, applied to visual work

**The distinction to state out loud in class:** a business pitch sells the *idea*. A story pitch makes the room *see the film*. Students who take both courses should get the second one as a specialization of a skill they already have a foundation in — and you can say that explicitly, which makes the department look coordinated rather than repetitive.

**One line worth stealing verbatim** from the Game Design deck into Deck 11, because it's the best sentence in either: *"Because as the designer, no one knows more about the project than you do."* Same is true of the board artist, and it's the right answer to student nerves.

## Specific transfers worth making

### From Game Design → Storyboarding

**1. "Don't get attached." → Deck 01, slide 4**
Your prototype slide says: *doesn't have to be perfect, don't get attached, replaceable, updateable.* That is exactly the argument for why production boards are crude. Same principle, different artifact. Worth using the identical phrasing so students who've taken both hear the echo.

**2. "A playtest is not a prototype of the game, but of the game experience." → Deck 12**
This is the single best transfer in the batch. The animatic is not a prototype of the film — it's a prototype of the *film experience*. That reframe explains why timing and temp audio matter more than drawing quality, and it does it in one sentence.

**3. "What experience do you want the player to have? What emotion?" → Final project brief**
This is Schell's Lens of Essential Experience in plain language. As the opening question on the final project brief — *what experience do you want the audience to have?* — it forces intent before execution.

**4. "What could keep this game from being great?" → the pitch gate**
Risk assessment, repurposed. A strong question to ask students pitching their own final premise, and one that separates students who've thought it through from ones who've had an idea.

**5. The listening and criticism material (Intro to Game Design, slides 27–39) → Deck 11**
*Listen to your team / audience / game / client*, plus *be able to take criticism*, is the best existing foundation for Deck 11's note-taking section. Storyboarding's version: listen to the room, listen to the sequence, listen to the note behind the note.

**6. "Secrets: what is known by the game only, by all players, by some players?" → Deck 09, marginal**
Repurposed as dramatic irony: what does the audience know that the character doesn't, and how do you stage that gap? Genuinely useful, but it's one slide and it's optional.

### From Acting for Animation → Storyboarding Deck 09

| Source slide | Transfer |
|---|---|
| 13 — *Acting is reacting* | Justifies staging the listener. The reaction is often the shot. |
| 14 — *Each scene is a negotiation* | Power/status staging. Height, distance, orientation now have a *reason*, not just a list of options. |
| 15 — *Play an action, in pursuit of an objective, while overcoming an obstacle* | **The single best line to import.** Forces students to board a verb. Better than "what does this character want" alone. |
| 17–18 — *Animate thoughts, not words* | Board the thought, not the line. The panel is the thought that motivates the movement. |
| 20 — *Subtext* | Are the words and the body saying the same thing? If not, stage the contradiction. |
| 25–28 — *Poses; too many poses; key storytelling poses* | Directly supports "pick the one frame that contains the performance." |
| 32–41 — *Shot types* | **Do not import.** This is Deck 03's material and the source of the current duplication. |
